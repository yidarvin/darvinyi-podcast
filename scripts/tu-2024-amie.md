[G] Exactly. And the paper cites estimates that in some settings sixty to eighty percent of diagnoses are made through history-taking alone. [O] There is a second gap too, on the evaluation side.
[G] There is, and I would argue it is the more durable contribution. Prior medical dialogue work was scored on things like fluency, relevance, informativeness, human-likeness, or on BLEU and ROUGE against a reference transcript. None of that tells you whether the consultation was any good. The closest prior thing to a real assessment framework was a multi-agent setup restricted to dermatology, where AI models played both the doctor and the patient, and history-taking was scored as complete or not complete. [G] That is the claim, and I think it holds. Which is why the honest description of this paper is not "a model beat doctors". It is "here is an evaluation apparatus for diagnostic dialogue, and here is what happened when we ran a system through it".
[O] Let us do the system, then. What is AMIE actually made of?
[G] The base model is Palm Two. It is instruction fine-tuned on a mixture. The static part is four datasets. Med Q-A, the USMLE-style multiple choice set, eleven thousand four hundred fifty training questions and one thousand two hundred seventy-three test questions, plus a hundred and ninety-one questions where clinicians wrote out step-by-step reasoning. Sixty-four long-form questions with expert-written answers. Sixty-five clinician-written summaries of notes from MIMIC three. And then the large real-world component.
[S] Which is the transcripts.
[G] Almost ninety-nine thousand de-identified audio transcripts of actual in-person doctor-patient visits, licensed from a dialogue research organisation, drawn from over a thousand clinicians across a ten-year period in the United States. Average conversation length was around a hundred and fifty turns.
[O] A hundred and fifty turns. That is a real consultation, not a chat exchange.
[G] It is, and that gap matters later. Those are in-person spoken visits, transcribed. The evaluation is synchronous text chat capped at twenty minutes. The training distribution and the evaluation distribution are not the same medium.
[S] Noted. So why did they need simulated data at all, if they had ninety-nine thousand real consultations?
[G] Two reasons the paper gives. Coverage and noise. Real transcripts do not span the space of conditions, and they are messy, with slang, interruptions, ungrammatical utterances, implicit references. So they built a self-play environment with two nested loops.
[O] Walk us through the inner loop, because this is the part I find genuinely clever.
[G] There is a vignette generator first. For a given condition, they retrieve sixty passages from a web search, twenty each on demographics, symptoms, and management plan. Palm Two filters out the passages that are not actually about the condition. Then AMIE is prompted with the surviving passages and a one-shot format exemplar to write plausible patient vignettes, including demographics, history, and an associated diagnosis and management plan.
[S] So the ground truth for the simulated case is generated by the same family of models that will later be trained on it.
[G] Correct, and hold that thought. Then the dialogue itself. Three agents. A patient agent that embodies the vignette and answers honestly. A doctor agent instructed to be an empathetic clinician who knows nothing in advance and asks no more than two questions per turn. And a moderator agent that watches and decides when the conversation has reached a natural conclusion. Then a fourth agent, a critic, which unlike the others is told the ground-truth diagnosis, and gives the doctor agent feedback.
[O] What does the critic actually grade on?
[G] Four things. Whether the doctor agent showed empathy and professionalism while addressing the patient's latest question concisely. Whether it avoided asking too many or repetitive questions. Whether it stayed natural and factually accurate without revealing it was an AI. And whether it asked enough questions to identify at least two of the most likely differential diagnoses before narrowing toward the ground truth.
[S] And then it re-runs the conversation.
[G] From scratch, with the same patient agent, but the doctor agent retains its previous dialogue history. That refinement is repeated twice. The refined dialogues are then folded into the next round of fine-tuning, which is the outer loop, and the next checkpoint plays a better doctor agent in the next round of inner self-play.
[O] And the volume?
[G] Eleven thousand six hundred eighty-six dialogues per fine-tuning iteration, covering five thousand two hundred thirty conditions. Four dialogues each from six hundred thirteen common conditions, and two dialogues each from four thousand six hundred seventeen rarer ones drawn from the MalaCards disease database and the MedicineNet index. Average simulated conversation was just over twenty-one turns.

[G] Three sequential model calls before every single reply. [O] So the empathy is partly a post-processing step.

[G] For evaluation practice, quite a lot, and that is where I would put the paper's weight. Three things transfer.
