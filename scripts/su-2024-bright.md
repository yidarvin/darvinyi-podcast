[S] It is on the docket because it is the rare benchmark that identifies a failure mode nobody was measuring, and then hands you a worked example of why you should read the table rather than the sentence describing it. Bridget, start with the title claim — what makes a query reasoning-intensive rather than merely hard?
[G] The authors lay out three levels. Level one is keyword retrieval — who sang the song I Think We're Alone Now — where the answer document contains the entity outright. That is Natural Questions territory. Level two is semantic retrieval, M-S MARCO territory, where the wording differs but the topic is plainly shared. Level three, which is what BRIGHT is built for, is where the query and the relevant document share almost no vocabulary and no topic label, and the only thing linking them is an underlying theorem, algorithm, or causal mechanism.
[O] Give me the concrete one, because that still sounds abstract.
[G] The paper's own example is lovely. Someone posts on the Sustainable Living forum asking whether they can reuse the water that pools in the saucers under their houseplants. The document that actually answers it is about soluble salts accumulating in soil, destroying soil structure and damaging roots. No shared keywords, no shared topic. You have to already know that recycling plant water concentrates salts before the connection exists at all.
[S] And every benchmark in wide use — B-E-I-R, the retrieval slice of M-T-E-B, Natural Questions, M-S MARCO — sits at levels one and two.
[G] Correct, and that is the gap the paper is claiming. There had been two prior attempts. BIRCO evaluates retrieval against multifaceted objectives, but only in an L-L-M reranking setting over a candidate pool of roughly one hundred documents per query, so it never tests retrieval over a realistic corpus at all. RAR-b converts existing multiple-choice commonsense, math and code datasets into a retrieval format, but its documents are the answer options — often under ten words. The BRIGHT authors describe that as more conceptual than practical, on the grounds that real retrieval means retrieving documents, not answers.
[O] So walk us through what they actually built.
[G] Twelve datasets, one thousand three hundred eighty-four queries, in three families. Seven come from StackExchange — Biology, Earth Science, Economics, Psychology, Robotics, Stack Overflow and Sustainable Living. Two are coding — LeetCode and Pony. Three are theorem-based — AoPS competition problems, and two slices of TheoremQA.
[S] The StackExchange half is where the human labour is. How is a positive document defined?
[G] An annotator browses posts newest to oldest and keeps one only if it has an answer that is either accepted or has more than five votes, and that answer contains at least one U-R-L. The post's title and body become the query. The annotator then visits every linked page, splits it into passages, and keeps a passage as positive only if it genuinely helps reason through the post — the paper's phrasing is that it must help with critical concepts or theories. Hard negatives are collected separately: the annotator searches the post's title, or an L-L-M-generated keyword summary, and keeps up to five topically similar but unhelpful pages.
[O] So the negatives are deliberately on-topic. That is the whole trick.
[G] That is the whole trick. If your negatives are random documents, lexical matching solves the task. Here the negatives are exactly what a search engine would surface for the same query.
[S] Who verifies the annotator? Because a single first-pass annotator is the part that would make me nervous.
[G] One computer science student does the first pass, then two PhD students in the relevant field verify it, and only unanimously approved examples survive. It is a real protocol. But this is also the place where I would want a number the paper does not give. They report unanimity as a filter; they never report the pre-unanimity disagreement rate. So you cannot tell whether the reviewers were rubber-stamping or throwing out half the material.
[O] Do they report agreement anywhere?
[G] On the two programmatically-filtered subsets, yes, and those numbers are respectable. For TheoremQA-Theorems, where GPT-4 matches a question to a formal theorem statement, they check fifty human-annotated instances and report a Cohen's kappa of point six two. For LeetCode, where GPT-4 decides whether a problem's defining algorithm is too common to be a fair target, they check eighty author annotations at kappa point seven three.
[S] Tell me about Pony, because that one sounds like a trap.
[G] Pony is a rare, low-resource programming language. The queries are coding instructions, and the positives are the syntax documentation a programmer would actually need to look up. The problem text almost never names the right function, so you have to reason from what the code must do to which built-in provides it. The corpus is small — under eight thousand documents — but there are twenty-two point five positive documents per query on average, by far the highest in the benchmark.
[O] And LeetCode?
[G] Problem statements as queries, and the site's own similar-questions links as positives, searched over a pool that combines LeetCode with CodeSearchNet — over four hundred thousand documents. They deliberately discard problems whose defining algorithm is so common that it turns up as an unrelated false positive throughout CodeSearchNet. That filtering is the GPT-4 judgment with the point seven three kappa.
[S] And the math side.
[G] AoPS pairs competition problems with other problems that require the same named technique — Fermat's Little Theorem, Ptolemy's theorem — and crucially they do not rewrite the problem text, because competition problems are already written in deliberately diverse ways. TheoremQA-Questions takes theorem-driven questions and has GPT-4 rewrite each into a concrete applied scenario so the theorem name never leaks into the query. Two hundred and six of an original eight hundred survive manual review for solvability. TheoremQA-Theorems instead retrieves the formal theorem statement itself out of ProofWiki, which is over twenty thousand formal definitions and proofs.
[O] Right. Thirteen retrievers. Give me the leaderboard.

[S] Fine. Now the part everyone quotes: writing out reasoning before you search.
[G] The intervention is simple. Prompt an L-L-M with the query and three instructions — identify the essential problem, reason step by step about what the relevant documents should contain, then draft an answer — and use that whole reasoning trace as the new query. And this is the part where you have to be careful, because the result is completely different depending on who wrote the trace.
[O] Careful how?
[G] GPT-4-written traces improve all thirteen retrievers. Llama-3-70B-written traces improve twelve of the thirteen — they lower GritLM, from twenty-one point zero to twenty point nine.
