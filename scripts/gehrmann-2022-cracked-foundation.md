[O] We will come back to that. Rosalind, why is this paper worth 35 minutes?
[G] Because it is the closest thing the generation literature has to a single connected diagnosis. Three failure classes that were each well documented in isolation, automatic metrics, human evaluation, and the datasets underneath both, argued as one problem across 244 cited works and roughly twenty years. And then the authors do something most surveys do not, which is check whether anyone is actually following the advice.
[S] Start with the framing, because I think the framing is the strongest thing here. What is the actual claim about why evaluation is bad?
[G] The claim is that this is an incentive problem, not a knowledge problem. The paper says it flatly in the introduction. Many recent papers critique evaluation techniques or propose new ones, and almost none of the suggestions are followed. The better methods already exist in the literature this survey is citing. Adopting them costs effort and does not win the leaderboard.
[O] And that produces the circular dependency, which is the line I keep coming back to. Evaluation of generation models can only improve if generation models use better evaluations. Nobody wants to be the first mover.
[G] There is a sharper version of it a paragraph later, and I think it is the single best sentence in the paper. There is a systemic difference between selecting the best model and characterizing how good this model really is. Current techniques are built for the first. Detecting real problems requires the second.
[S] That is well put but it is also twenty years old as a complaint. What made it urgent in 2022 specifically?
[G] That the models got good enough to break the metrics. The older automatic measures work on surface-level features, and once outputs are fluent, the surface stops discriminating. The metrics can still see fluency improvements. They cannot see whether the content is attributable to the input. So the paper argues the ineffective evaluations lead to overestimates of model capabilities, and the failures that survive are the deep ones, shortcuts, overfitting, hallucination.
[O] Let us take the three legs in order. Metrics first. What is the case against them beyond the usual grumbling about BLEU?
[G] The case is that the target itself is wrong. Most automatic metrics measure human-likeness, similarity to a reference, and the paper leans on Belz and Gatt from 2008 for the strong form of this, that a system's ability to produce human-like outputs may be completely unrelated to its effect on human task performance. Then two independent results, Kryscinski and colleagues in 2019 and Fabbri and colleagues in 2021, find ROUGE does not significantly correlate with either the content quality or the linguistic quality of a summary.
[S] Those are correlations against human ratings, which are themselves the thing this paper says is unreliable. Does that not eat itself?
[G] It is a real tension and the paper does not fully resolve it. But the ROUGE results do not depend only on the human side. Graham in 2015 tested all 192 ROUGE configurations against BLEU and found none of them beat it, and that the best performer was the precision variant of ROUGE-2, which is not what anybody runs. Rankel and colleagues in 2013 found the same shape, that the rarely-used configurations outperform the commonly-used ones. The field standardized on a configuration that is not the good one.
[O] And people did try to fix this with learned metrics. BLEURT, COMET, embedding-based approaches. Did that not work?
[G] Not as cleanly as the story goes. Bhandari and colleagues in 2020 found no distributional metric outperforming ROUGE on segment-level correlation on TAC and CNN Daily Mail. And the paper's deeper objection is structural, in a section titled "The Myth of the Single Reliable Number." Graham's analysis of the DUC 2004 data found almost no correlation between the language quality of a summary and its coverage of the source meaning. Two dimensions people routinely collapse into one score.
[S] So the objection is not that the number is inaccurate. It is that a scalar cannot represent the object.
[G] Exactly that. And faithfulness has the same shape. Maynez and colleagues separate intrinsic hallucinations, which misrepresent facts in the input, from extrinsic ones, which ignore the input entirely. Their example is turning a former London mayoral candidate into a former London mayor. Some extrinsic hallucinations are actually factual and may even be desirable. You cannot express that distinction in one number.
[O] Second leg. Human evaluation. This is the one where I expect the paper to say "just do it more carefully."
[G] It says the opposite, that careful is not well defined. The anchor result is Howcroft and colleagues in 2020, who coded 165 NLG papers and found generated text evaluated along 204 distinct quality dimensions, which they could map down to only 71 actual criteria. The word fluency alone splits into 15 different criteria depending on how each paper used it.
[S] That is a measurement crisis, not a rigor crisis. Nobody is measuring the same thing.
[G] And the documentation is worse than the taxonomy. In that same sample, of 478 specific evaluation questions, more than half, 279 of 478, never define the criterion being measured. And 311 of 478, about 65 percent, never report the exact question given to the raters.
[O] So the study is unreplicable by construction, because you cannot recover what was asked.
[G] Right. And then there is the statistical layer, which is where I think this section really lands. Card and colleagues in 2020 found a typical machine translation test set of 2000 sentences has roughly 75 percent power to detect a 1 BLEU point difference. Wei and Jia in 2021 push it further, that detecting a difference of 1 point on the 1 to 100 WMT scale would need roughly 10,000 perfect annotator judgments.
[S] Ten thousand. And the field's convention is what, a hundred items?
[G] The paper says most opt for the typical 100 data points. And Howcroft and Rieser show that treating ordinal ratings as interval data makes the tests even more underpowered, which is what most papers do with Likert scales. Amidei and colleagues counted 68 out of 85 recent papers doing exactly that.
[O] Alright, third leg, the datasets. This is the one I find most damning because it is upstream of everything else.
[G] It is the quiet one. Over 70 percent of XSum's reference summaries contain information not present in the source article, per Maynez and colleagues. So a model that is perfectly faithful is penalized against that reference. And CNN Daily Mail is worse conceptually. Its reference summaries are homepage bullet points that were assembled for a reading comprehension task, not a summarization one.
[S] I remember the Stiennon result on that.
[G] Human raters given both actually prefer the article's own first three sentences to the CNN Daily Mail reference summary. The reference is worse than a trivial extractive baseline. And the field's concentration on these is measurable. The paper samples 20 summarization papers from 2021 and finds 27 datasets used in total, with CNN Daily Mail appearing 5 times and XSum 4 times, and only 2 of the 27 being non-English.
[O] There is one piece of genuinely new analysis in here, though, right? Figure two is not a citation.
[G] It is the survey's own re-analysis, and it is my favourite thing in the paper. Mille and colleagues collected fresh test sets for XSum and the German and Spanish subsets of MLSum, using the original collection procedure but focused on COVID-19 news, so content that is in neither the training data nor the original test sets. The authors re-plot BLEURT scores for 33 models on original versus new.
[S] And?
[G] Every single one of the 33 points falls below the diagonal. Not most. All 33. Every model gets worse the moment the topic shifts, and a static independent-and-identically-distributed split can never surface that.
[O] So let us do the constructive half, because I think people under-remember that this paper has a proposal, not just a complaint.
[G] Section six proposes what they call an evaluation report, shipped as part of a model card. Four components. A data card for each dataset. A benchmark matrix, meaning multiple datasets crossed with multiple automatic metrics and documented human evaluation, side by side rather than collapsed. An error analysis broken into categories like hallucination type and grammaticality instead of one aggregate quality score. And a model audit, a suite of targeted stress tests.
[S] What does the audit output look like? Because "run more tests" is not a proposal.
[G] Signed deltas with uncertainty, framed causally, explicitly in the spirit of CheckList. Their mock example in figure three shows a model handling 1 percent spelling noise at minus 9 plus or minus 3 points on a metric, and OCR-derived input at minus 5 plus or minus 3. Then an unseen topic at minus 29 plus or minus 8, and a 2018 to 2020 time shift at minus 23 plus or minus 5.
[O] That is the thing I would actually want as a deployer. Not "this model is state of the art," but "expect a 29 point drop off-topic."
[G] The paper writes the sentence out, roughly, when the model summarizes news from the same source but with COVID-19 content, expect quality drops of 20 plus or minus 5 percent according to BLEURT. That is a performance guarantee, which they name as the long-term goal.
