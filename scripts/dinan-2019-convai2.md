[O] They ranked with three metrics and then handed the whole thing to human beings, which is exactly why we are talking about it. This is a competition report that audits itself in public.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio, where an optimist, a skeptic, and a visiting scholar take one paper apart. Today's guest is Desmond, who knows this work well.
[S] The paper is "The Second Conversational Intelligence Challenge," known as ConvAI2, by Emily Dinan, Varvara Logacheva, Valentin Malykh, and fourteen more authors across Facebook AI Research, the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology, Montreal, McGill, Carnegie Mellon, and Microsoft Research. It is the report on a NeurIPS twenty eighteen competition, posted to arXiv in early twenty nineteen.
[O] It is on the docket because it is one of the earliest documents where a benchmark's own organizers show you, with their own numbers, the automatic leaderboard and the human leaderboard disagreeing about who won.
[G] Desmond here. And I would sharpen that framing slightly. This is not a method paper and it should not be read as one. Its contribution is infrastructure plus an unusually candid post-mortem. The modelling in it is entirely of its moment and has aged badly. The evaluation post-mortem has aged extremely well.
[S] Set the stage. What did open-domain chit-chat evaluation look like before this?
[G] The authors' own complaint is that progress was, in their words, significantly hampered by the absence of established benchmark tasks for non-goal-oriented dialogue and solid criteria for automatic assessment. Goal-directed dialogue had somewhere to compete. There were five iterations of the DSTC challenge on things like restaurant booking and dialogue state tracking. Question answering had quiz-bowl-style contests. Chit-chat, meaning conversation that is not trying to accomplish anything, had nothing comparable.
[O] There was a first ConvAI, though. What happened to it?
[G] It ran at NeurIPS twenty seventeen and the paper is quite frank that it underdelivered. The task was to discuss specific Wikipedia snippets with humans, which the authors say was not engaging to all human participants. Ten dialogue systems took part, and the best bot scored two point seven four six on dialogue quality against three point eight for humans.
[S] And no training data was provided.
[G] That is the other diagnosis. In twenty seventeen no data was given at the start, it was collected by volunteers as the competition went along, which the authors suggest may have led to fewer participants. So the second edition changes three things deliberately. Chit-chat about personal interests instead of encyclopedia articles. A full training set on day one. And evaluation through three separate channels rather than one.
[O] Desmond, the task itself is not new to this paper. It is Persona-Chat.
[G] Correct, and this is the key inheritance. Persona-Chat is Zhang and colleagues, twenty eighteen. Two crowdworkers each get a short persona, at least five profile sentences, things like "I like to ski" or "I have four children." They chat and try to get to know each other, and neither knows the other's persona. ConvAI2 does not invent that task. What ConvAI2 does is turn that dataset into a benchmark, with a leaderboard, a hidden test set, and a human evaluation protocol.
[S] So walk me through the data as they actually shipped it.
[G] The public training set is roughly one hundred and thirty one thousand examples, across seventeen thousand eight hundred and seventy eight dialogues, drawn from one thousand one hundred and fifty five personas. Validation holds out one hundred personas never seen in training. And then the crucial piece, because the original Persona-Chat test split had already been released publicly, they crowdsourced an entirely new hidden test set. One hundred further personas, six thousand six hundred and thirty four examples, one thousand and fifteen dialogues, never shown to competitors.
[O] That is the bit I want to flag as good practice. The public test set was burned, so they went and bought a new one.
[S] Fine, but a fresh test set only protects you from memorization. It does not protect you from a model that wins by matching words.
[G] Which is precisely the second control, and the authors built it for exactly that reason. They crowdsourced rewritten, or "revised," versions of the same personas. Rephrases, generalizations, specializations, with little or no word overlap with the original. Their examples are "I just got my nails done" becoming "I love to pamper myself on a regular basis," and "I am on a diet now" becoming "I need to lose weight."
[O] So you can measure whether a model was actually using the persona or just copying words out of it.
[G] That is the design intent, yes.
[S] Metrics. And I want you to be careful here, because I have seen people garble this table.
[G] Rightly so, because the three metrics do not point the same direction and they do not apply to the same models. Perplexity is a fluency measure and lower is better, and it is computable only for probabilistic generative models. F one is word overlap between the predicted response and the gold response, higher is better, and it is the only one of the three computable for any model, retrieval or generative. Hits at one out of twenty is the accuracy of picking the true next utterance out of the gold plus nineteen random distractors. Higher is better, and it requires a model that can score candidates at all.
[S] So a retrieval-only model has no perplexity number, and a pure generator may have no hits at one.
[G] Correct, and the table is full of dashes for that reason. Which makes the ranking rule itself interesting. Teams were ranked by the minimum rank of their score in any of the three metrics. Your overall position is your single best showing on whichever metric suited your model.
[O] That is actually a thoughtful accommodation. You are not penalized for building a kind of model that one metric cannot see.
[S] It is also a rule that rewards specializing in whichever metric is easiest to game. Hold that thought.
[G] The top seven from that round were invited to a human evaluation on Mechanical Turk. And I want to be precise about the funnel, because it narrows at every stage and people conflate the stages. Twenty three teams in the automatic round. Seven invited. High Five declined to take part, so six finalists went through to Mechanical Turk, evaluated alongside the Key-Value Profile Memory retrieval baseline. And the third channel, the "wild" live chat with volunteers over Messenger and Telegram, involved only the top-performing models.
[O] Describe the Mechanical Turk protocol, because that is what decided the prize.
[G] A fresh worker is paired with a model. Both get personas. They chat for four to six turns each, in an interface the authors deliberately built to mirror the one used to collect Persona-Chat originally. Then the worker is asked "How much did you enjoy talking to this user?" on a one to four scale, and separately asked to pick the model's real persona out of two options. One hundred such conversations per model.
[S] One hundred. Remember that number.
[O] And there is a calibration step.
[G] There is. They applied a Bayesian calibration method from prior work to reduce annotator bias, and they report that the calibration did not affect the ordering of the scores. The final leaderboard numbers are post-calibration.
[O] So let us get to it. Who won the automatic round?
[G] Hugging Face, and not narrowly. They posted the best perplexity at sixteen point two eight, remembering lower is better there, the best hits at one at eighty point seven, and the best F one at nineteen point five. The paper says plainly that they performed the best in every single metric. For reference the provided baselines were the Key-Value Profile Memory retrieval model at hits at one of fifty five point two, an LSTM sequence-to-sequence model with attention at perplexity twenty nine point eight, and a plain language model baseline at perplexity forty six.
[O] A clean sweep. All three metrics, one team.
[S] And then humans looked at it.
[G] And then humans looked at it. On Mechanical Turk, Lost in Conversation won the competition with an engagingness score of three point one one out of four. Hugging Face, the team that had just swept every automatic metric, came second at two point six eight. Human-human pairs, for reference, scored three point four eight.
[O] So the automatic winner is not the winner.
[S] Which is the headline, and I want to be careful not to overread it. A gap of three point one one to two point six eight on a one to four scale, from one hundred conversations per model, with no confidence intervals printed anywhere in that table.
[G] That is a fair objection and I want to give it its full weight. The paper does assert that Lost in Conversation had a statistically significant win over Hugging Face. It does not report the test, and it does not report intervals in the table. Figure four does show error bars before and after calibration, and pre-calibration they are visibly wide.
[S] So we are told it is significant and asked to take it on faith.
[G] Essentially, yes. What they do instead is something different, and I think more interesting. They tried to rule out rater bias directly. They took a random sample of the Mechanical Turk logs from those two teams and re-scored them blind. The original Turkers had it two point eight to three point two nine in favour of Lost in Conversation. Blind annotator one had two point four seven to two point seven eight. Blind annotator two had two to two point seven one. All three orderings agree.
[O] So the reversal survives a second look.
[G] It survives a second look, but I want to state clearly who did the looking, because the paper does. Quote, we performed a blind evaluation ourselves of a random sample. That is the authors re-scoring their own competition's logs. It is blind with respect to which model produced which conversation. It is not independent of the organizers.
[S] Thank you. That distinction matters and it usually gets lost in the retelling.
[O] Noted, and I still think it is worth something. Two additional readers, both scoring lower in absolute terms than the Turkers did, and both preserving the ordering. That is a real check even if it is an in-house one.
[S] Agreed, it is evidence. It is just not the evidence people cite it as.
[O] Now the F one thing. Desmond, tell it properly.
[G] During the automatic stage the organizers found that always replying with the fixed string "i am you to do and your is like" would outperform the F one score of every model in the competition. Nine words, assembled simply by picking several frequent words from the training set. It scores an F one of nineteen point six on the test set and twenty point five on validation. Compare Hugging Face at nineteen point five on test and nineteen point one on validation.
[S] So a nine-word context-free nonsense string beats the best real system in the competition on a metric that was used to rank people into the human round.
[G] On the test set, yes. And the authors connect it to prior work, Liu and colleagues, who had already shown that word-overlap metrics correlate poorly with human judgement for dialogue. Their own conclusion in the paper is that this is another indication that we should reconsider using F one to evaluate dialogue systems.
[O] I want to defend the organizers slightly here. They found this, they published it, and they did not quietly drop it from the writeup.
[S] They also did not drop it from the ranking. Both things are true. And look at who benefits. ADAPT Centre ranks second overall largely on an F one of eighteen point three nine. A metric the same paper demonstrates is beatable by gibberish.
[G] That is a legitimate reading of the min-rank rule interacting with a broken metric, and the paper does not address that interaction directly.
[O] What about the revised personas? Did that control actually catch anything?
[G] It did, and this is one of the more satisfying results in the paper. Figure one shows hits at one on the original test set against the paraphrased set, for the five systems capable of ranking. The Key-Value Memory Network baseline deteriorates sharply once the personas no longer share literal words with the conversation, falling from fifty five point two to roughly thirty nine. Hugging Face drops from eighty point seven to somewhere around sixty seven, and Little Baby is close to unchanged, around sixty five either way.
[O] So the control fires on the baseline and mostly does not fire on the good systems. That is the instrument working.
[G] The authors read it that way, and attribute the robustness to sufficient pretraining and regularization among other factors. I would flag "among other factors" as doing real work in that sentence.
[S] There is a second probe though, and it is less flattering.
[G] There is. They added the partner's own last message to the candidate list as a distractor. A model should almost never parrot its partner, so hits at one ought to be roughly unchanged. Most models suffered substantially. Hugging Face was the most resistant, but the paper says it still suffered to some degree. The authors' read is that models were relying too heavily on word overlap with the last utterance when ranking.
[S] So the same shortcut the revised personas were meant to catch shows up on a different axis, and this time it catches the winners too.
[G] Yes. I think that is the correct reading of figures one and two taken together.
[O] Let me make the optimist case, and then you can both take it apart. What I see is a competition that ran three independent evaluation channels, published the disagreements between them, invented two adversarial controls, falsified one of its own metrics in print, and then went looking for a behavioural explanation when the leaderboards disagreed. In twenty eighteen. Most benchmarks today do not do half of that.
[S] Here is the deflationary case. The grand prize rests on one hundred paid conversations of four to six turns each, in an interface built to look like the training data, with no confidence intervals, ranked on a four-point subjective scale. The third channel, the wild volunteer chat, which is by far the closest thing here to real deployment, was thrown out entirely.
[O] Thrown out for a stated reason.
[S] For an excellent stated reason. [O] Why not? What is the mechanism?
[G] The authors offer one, and it is convincing. They say they often observed models repeating persona sentences almost verbatim, which gets you a high persona-detection score and a low engagingness score. Reciting your profile at someone makes you very identifiable and very boring.
[S] So then what actually explains the reversal at the top? Hugging Face swept the automatic metrics and lost the human round. Do they get an answer or just a shrug?
[G] They get a partial answer, and I think it is the most valuable thing in the paper. They went back to the blind annotators' written comments and noticed a recurring complaint that the Hugging Face model asked too many questions. So they counted. Across one hundred Mechanical Turk conversations per system, they measured how often a response opened with who, what, when, where, why, or how. Hugging Face did it one hundred and seven times. Humans did it twelve.
[O] One hundred and seven against twelve.
[G] And an appendix conversation makes it vivid. The bot asks, "what is your favorite rock? mine is rock." That conversation scored two out of four.
[S] So the failure is at the level of the conversation, not the utterance.
[G] Exactly, and that is the paper's real thesis. Their tentative conclusion, and they do label it tentative, is that over-asking questions hurt the human score. Now notice what none of the three automatic metrics can see. Perplexity scores a single gold response. F one compares a single predicted response to a single gold one. Hits at one picks a single utterance, and it does so against the gold human dialogue history, not against what the model itself said earlier. All three are per-utterance. The failure is a property of the whole exchange.
[O] So no amount of tuning those three metrics finds this.
[G] Not in principle, no. And the authors say as much in their conclusion. Future systems must go beyond single-word metrics like perplexity, and measure repetition, consistency, and balance of dialogue acts across a conversation.
[S] Did their other proxies find anything? They tried a few.
[G] They tried three and were honest that all three are muddy. On response length, higher-scoring models tended to draw longer replies from their human partner, which they read as implicit engagement, but they immediately note it may be humans mimicking the bot's length. On rare words, humans use far more of them than any model, and the bottom three models do score lower than the top three, but the paper says the relationship otherwise is not completely clear.
[O] And repetition?
[G] Repetition is the cleanest refutation of the whole proxy programme. If repetition drove engagingness you would expect the winner to repeat itself least. Lost in Conversation, the winner, produced unique responses eighty six percent of the time. Hugging Face, which it beat, was at ninety seven percent. Humans are at ninety nine. The paper's own verdict is that there is not a clear relationship.
[S] So the winner repeats itself more than the runner-up and still wins. That kills the simple story.
[G] It does, and to their credit they print it rather than burying it.
[O] Desmond, one more thing I want on the record. There is a training-data problem in the comparison itself, isn't there?
[G] There is, and the paper hands you the evidence without drawing the conclusion. Competitors were explicitly allowed to bring any additional training data as long as it was public. Per the paper's own model summary table, Lost in Conversation is a generative Transformer based on OpenAI GPT, trained on Persona-Chat plus DailyDialog plus Reddit comments. Hugging Face is a pretrained generative Transformer using the Billion Words corpus plus CoNLL twenty twelve, transferred to Persona-Chat.
[S] So the headline comparison is not architecture against architecture. It is one architecture-plus-corpus recipe against another.
