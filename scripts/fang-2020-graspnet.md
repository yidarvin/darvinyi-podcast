The paper reports four different magnitudes and they are wildly different sizes. One hundred and ninety cluttered scenes. Five hundred and twelve R G B D images per scene, giving ninety seven thousand two hundred and eighty images. Eighty eight objects, each with a high quality 3D mesh. And then between three million and nine million scored grasp poses per scene, which is where the billion comes from. Four units, four orders of magnitude apart. Quote any of them without the unit and you have said nothing.
[S] Anouk, before the method. What was broken in 2020 that needed a new benchmark?
[G] Two things, and they are separable. The first is representation. Grasp poses were being written down two incompatible ways. Either as a two dimensional oriented rectangle in the image plane, or as a full six degree of freedom pose in three dimensions. Each representation came with its own evaluation metric. So a rectangle method and a point cloud method could not be put on the same number, and the field was effectively running two leaderboards that could not see each other.
[O] And the rectangle metric had a specific problem.
[G] It had a specific and, by 2020, terminal problem. The Cornell dataset's metric counts a predicted grasp as correct if the rotation error is under thirty degrees and the rectangle intersection over union with some labeled grasp is above zero point two five. The paper's own diagnosis is that this tolerance is loose precisely because the ground truth annotations are not exhaustive. You cannot demand tight agreement with a sparse label set. And the consequence is in the paper in one sentence: Cornell had, by then, achieved over ninety nine percent accuracy.
[S] So the benchmark had stopped discriminating.
[G] It had stopped discriminating. And the paper's reading of that is not that grasping was solved. It is that the metric was over-estimating everyone.
[O] What is the second broken thing?
[G] Data. Human-annotated grasp datasets are small, because a person has to draw every rectangle. Look at the paper's own comparison table. Cornell: eight thousand and nineteen total grasps. The multi-object set from Chu and colleagues: two thousand nine hundred and four total grasps, across ninety six images. These are thousands, not millions. The escape hatch was simulation, and the simulated sets are genuinely large — Dex-Net 2.0 records six point seven million total grasps over fifteen hundred objects — but the paper's objection is the visual domain gap. Train on rendered depth, deploy on a real R G B D camera, lose performance.
[S] So real sensors, or scale. Pick one.
[G] That was the trade, yes. And the paper's contribution is a way of not picking.
[O] Which is what, mechanically?
[G] Do not annotate grasps in the scene at all. Annotate each object once, in isolation, by exhaustive analytic search over its mesh. Then track the object's six degree of freedom pose into every scene it ever appears in, and project the object's entire grasp set forward. You pay the annotation cost once per object, and you harvest it across every scene and every viewpoint.
[S] That is elegant. It also means the whole edifice rests on the pose tracking being right, and I want to come back to that.
[O] Take the object set first. Eighty eight objects — where from?
[G] Three sources. Thirty two selected from the Y C B object set. Thirteen adversarial objects from Dex-Net 2.0 — those are objects deliberately chosen to be hard to grasp. And forty three collected by the authors themselves. Eighty eight in total.
[O] And the scenes?
[G] Around ten objects picked at random and placed in clutter, one hundred and ninety times. A robot arm carries two R G B D cameras — an Intel RealSense four thirty five and a Kinect four Azure — along a fixed trajectory covering two hundred and fifty six distinct viewpoints on a quarter sphere. Two cameras, two hundred and fifty six viewpoints, five hundred and twelve images per scene. That is where ninety seven thousand two hundred and eighty comes from.
[S] Why two cameras? That doubles the collection cost.
[G] The stated reason is up front in section three point two, and it is a good one. Different quality of depth image will, in the paper's words, inevitably affect the algorithms. So they capture both and report both. Hold that thought, because the paper does something with it later that I do not think it finishes.
[O] Now the pose annotation. You said one annotation per object per scene.
[G] One manual six-D pose annotation per object, for the first frame of each scene. Every other frame's pose is obtained by composing that annotation with the recorded camera-to-camera transform — that is equation one. And the camera poses are not from forward kinematics. They photographed a fixed ArUco marker at all two hundred and fifty six collection points and solved for camera pose against the marker frame. The paper's own characterisation is that these camera poses are, quote, pretty accurate.
[S] Pretty accurate. That is the entire foundation of the dataset and the adjective is "pretty accurate", with no error bar.
[G] That is a fair reading of what is printed. There is no quantified calibration error in the main paper.
[O] Let us get to the scoring, because this is the part I actually find beautiful. How is a grasp graded?
[G] Force closure. Given a grasp pose, the associated object mesh, and a friction coefficient mu, the force closure metric returns a binary label: is this grasp antipodal under that coefficient, yes or no. It is a physical computation, not a learned one, and not a human judgment.
[O] And that binary becomes a score how?
[G] By sweeping. They start mu at one and decrease it in steps of zero point one, down to zero point one, until the grasp stops being antipodal. The lowest mu at which the grasp still holds is the mu that goes into the score. Then the score s equals one point one minus mu.
[S] Which gives you what range?
[G] It lies in the half open interval zero to one, with one included. Ten discrete levels, since mu takes ten values. And the direction is the intuitive one: a grasp that survives at low friction is a grasp that does not need the surface to be sticky to work. That is a better grasp, and it gets the higher score.
[S] I want to nail one thing down, Anouk, because I have seen it confused. Is mu a property of the grasp, or a knob on the evaluation?
[G] It is both, at two different times, and you are right to separate them. At annotation time, mu is a minimum they search for — the lowest friction at which this grasp still closes — and that minimum is what defines the label's score. At evaluation time, mu is a threshold that is supplied to the scorer, and the same predicted grasp gets a different binary label at different supplied mu. Same symbol, two roles. Annotation-time minimum, evaluation-time threshold.
[S] Good. That is the kind of thing that makes a reimplementation silently wrong.
[O] So the object-frame grasps get projected into the scenes.
[G] Projected using the annotated six-D object poses, which is equation three, with a collision check against a simplified gripper model to throw out grasps that would hit the background or another object. What survives is between three million and nine million scored grasp poses per scene. Across one hundred and ninety scenes, the paper says over one point one billion grasp poses, and the comparison table lists roughly one point two billion. And usefully, the label balance is stated: the ratio of positive to negative is around one to two.
[O] Now the evaluation protocol, which is the thing you said is the actual contribution.
[G] Here it is. For a predicted grasp — any predicted grasp, from any method, in any representation — the benchmark does not look for a nearby ground truth label. It associates the prediction with a target object by looking at the point cloud inside the gripper, and then runs the exact same force closure computation that generated the labels, at a supplied mu. The grasp is scored online, by the physics, not matched against a frozen list.
[O] And that is why the label set never becomes the ceiling.
[G] Precisely why. Grasp space is continuous and the set of feasible grasps is infinite. No human-labeled set is exhaustive, which is what forced Cornell's loose tolerance in the first place. If you score by the generating rule instead of by the label list, the incompleteness of the labels stops being your metric's problem.
[S] And the aggregate metric on top of that?
[G] Precision at k, because in real grasping you predict many candidates and execute, so what matters is the fraction of the top ranked ones that are true. Then two levels of averaging, and please do not swap them. A P sub mu is the mean of precision at k for k running from one to fifty, at one fixed friction mu. And the headline A P is the mean of A P sub mu over mu from zero point two to one point zero in steps of zero point two. So five friction levels. The analogy the paper draws is COCO reporting average precision at different intersection-over-union thresholds.
[S] So A P sub zero point eight and A P sub zero point four, which appear in the tables, are single-friction slices.
[G] Single-friction slices, yes. And there is a pose non-maximum suppression step run before scoring, so the metric is not dominated by fifty near-duplicate grasps on the same object. The details of that are deferred to the supplementary.
[O] And the splits.
[G] One hundred scenes for training, ninety for testing, and the test set is cut three ways: thirty scenes of seen objects, thirty of unseen but similar objects, and thirty of novel objects. Three graded levels of generalization. And I will say now what I will say again later — never quote a number from one of those splits as though it came from another. The gaps between them are enormous.
[S] They also built a network. Why, if the point is the benchmark?
[G] To have something to demonstrate the benchmark with, and because they had a genuine architectural idea. The idea is decoupling. Rather than regressing the pose matrix directly, they split a grasp into an approaching direction, an in-plane rotation about that axis, an approaching distance, and a gripper width, and predict each separately. A PointNet plus plus backbone with four set abstraction layers, radii zero point zero four, zero point one, zero point two and zero point three metres, produces per-point features.
[O] And the heads?
[G] Three. ApproachNet classifies each of one thousand and twenty four sampled points for graspability and scores three hundred candidate approach viewpoints. OperationNet takes cylinder-aligned point regions around the chosen approach vector and classifies in-plane rotation into twelve bins, covering zero to one hundred and eighty degrees only, because the gripper is symmetric — and regresses width and confidence across four gripper depth bins at one, two, three and four centimetres. And then ToleranceNet, which is the novel bit.
[S] Define the novel bit precisely.
[G] It predicts what they call the grasp affinity field: for a given grasp, the farthest perturbation in the sphere of nearby poses at which the grasp's score still exceeds zero point five. The motivation is human — people pick grasps that tolerate error, not grasps that are optimal on a knife edge. At inference it is used to re-rank, pushing robust grasps up the ordering.
[O] And the training is not exotic.
[G] Not remotely. One Nvidia R T X 2080. Adam. Batch size four. Learning rate zero point zero zero one, dropped to zero point zero zero zero one after sixty epochs and again by a factor of ten after one hundred. Twenty thousand points sampled per scene. This is a single-GPU paper.
