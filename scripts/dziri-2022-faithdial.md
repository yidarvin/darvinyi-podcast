[S] And here is the asterisk. The thing measuring that drop is a classifier trained on labels produced by the very annotation project that produced the improved data. Of course it likes the new data.
[O] The human raters like it too. On the same comparison, human-judged hallucination falls from 55.8 percent to 23.2.
[G] You are both right, and the structure of the paper is what makes that possible. There are two quite different bodies of evidence in here, and they deserve quite different amounts of trust. Sorting that out is most of the work.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio. Today we are on FaithDial, a faithful benchmark for information-seeking dialogue, by Nouha Dziri, Ehsan Kamalloo, Sivan Milton, Osmar Zaiane, Mo Yu, Edoardo Ponti and Siva Reddy. It appeared in TACL in 2022.
[S] I am the skeptic, and this one earns its place because it is a data-quality paper that doubles as an indictment of three benchmarks people were still training on at the time.
[G] Constance here, thank you for having me. I would frame the paper as an argument about where hallucination actually lives. The claim is not that the models were badly designed. The claim is that the supervision was already lying to them.
[O] Set up the task first, Constance. What does the data look like?
[G] It is the Wizard of Wikipedia format. A Seeker asks questions about a topic. A Wizard is handed a knowledge snippet, typically a single Wikipedia sentence, and is meant to answer using it. Crucially, in the original corpus the Wizard is a human, and those transcripts become the training data everyone downstream fine-tunes on.
[S] And the humans did not use the snippet.
[G] Frequently not. The paper leans on a companion study by Dziri and colleagues which found that more than 60 percent of three popular dialogue benchmarks are rife with hallucination. The human Wizards ignored the knowledge they were given and answered from a made-up persona instead. The paper's opening figure is a Wizard handed a snippet about a broken heart as a metaphor, who instead volunteers a story about breaking up with his girlfriend.
[O] Sixty percent is an average, though. Give me the split by dataset.
[G] Table 1 does. Full hallucination is 19.7 percent in Wizard of Wikipedia, 61.4 percent in CMU-DoG, and 46.8 percent in TopicalChat. That split is exactly why they picked Wizard of Wikipedia as the thing to edit. It is the least corrupted of the three, so there was the least to repair.
[S] Noisy supervision is not automatically a catastrophe, though. Models can be robust to label noise.
[G] Not here. The same companion study found that GPT two does not merely replicate the hallucination when trained on Wizard of Wikipedia. It amplifies it, by around 20 percent.
[O] That is the part that changes my priors. Amplification means the noise is not averaging out, it is being learned as a style.
[S] Then why not just filter? Drop the bad turns and train on what is left. That is the standard move.
[G] The paper addresses that directly and rejects it. Filtering a single turn out of a dialogue either breaks the cohesion of the discourse, because the next turn refers back to the one you removed, or it forces you to discard the entire conversation. And roughly four in five Wizard turns are implicated, so you would be throwing away most of the corpus.
[O] So they edit instead.
[G] They edit instead. That is the whole move, and it is worth appreciating how much cheaper it is than collecting a new corpus. You keep the conversational skeleton, the topics, the Seeker's curiosity, and you surgically repair the Wizard.
[S] Define repair. Because "make it faithful" is not an annotation instruction, it is a wish.
[G] They define it in three parts before touching any data. The Wizard should be truthful, meaning it only states information attributable to the knowledge snippet. It should be conversational, meaning it phrases that knowledge naturally, supports follow-up, prompts the Seeker's opinions. And it should acknowledge ignorance when the snippet does not cover the question, while still moving the conversation forward.
[O] That third one is the interesting one. It is not just deletion, it is teaching a bot to say it does not know.
[G] And to say it while remaining useful. In that same opening figure, the edited Wizard replies, I am a machine and I cannot feel pain, but I surely know that a broken heart is intense emotionally and physically. The bot persona is made explicit rather than suppressed, and the rest of the reply is grounded back in the snippet.
[S] Which workers, and how many turns?
[G] Amazon Mechanical Turk, and here is the number that I think should follow this paper everywhere it goes. After a qualification test, a pilot round of about 60 tasks with feedback and access revocation, and then a linguistics-major student reviewing roughly ten tasks per worker per day with repeated mistakes leading to a block, they ended up with 10 well-trained workers. Ten people produced the entire corpus.
[O] Ten well-trained people is not obviously worse than a hundred untrained ones.
[S] For consistency, no. For construct validity, yes. Ten people share conventions. And they were all located in the United States and Canada, which the paper states plainly.
[G] That is a fair reading and the paper does not contest it. I would add the scale for context: FaithDial totals 50,761 turns across 5,649 dialogues. Workers edited 84.7 percent of Wizard turns, which is 21,447 utterances, and 28.1 percent of Seeker turns, 7,172 utterances.
[O] Why edit the Seeker at all? The Seeker was not the one hallucinating.
[G] Repair work. If you rewrite the Wizard's turn, the Seeker's next line can stop making sense, so they fix it to restore coherence. And you can see it is secondary from the rates: on average 3.8 Wizard turns per conversation were modified against only 1.2 Seeker turns. The edits are surgical, not a full rewrite.
[S] What about the training split? Is this a training corpus or a benchmark?
[G] A benchmark, and the paper is explicit about that. They sampled 44 percent of the train set, 4,094 conversations, but 100 percent of validation, 764, and 100 percent of test, 791. The footnote says the smaller training set is a budget limitation, and that the goal is a high-quality faithful dialogue benchmark rather than a large-scale dataset for training.
[O] I want to give them credit for saying that out loud instead of pretending the sample was principled.
[S] Agreed, that is honest. Now the part I actually care about. How do they know the edits are any good?
[G] This is the weakest link, and I want to be precise about what they did. There is no clean way to measure inter-annotator agreement on free-text edits, so they validate indirectly, in two steps, both on the same 500 responses. First, three new workers re-label the attribution classes and the speech acts. That gives Fleiss kappa of 0.75 on the attribution taxonomy and 0.61 on the speech-act taxonomy.
[S] Which the paper calls substantial.
[G] Substantial by the Landis and Koch scale they cite, yes. Not near-perfect. Then, second, three further new workers judge the faithfulness of those same 500 already-edited responses by majority vote. Assuming the pre-existing labels are correct, the majority vote reproduces them at an F one of 90 percent on the attribution taxonomy and 81 percent on the speech-act taxonomy. On that basis they report the corpus is 94.4 percent faithful and 5.6 percent hallucinated.
[S] Stop there. Read back what that second step measures. Three new people were asked to apply the same taxonomy, and they agreed with the original labelers most of the time. That is reliability. It is not validity. Nobody outside this annotation pipeline ever checked whether a FaithDial response is actually faithful.
[G] That is a correct characterization, and I will not soften it. The 94.4 percent figure is a statement about agreement on a schema that this same research group designed, applied through ten workers, and then re-measured with more workers using the same schema.
[O] Let me steelman it, though. Faithfulness to a knowledge snippet is not a matter of external fact. It is a relation between two texts that are both on the page. That is much closer to an objective judgment than, say, rating helpfulness.
[G] That is the strongest version of the defense and I think it has real force. The task is entailment-like, and entailment is the kind of thing where high agreement means something. But it does not fully dissolve the objection, because the taxonomy still decides where partial hallucination ends and entailment begins, and that boundary is a design choice.
[O] Fair. Take us to the results. What does the before-and-after look like?
[G] The headline chart puts the two corpora side by side under the same taxonomy. FaithDial is 94.4 percent entailment, 5.6 percent hallucinated. Their own large-scale audit of the entirety of Wizard of Wikipedia gives 20.9 percent entailment, 59.4 percent partial hallucination, 12 percent full hallucination, and 6.8 percent uncooperative. Twelve plus 59.4 is the 71.4 percent hallucination figure they quote in the text.
[S] Hold on. Table 1 said Wizard of Wikipedia was 19.7 percent full hallucination and 42.3 percent partial. Now the same dataset is 12 and 59.4. Those are different numbers.
[G] They are, and this is worth flagging carefully rather than resolving. Those are two different audits. Table 1 is a schema from the prior paper applied to a sample, and it is presented as prior work. The figure is described as this paper's own large-scale audit of the entirety of Wizard of Wikipedia. They agree roughly on the combined unfaithful total, around 62 versus 71 percent, but the category boundaries do not line up.
[S] So the same paper reports its motivating dataset's corruption rate two different ways and never reconciles them.
[G] Yes. I would not call it an error, because the sources are different and both are labeled. I would call it an unreconciled seam that a reader should not average across.
[O] There is a detail in that chart I liked, which is the speech-act breakdown. It is not just faithful, it is conversationally varied.
[G] It is a three-level chart, and the levels matter. Inside FaithDial's faithful slice, 78.26 percent is edification, meaning objective content delivery, against 13.04 percent disclosure and 8.6 percent question standing on their own. Then inside that edification slice, the Wizard interleaves disclosure at 36.2 percent, question at 35.5, and acknowledgment at 18.3.
[O] So the faithful turns are not just reciting Wikipedia at you.
[G] That is the argument, and there is a second measurement supporting it. Using density and coverage from Grusky and colleagues, where density measures how much of a response is copied verbatim from the snippet, FaithDial's responses cluster near the density floor across the same coverage range where Wizard of Wikipedia's mass sits high. Paraphrase, not extraction. Copying spans from the snippet was explicitly forbidden and automatically checked.
[S] Good. Now the critic, because that is where I want to push. They train a hallucination classifier on these labels.
[G] They do. It is called FaithCritic. Fourteen thousand Wizard of Wikipedia utterances labeled hallucination as negatives, twenty thousand positives from entailment-labeled originals plus the newly edited turns, thirty-four thousand training examples, RoBERTa large as the backbone. In-domain accuracy is 86.5 percent. Zero-shot onto the BEGIN attribution benchmark it gets 71.6 percent, against 61.1 for a critic trained on MNLI, 59.8 for one trained on DNLI, and 58.8 for DECODE.
[O] Ten and a half points of zero-shot headroom over the best alternative is a real result.
[G] It is. And I will flag the honest surprise in that table too, which the authors themselves call surprising: the MNLI-trained critic transfers to FaithCritic at 81.6 percent zero-shot. Generic entailment training captures a lot of the same signal.
[S] Which slightly deflates the novelty. If off-the-shelf natural language inference gets you to 81.6 on their own task, how special is the dataset.
[G] Special enough on the direction that matters, is my read. FaithCritic transfers to BEGIN better than MNLI does, and BEGIN is the dialogue-specific target. But your point stands that the two are closer than the framing suggests.
[O] I want to raise something the writeup on the site caught, about that abstract.
[G] Yes, and I think this is the sharpest thing to say about that table. The abstract claims the critic boosts performance by 12.8 F one on the BEGIN benchmark. That table reports accuracy, not F one, and the gap in it is 10.5, not 12.8. There is no F one on BEGIN table anywhere in the main text or the appendix. So the abstract's headline number is not something a reader can locate in the paper.
[S] That is not a rounding quibble. That is the number in the abstract.
[G] It is, and I would treat it as a quote from the abstract rather than a verified result. The site's writeup handles it correctly, by the way. It reports the 10.5-point accuracy gap and does not repeat the 12.8 figure.
[O] Let us get to generation, because that is the main event.
[G] Seven systems: GPT two, DialoGPT, DoHA, T five, and then T five fitted with a controllable-generation head, with loss truncation, or with the authors' own contrastive objective. Six of the seven are trained three ways: on Wizard of Wikipedia, on FaithDial, and on Wizard of Wikipedia then FaithDial. The contrastive variant only gets the last two, because its objective needs faithful positive examples that Wizard of Wikipedia cannot supply.
[S] Before the numbers, tell me what the metrics are measured against. That is where these tables usually go wrong.
[G] Good instinct, and the answer is more careful than you might expect. In that table, BERTScore and word-overlap F one are both scored against the knowledge snippet, not against the gold response. Only BLEU and ROUGE are scored against the gold response. And the faithfulness metric Q two is also against the knowledge.
[S] So the gold-response metrics are scored against text those same ten workers wrote.
[G] Correct, and BLEU and ROUGE are the two I would weight least for that reason. But the faithfulness metrics are anchored to the Wikipedia snippet instead, and two of them, Q two and BERTScore, come from outside this project entirely.
[O] Give me the headline swap.
[G] Hold T five fixed. Trained on Wizard of Wikipedia, its critic-flagged hallucination is 46.5. Trained on FaithDial, 4.3. A 42.2 point drop. Q two on the entailment variant rises from 75.2 to 79.5.
[S] The 4.3 point Q two gain is the one I would not lean on.
[G] Nor would the authors, and to their credit they say so in a footnote. They warn that T five trained on Wizard of Wikipedia may have a relatively high Q two score precisely because that metric is not robust to partial hallucinations. They flag their own number as possibly inflated.
[O] That is a real mark in their favor. What about the contrastive objective they propose?
[G] Within the FaithDial-trained block, it reaches a critic score of 1.4, the lowest of any model in that block. And on word overlap with the knowledge it scores 55.8, which is the lowest among the four T five backbone variants — the plain model is 59.2, the controllable one 62.2, loss truncation 59.1.
[S] Lowest word overlap sounds like a bad thing until you remember what they are arguing.
[G] Exactly. Low word overlap with the snippet means it is not solving faithfulness by copying. The paper's own phrasing is that it achieves both faithfulness and abstractiveness. But I want that claim scoped properly, because it is easy to overstate. GPT two and DialoGPT score lower still on that metric, 50.4 and 52.3.
[O] So the claim is false?
[G] No, the claim is about the T five family, and within that family it holds. GPT two and DialoGPT are different backbones, and they buy that low overlap by being far more prone to hallucination — critic scores of 5.8 and 5.6 against the contrastive model's 1.4. Low overlap alone is not a virtue. Low overlap with low hallucination is.
[S] Fine. I accept the scoping. Now human evaluation, because automatic metrics on your own benchmark are the least interesting evidence here.
[G] Sixteen hundred responses, 200 per model across eight models, three raters each, with Krippendorff's alpha between 0.75 and 0.92 across the seven rated qualities. The contrastive model is the best in every single column: 97.2 percent interpretable, 19.9 percent hallucination, and the top cooperativeness, abstractiveness and engagingness scores.
[O] And the data swap in human terms?
[G] The same T five, Wizard of Wikipedia versus FaithDial: hallucination falls from 55.8 percent to 23.2. A 32.6 point drop, judged by people, not by their classifier.
[S] That is the number that moves me most so far, and I want to say so. It answers my opening objection. The critic and the humans agree on direction and roughly on magnitude.
[O] Noted and appreciated.
[S] But it does not answer the other objection. The model was fine-tuned on FaithDial and tested on FaithDial's own test set, graded partly by FaithDial's own conventions. It might just be reproducing ten people's editing style.
[G] And that is precisely the objection the last experiment is built to answer, which is why I think it is the most important table in the paper.
[O] Zero-shot transfer.
[G] Take the FaithDial-trained model, do not fine-tune it any further, and test it on TopicalChat, CMU-DoG, and Wizard of Wikipedia, against a same-architecture model trained in-domain on each of those targets. On TopicalChat the critic score drops 35.7 points, from 95.0 to 59.3. Q two on entailment gains 13.9. Human-judged hallucination falls 30.4 points, from 71.4 percent to 41.0.
[O] And CMU-DoG?
[G] The critic drops 73.7 points, 95.5 to 21.8. Human hallucination falls 20 points, 68.4 to 48.4. And on Wizard of Wikipedia itself, human hallucination falls 23.8 points, 48.0 to 24.2. A model that never saw any of these training sets beats the model trained on each of them, on faithfulness.
[S] That is a strong result and I will grant it. Now tell me what it costs, because I refuse to believe there is no cost.
[G] There is, and it is visible in that same table. Across three datasets and eight metrics, there are exactly four cells where the in-domain baseline beats the FaithDial-trained model. On Wizard of Wikipedia, word overlap with the knowledge falls slightly, 59.6 to 57.4, and the paper flags that exception explicitly. On TopicalChat, cooperativeness drops from 3.53 to 3.07 and engagingness from 2.56 to 2.20. On CMU-DoG, cooperativeness drops from 3.43 to 3.29.
[O] Four cells out of twenty-four is a good trade.
[S] It is a good trade, but the pattern is not random. Three of those four are the conversational-quality columns. The model got more faithful and less pleasant to talk to.
[G] That is a fair summary of the direction, and the paper states it in one sentence: the FaithDial models tested on TopicalChat and CMU-DoG enjoy a larger degree of abstractiveness than in-domain models but have lower scores of cooperativeness and engagingness. I will add one correction to the pessimistic reading, though. On CMU-DoG, engagingness goes the other way, and substantially: 1.57 up to 2.14.
[S] So it is not a uniform tax on engagement.
[G] Not uniform. On the dataset that was most corrupted to begin with, CMU-DoG, at 61.4 percent full hallucination, the FaithDial model is both more faithful and more engaging. The engagement cost shows up on TopicalChat.
[O] Then let me make the optimist case properly. This paper demonstrates that a benchmark's headline failure mode was in the supervision, not the architecture, and that fixing 21,447 utterances buys you a model that generalizes to datasets it never saw. That is a better return on effort than most modeling papers deliver.
[S] And the deflationary case. Every faithfulness number in here is defined by a taxonomy this group wrote, applied by ten people in two countries, and validated by measuring how well more people agree with that same taxonomy. And the metric carrying the largest headline drops, the 73.7 points on CMU-DoG, is a classifier trained on this project's own labels that is never once checked against human judgment anywhere in the paper.
[O] Is that last part actually true, Constance? It seems like an odd gap.
[G] It is true, and I checked it carefully. The critic section reports only transfer accuracy across MNLI, BEGIN and FaithCritic. There is no experiment anywhere correlating the critic's output with human faithfulness ratings, despite the critic being a headline column in both the generation table and the transfer table. That is the single most surprising omission in the paper.
[S] Thank you.
[G] But I want to score this properly rather than hand it to either of you. The critic is not load-bearing on its own, because the human evaluation runs alongside it in both tables and moves in the same direction. Where the critic says 35.7 points on TopicalChat, humans say 30.4. Where the critic says a large drop on Wizard of Wikipedia, humans say 23.8. The two instruments corroborate each other even though neither was calibrated against the other.
[O] So the conclusion survives even if you delete the critic entirely.
[G] Broadly, yes, on the transfer results. That is my read. Where I think the skeptic wins outright is the in-domain table: training on FaithDial and testing on FaithDial, graded partly by FaithDial's own critic and partly against FaithDial's own gold text, is close to circular. The transfer experiment is where this paper actually earns its claim.
[S] One more thing while we are counting problems. There is a small internal inconsistency in the appendix worth naming.
[G] There is. The abstractiveness-strategy analysis is introduced in the appendix text as a manual annotation of 150 responses, and the caption of the table it produces says the manual analysis covered 200 responses. Two different counts, two pages apart, for what is presented as the same annotation round.
[O] Does it change any conclusion?
[G] No. Those five rows are illustrative examples with frequencies, not a corpus-wide partition — they sum to about 59 percent, not 100. So nothing downstream depends on it. But combined with the two different Wizard of Wikipedia audits, it is the second place in this paper where a reader has to decide which of two numbers to believe.
[S] Which for a paper whose whole thesis is annotation quality, is a slightly awkward look.
[O] That is a fair jab. Implications, then. What should someone building evaluations take from this?
[G] Two things. First, audit your training corpus with the same seriousness you audit your test set. This paper's most durable contribution may simply be establishing that three widely used dialogue benchmarks were majority-hallucinated, which nobody had quantified at that scale.
[O] And the second?
[G] Watch what your metric was trained on. This paper is a clean case study in the trap: build a dataset, train a classifier on that dataset's labels, then use the classifier to score models trained on that dataset. Every step is reasonable, and the composite is circular. The escape hatch they used, and it is the right one, is to move the evaluation to data nobody in the pipeline touched.
[S] My takeaway is that the data-centric diagnosis holds and the certification does not. Rewriting the corpus demonstrably reduces hallucination, including out of domain, which is real. But "94.4 percent faithful" should be read as "94.4 percent faithful according to a taxonomy this group wrote and re-measured against itself", and the classifier doing the loudest work is never checked against a person.
[O] Mine is that the trade is worth it and the effort is replicable. Ten trained annotators, roughly fifty thousand turns, and you flip a benchmark from 20.9 percent faithful to 94.4, with gains that survive being moved to two datasets the model never saw. That is a template, not a one-off.
[G] And the paper's own takeaway, stated as they state it: hallucination in knowledge-grounded dialogue is substantially a property of the data rather than the model, and a curated edit of an existing corpus is a more efficient fix than collecting a new one. The cost, which they name in a single sentence and which I would name louder, is a measurable drop in cooperativeness out of domain.
