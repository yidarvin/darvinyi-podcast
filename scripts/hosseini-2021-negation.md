[O] Ask BERT to finish the sentence, the capital of Cuba is mask, and it says Havana, confident and correct, exactly what you want from a model that has read most of the internet.
[S] Now add one word. Ask it the capital of Cuba is not mask, and it still says Havana.
[O] Which sounds like a party trick until you remember this exact model family sits underneath real fact lookup and reasoning pipelines today.
[S] It is not a party trick, it is the entire premise of today's paper, a two thousand twenty-one paper out of Mila, McGill, and Microsoft Research that tries to teach BERT what the word not actually does to a sentence.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio, where an optimist, a skeptic, and a guest scholar take apart one paper from the litsearch site. Today's paper is Understanding by Understanding Not, Modeling Negation in Language Models, by Arian Hosseini, Siva Reddy, Dzmitry Bahdanau, Devon Hjelm, Alessandro Sordoni, and Aaron Courville, published at NAACL two thousand twenty-one, and cited well over one hundred times since.
[S] Joining us is Eleanor, who has spent a lot of time inside this paper's tables. Eleanor, welcome.
[G] Thanks for having me. It is a short paper, twelve pages, but it earns every one of them, and the sharpest thing about it is exactly the tension the two of you just set up in the open.
[G] Negation shows up everywhere, sentiment analysis, question answering, knowledge base completion, natural language inference, so this is not a niche linguistics complaint. Nora Kassner and Hinrich Schutze established the diagnosis in two thousand nineteen, showing pretrained language models cannot reliably tell a negated cloze prompt from its positive twin, and that Havana example is actually their finding, restated in this paper's own introduction as the motivating case.
[O] So the model is not reasoning about the logical content of not, it is pattern matching on the subject and ignoring the negation token.
[G] That is the authors framing exactly. Separately, Aakanksha Naik and colleagues ran stress tests on natural language inference and found the same instinct, models see the word not or no anywhere in a sentence and lean toward predicting contradiction, even when the correct label is neutral or entailment. Then Hossain and colleagues built dedicated negation splits of RTE, SNLI, and MNLI in two thousand twenty, and showed state of the art models still do badly on them. So by two thousand twenty-one you have two solid diagnoses and no general fix.
[S] What about the negation scope and focus detection literature, people like Fancellu and Lu, that is not nothing.
[G] It is real work, but it is a different job, it treats finding the span a negation covers as its own parsing subtask, not something a language model's ordinary predictive distribution should just get right on its own. Nobody had shown a training objective that goes straight at a pretrained model's negation blindness, and this paper is careful about how it tests that, it holds out the two benchmarks that diagnosed the problem, negated LAMA, the knowledge probing test built from fill in the blank facts, and the negation NLI splits, purely for evaluation. Zero negated LAMA examples and zero negation NLI examples touch the training data.
[O] So whatever gain shows up on those two benchmarks is out of distribution by construction, worth remembering for later.
[G] The recipe itself has two halves. First you need negated sentences at scale, and second you need a loss that actually uses them to suppress the wrong answer.
[S] Start with the sentences, because manufacturing negation automatically sounds easy to get wrong.
[G] It is genuinely careful engineering. They mine declarative sentences from Wikipedia, parse each one with Stanza for its dependency tree, part of speech tags, and morphology, and throw out anything over twenty words. Then they match the parse against a hand built library of patterns, one per syntactic construction, simple past, simple present, imperative, present tense with an auxiliary or a modal, past perfect, copula sentences, sentences already negated with not, negative polarity words like anywhere or anyone, and a handful of others. Each matched pattern triggers a small set of actions, move a token, replace a token, insert a token, or lemmatize it, and the output is a grammatically negated sentence plus one flagged word, the unlikelihood token, the word that should now be false.
[O] Give me one concrete example of the transform in action.
[G] One of their appendix examples starts with, that tournament helped demonstrate the high caliber of play in women's soccer. The negator turns it into, that tournament did not help demonstrate the high caliber of play in women's soccer, and flags tournament as the unlikelihood token, the word BERT should now find implausible given the negated sentence.
[S] That still sounds like a hand engineered pile of grammar rules, not a learned system. How do you know it is not just overfit to Wikipedia's most common sentence shapes?
[G] It is hand engineered, that is fair, but the coverage numbers argue against a narrow fit. They sampled nine hundred thirty Wikipedia sentences, and eight hundred ninety-nine of them, ninety-six point seven percent, matched some rule. Then a native English speaker manually checked one hundred random negations for correctness, and ninety-four percent were grammatically right. So the failures are real but a small minority, mostly odd verb forms after the transformation.
[S] Okay, so now you have a pile of negated sentences. What stops the model from just learning to distrust every sentence with a not in it?
[G] This is the actual contribution, what they call unlikelihood training with reference. You never train on a negated sentence alone, you train on a pair. Sentence A is left completely untouched, the factual reference. Sentence B is A's negated form, with the unlikelihood token replaced by a mask. You concatenate A and B and feed the whole thing into BERT, then penalize whatever probability BERT assigns to the true, now false token at that mask position, with A sitting right there in context telling it the sentence is false.
[O] So the model is not told in the abstract that negation flips meaning, it is shown one true sentence right next to its false twin, and punished specifically for still believing the twin.
[G] Exactly, and the loss itself is simple, negative log of one minus the model's probability on that wrong token, so the closer the model's probability on the false answer gets to one, the harder the loss pushes back. On its own this is dangerous, though, a model that just learns to always predict something implausible after any mask has not learned negation, it has learned to be useless.
[S] So how do they stop that collapse?
[G] Two guardrails. For every contradictory pair, they also train on a matched non-contradictory pair, where B is just A with one token masked, no negation at all, and there they apply a knowledge distillation loss, a KL divergence that pulls the fine-tuned model's prediction back toward whatever the original, un-fine-tuned BERT would have said. Separately, they run a second distillation pass on plain unmodified Wikipedia sentences with no paired structure at all, purely to guard against forgetting ordinary language modeling. The unlikelihood loss and the paired KL loss are combined with a weight of zero point four on the unlikelihood term.
[O] And the training recipe itself is lightweight, this is not a from scratch pretraining run.
[G] Not remotely. This is BERT base cased, further trained for five epochs, twenty thousand examples per objective, batch size thirty-two, a small learning rate around one times ten to the minus five. They call the result BERTNOT. And the detail that matters most for how you should read the results, not one negated LAMA example and not one negation NLI example is anywhere in that training data, both are held out completely, purely for evaluation.
[S] Quick check before the numbers, how do you know the reference sentence is actually doing something, and this is not just unlikelihood training working on its own?
[G] They checked exactly that, it is footnote territory but it matters. Strip out the reference sentence entirely, just apply the unlikelihood loss to a negated sentence in isolation with no true twin alongside it, and negated LAMA error only comes down to about eight point one four percent, barely half the improvement you get with the reference in place, three point nine five percent. So the reference is not a minor implementation detail, it is most of the mechanism, grounding the unlikelihood signal in something the model can verify against does the bulk of the work.
[O] That is a clean ablation, good.
[O] Let's get to numbers. Negated LAMA first, since that is the headline.
[G] Plain pretrained BERT gets a mean top one error rate of nine point oh one percent, averaged across the four subsets, SQuAD, ConceptNet, T-REx, and Google-RE. BERTNOT brings that down to three point nine five percent, and it wins every single one of the four subsets outright, not just on average.
[O] That is basically a five point drop in error, exactly what the abstract promises, get it down to about four percent.
[G] Five point oh six points, if you want the exact number. There is a nice ablation buried in there too, a BERT plus KL variant, distillation only, no unlikelihood term, gets to seven point nine eight percent on its own. So distillation alone buys about one point of that five point improvement, and the unlikelihood loss, the actual novel piece, is doing the other four.
[S] And the cost. What happens to ordinary, non negated factual recall, because a model that gets negation right by refusing to commit to anything is not a win.
[G] That is the good news for the paper. Original LAMA precision barely moves, seventeen point one three mean precision at one for plain BERT, seventeen point two six for BERTNOT, so if anything it is very slightly better on average. It does lose one individual column though, ConceptNet, fifteen point four nine versus BERT's fifteen point six five, so it is not a clean sweep in that direction, but there is no broad recall cost.
[O] Eleanor, can we do the Havana move again, one concrete before and after?
[G] There is a lovely one in their qualitative table. Ask BERT, Mac OS is developed by mask, it says Apple at a log probability of negative one point nine. Negation made BERT more confident in the wrong answer.

[G] It is. And BERTNOT, on that same negated query, flips its top prediction to Microsoft.
