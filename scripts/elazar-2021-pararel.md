[S] Set the table for us. Where does this sit in twenty twenty-one?
[G] By early twenty twenty-one there is already a real literature treating masked language models like BERT and RoBERTa as implicit knowledge bases. You feed in a cloze prompt, something like "Dante was born in mask," and read the model's top prediction off as a fact. LAMA started this, LPAQA extended it with mined paraphrases, AutoPrompt automated the prompt search entirely.
[O] And all of them report one number.
[G] Exactly one: how often the top prediction is correct. Accuracy. Nothing in that line of work checks whether the model gives the same answer when you ask the same fact a different way.
[S] Why does that matter, beyond tidiness?
[G] Because the entire appeal of treating a language model as a knowledge base is that you can query it in free natural language instead of a fixed schema. If "Homeland originally aired on mask" and "Homeland premiered on mask" pull different answers out of the same frozen weights, the model is not storing a fact, it is pattern-matching a phrasing.
[O] There is prior work on consistency generally, though, right? This is not the first paper to use the word.
[G] Right, and the authors place themselves carefully in that line. Ribeiro and colleagues studied consistency in question-answering models. Li and colleagues looked at consistency in natural language inference. Kassner and Schütze showed models will affirm "birds can fly" and "birds cannot fly" with almost equal confidence depending on how you ask, which is consistency under negation rather than under paraphrase. What none of that gives you is a systematic, multi-relation resource built specifically to measure paraphrase consistency of factual knowledge, across several models at once, plus a first attempt at fixing it.
[S] Define consistency the way the paper defines it. Precisely.
[G] A model is consistent on a relation if, given two so-called quasi-paraphrase cloze patterns for the same subject, it makes non-contradictory predictions across a large set of entities. Quasi-paraphrase is a term borrowed from Bhagat and Hovy — a looser, more practical notion than strict logical paraphrase.
[O] And "consistent" here has nothing to do with being right.
[G] Nothing at all, and that is the single most important design decision in the paper. A model that confidently and consistently names the wrong city as someone's birthplace is fully consistent by this definition. Correctness is tracked completely separately, as Accuracy. The Consistency score for a relation is simply the share of agreeing pattern pairs — for every subject, you check every possible pair among that relation's patterns, and Consistency is the percentage of those pairs where the two predictions agree.
[S] What about relations where more than one answer is legitimately correct? Shares-a-border-with, that kind of thing.
[G] Those many-to-many relations are pulled out of consistency scoring entirely and scored instead for determinism, meaning does the model at least return results in a stable order. Seven of the thirty-eight relations fall into that bucket; the other thirty-one, the many-to-one relations, carry the main Consistency numbers.
[O] Now the resource itself, PARAREL. Walk me through how you actually build three hundred twenty-eight trustworthy paraphrases.
[G] Four steps, and none of them alone would be enough. Start from LAMA's one base pattern per relation, across thirty-eight of LAMA's forty-one T-REx relations — three were dropped for being poorly defined or too heterogeneous. Second, pull in the subset of LPAQA's automatically mined paraphrases, produced by back-translation and by sentence extraction, that the authors manually judged to actually be correct paraphrases, and discard the rest. Third, mine further patterns straight from Wikipedia using SPIKE, a syntax-aware search engine, by searching for sentences containing a known subject-object pair and manually extracting the pattern connecting them. Fourth, add more from the annotators' own linguistic judgment, then have two additional experts review every single pattern and discard anything they could not agree on.
[S] That's four human passes on three hundred twenty-eight patterns. What does that buy you, on average, per relation?
[G] Eight point six three patterns per relation on average, as few as two for the sparsest relation and as many as twenty for the richest. The paper even splits that variety into syntactic versus lexical terms: an average of four point seven four distinct syntactic forms and six point oh three distinct lexical forms per relation.
[O] And then they checked their own work against human annotators.
[G] They did, and it's worth stating precisely, because it matters later. For each relation they sampled up to five paraphrase candidates against the base pattern, populated with real T-REx subjects and objects, plus mismatched control pairs pulled from different relations as a check. That's one hundred fifty-six candidate paraphrase pairs and sixty-one controls, shown to NLP graduate students, who judged whether each pair actually was a paraphrase. One annotation per pair. Agreement came back at ninety-five point five percent on the real paraphrase pairs and ninety-eight point three percent on the controls.

[O] Before we get to numbers, there's a restriction on what the models are even allowed to answer, right?

[G] Easier, and the paper says so directly. [S] Give me the extractability numbers first, table two, before we get to the headline table.

Every one of the seven real models tested hits a perfect one hundred percent Succ-Patt. Even the trivial majority baseline, the one that always predicts a relation's single most common object, falls slightly short of that, at ninety-seven point three percent, since it never actually reads a pattern to begin with.

[G] Right. Succ-Objs is the more interesting number: the share of entities predicted correctly by at least one of a relation's patterns. [S] And Unk-Const, Know-Const?
[G] Those split consistency by whether the model ever got the fact right at all. [S] Let's get to the fix. They don't just diagnose the problem.
[G] Section eight. They propose continuing pretraining with a new consistency loss. For a given relation, you encode every paraphrased pattern for a subject, and pull the model's output distributions over the candidate set toward each other with a two-sided K L divergence term, summed over every pair of patterns in that relation. The straightforward version of this objective, forcing the actual top-one predictions to match exactly, is discrete and non-differentiable, so they relax it to matching the full softmax distributions instead.
[O] And they keep the ordinary masked-language-modeling loss running alongside it.
[G] They have to.
