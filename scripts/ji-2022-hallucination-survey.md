[O] Almost every paper you have read in the last four years that uses the words intrinsic and extrinsic hallucination is pointing, directly or through one hop, at this survey. Five thousand four hundred and eighty citations. It is the closest thing the field has to a shared definition of what hallucination even means.
[S] And the survey's own text says most of the literature it organizes does not actually use that distinction. Not as a rhetorical flourish. As a stated finding, in the future-directions section. Most existing hallucination metrics, in its words, measure intrinsic and extrinsic hallucinations together as a unified metric.
[O] Which I would call an honest survey rather than a broken taxonomy.
[S] I would call it the most interesting sentence in fifty-nine pages, and the one that nobody citing this paper's categories seems to have read.
[O] This is Litsearch Audio. Today it is Survey of Hallucination in Natural Language Generation, by Ziwei Ji, Nayeon Lee, Rita Frieske, Tiezheng Yu, Dan Su, Yan Xu and colleagues, out of the Center for Artificial Intelligence Research at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, with Pascale Fung as senior author. First posted to arXiv in February 2022, published in ACM Computing Surveys.
[S] It is on the docket because it is a rare thing. A paper whose contribution is vocabulary. No model, no benchmark, no experiment. And vocabulary is exactly the kind of contribution that gets cited without ever being checked.
[O] So Ambrose is here, a researcher who has read this one closely, including the parts that grew after publication. Ambrose, welcome. Where do you want to start?
[G] With which paper we are talking about, because there are seven of them, and the differences matter more than usual here.
[S] Seven. Unpack that.
[G] The arXiv entry has revisions one through seven. Version one, February 2022, runs forty-one pages. Version seven, July 2024, which is what you get if you click through today, runs fifty-nine. So it grew by eighteen pages. About forty-four percent.
[O] That is not the number I had in my head. I would have said it roughly doubled.
[G] A lot of people say that, and it is not right. Forty-four percent is real growth, but it is not doubling. And the composition matters more than the total. About seven of those eighteen pages are a single thing, an entirely new section thirteen on hallucination in large language models, carrying its own footnote that reads, this section was updated in January 2024.
[S] So the remaining eleven pages are what?
[G] Bibliography growth, mostly. Version seven's reference list runs to three hundred and twenty-nine entries. Plus additions scattered through existing sections. And I want to be careful, because this is where people over-claim. The paper does not tell you where those additions landed. It is not the case that the six task sections were substantially rewritten. Nothing in the document localizes the residual growth.
[O] So somebody who knows this survey by reputation from 2022 may be citing a paper meaningfully different from the one on arXiv today.
[G] Yes, and in one specific direction. The large-language-model material is the part that postdates version one, and it is the part a 2024 or 2025 reader is most likely to think the survey has always said.
[S] Right. Then let us do the thing the paper is famous for, and let us start with attribution, because I have repeatedly seen this survey credited with coining intrinsic versus extrinsic.
[G] It does not coin it, and it says so in the first line of the categorization subsection. Following the categorization from previous works, with three citations attached. What it did was different, and I would argue harder. It took a distinction that already existed, mostly in the summarization literature, and applied it consistently across six tasks in one document.
[O] Give me the definitions cleanly.
[G] Intrinsic hallucination is generated output that contradicts the source content. Extrinsic hallucination is generated output the source can neither support nor contradict. Their worked example, in Table 1, is a short passage saying the first vaccine for Ebola was approved by the FDA in 2019. A summary saying it was approved in 2021 is intrinsic, because it contradicts the source. A summary adding that China has already started clinical trials of a COVID nineteen vaccine is extrinsic, because the source neither states it nor denies it.
[S] And the second one is not necessarily wrong.
[G] Correct, and the paper concedes that in the same subsection. Extrinsic hallucination, in its words, is not always erroneous because it could be from factually correct external information. It can even be helpful, because it adds background knowledge and improves informativeness. Most of the literature still treats it with caution, because unverifiable is a risk even when it happens to be true.
[S] So one branch of the field's canonical two-way split contains both the model helpfully adding a true fact and the model asserting something nobody can check. That is a very wide box.
[O] It is a wide box, and I would still take it over what came before, which was six literatures using six different vocabularies for the same failure.
[G] That is the strongest defense of the paper, and it is made explicitly in the task-comparison subsection. What counts as the source is different in every task. In summarization it is the input document. In data-to-text it is non-linguistic data, a table or a box score. In dialogue it is the dialogue history and any external knowledge sentences. And the tolerance differs too. Very low for summarization and data-to-text, deliberately higher for open-domain dialogue, because there you want engagement and diversity, not only fidelity.
[O] That is the insight, right there. The same word indexes a different relation in each task, and this is the first place it is laid out side by side.
[G] With one honest gap that the paper flags itself. For generative question answering it says outright that there is no standard definition or categorization yet, and that the literature mainly treats the source as world knowledge. And for machine translation it says the categorizations vary within the task and the terms often overlap.
[S] Say more about faithfulness versus factuality, because I suspect that is where the modern confusion actually comes from.
[G] Terminology clarification, section two point three. Faithfulness is staying consistent with the provided source, the antonym of hallucination. Factuality is being consistent with fact. Whether those are the same thing depends entirely on what you let fact mean. Maynez and colleagues define the fact as world knowledge, which makes factuality and faithfulness distinct. Dong and colleagues use the source input as the fact, which collapses them into one. The survey explicitly adopts the Maynez reading.
[O] And that choice is load-bearing for everything downstream.
[S] Causes. What does the paper claim actually produces this behaviour?
[G] Two families. From data, the core mechanism is source-reference divergence. Your training targets contain information your sources do not support, so the model learns to produce exactly that. The canonical case is the WIKIBIO dataset, where the source is a Wikipedia infobox and the target is the article's first sentence. Dhingra and colleagues found sixty-two percent of those first sentences carry information the infobox does not state.
[O] So the dataset is teaching the behaviour.
[G] Explicitly. Add unfiltered duplicates, where Lee and colleagues showed duplicated pretraining examples bias a model toward regurgitating memorized phrases, and add what the survey calls innate divergence, which is tasks like open-domain dialogue where diverging from the source is the point.
[S] And the training-side causes?
[G] Four of them. Imperfect encoder representations. Erroneous decoding, which has two halves, attending to the wrong span, which produces the classic mixing-up-two-similar-entities failure, and the decoding strategy itself, where Dziri and colleagues found top-k sampling positively correlated with increased hallucination. Exposure bias, the mismatch between teacher-forced training and autoregressive inference, which compounds as the output gets longer. And parametric knowledge bias, where Longpre and colleagues found models prioritize what they memorized in pretraining over what the input in front of them actually says.
[O] That last one is the whole retrieval-augmented-generation failure mode, described in early 2022.
[G] It is, and the survey pairs it with something I think is underrated. It cites work showing a discrepancy between a model's surface generation and its internal knowledge. In its phrasing, models can realize they are generating something hallucinated. That observation is the seed of every reference-free detection method in section thirteen.
[O] Metrics. How do you measure this?
[G] Three tiers, statistical, model-based, and human. The statistical tier is n-gram overlap, and the interesting entry is PARENT, from Dhingra and colleagues, which uses both the source and the target as references rather than the target alone. That sounds like a detail and it is not. Traditional metrics like ROUGE and BLEU only look at the target, so they are structurally blind to whether you invented something the table never contained.
[S] How blind? Give me the number.
[G] The metrics section opens with it. Falke and colleagues analyzed three abstractive summarization systems and found twenty-five percent of the generated summaries had hallucinated content, while ROUGE, BLEU and METEOR were reporting those same systems as state of the art. Zhou and colleagues make the sharper version of the point, that a summary can carry a large amount of hallucinatory content and still score highly on ROUGE.
[O] Model-based metrics.
[G] Five families, and I would keep that count straight, because people usually say four. Information-extraction-based, which turns both texts into relation triples and compares them. Brad Pitt born in 1963 against Brad Pitt born in 1961, mismatch, hallucination. QA-based, which generates questions from the output, answers them against the source, and compares the answers. NLI-based, which uses entailment probability between source and generation as the score. Faithfulness classification, which builds purpose-made datasets because NLI labels and faithfulness are not the same thing. And LM-based, which trains a conditional and an unconditional language model and flags a token when the unconditional model's loss is lower.
[S] Every one of those has a dependency you can break.
[G] The paper says so for each. The extraction and QA families propagate errors from the auxiliary model. A bad extractor or a bad question generator manufactures a hallucination that was never in the text. For entailment, Falke and colleagues found off-the-shelf NLI models transfer poorly to summarization, and Goyal and Durrett point out that sentence-level entailment cannot localize which part of a generation is wrong.
[O] And the faithfulness-classification example is the one that made this click for me.
[G] It is a good one. Premise, Putin is president. Hypothesis, Putin is U.S. president. An NLI annotator could plausibly label that neutral, or even entailed. From a faithfulness standpoint it is unambiguously hallucinated, because U.S. is unsupported. That gap is the entire reason task-specific faithfulness datasets exist.
[S] I want to flag something about the numbers in this paper, because they travel as a set.
[O] Go on.
[S] Four percentages circulate from this survey. Sixty-two percent of WIKIBIO first sentences carrying unsupported information. Twenty-five percent of state-of-the-art summaries containing hallucinated content. Seventy-four percent of QMSum samples consisting of inconsistent facts. And forty-one down to twenty-one percent, the reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback reduction. Four different studies, four different tasks, four different denominators. There is no trend there. The survey never claims one, but quoting them together implies one.
[G] That is right, and it is worth being explicit that none of these are the survey authors' own measurements. Every one is a figure reported by a paper they cite. The seventy-four percent is Zhong and colleagues on QMSum, a query-based meeting summarization dataset, and it appears inside a future-directions subsection about dialogue summarization rather than as a headline finding. The forty-one to twenty-one is from the InstructGPT paper, for closed-domain tasks specifically.
[O] Which is a real result and also a narrow one. Closed domain is doing work in that sentence. Let us get to mitigation. What is on the menu?
[G] It mirrors the causes. Data-related methods are build a faithful dataset, clean an existing one automatically, or augment the input. The build-it-by-hand recipe is more concrete than you would expect. A three-stage revision. Phrase trimming to remove spans the source does not support, decontextualization to resolve coreferences and drop context-dependent phrases, and syntax modification so the purified sentence still reads smoothly.
[S] And the modeling side.
[G] Architecture changes at encoder, attention and decoder. Dual encoders for structured knowledge, sparse or inductive attention, uncertainty-aware and constrained decoders. Then three training frameworks, which is the paper's own Figure 1. Planning or sketching, where a separate content-planning step feeds the generator. Reinforcement learning with a hallucination-aware reward. And multi-task learning, adding an auxiliary task like word alignment or entailment alongside the target task. Then controllable generation, and post-processing, which is a generate-then-refine strategy.
[O] The reinforcement-learning entry is quietly prophetic. Reward functions built out of evaluation metrics.
[G] It is, and the survey names the mechanism plainly. Several of the reward functions for reducing hallucination are inspired by existing automatic evaluation metrics. Which is a warning as much as a recipe. If your metric has a blind spot, you have just trained directly against the blind spot.
[S] Before the argument, I want one pass through the task sections, because I suspect that is where the taxonomy actually gets tested. Which task fits it worst?
[G] Machine translation, comfortably. The survey says so itself. The categorization of hallucination in translation is unlike that in any other task and uses various terms that often overlap. To keep consistency it imports the intrinsic and extrinsic labels from one paper, Zhou and colleagues, rather than finding them already in place.
[O] What does the native translation vocabulary look like instead?
[G] Richer and stranger. Raunak and colleagues split hallucinations into those under perturbation, which you find by comparing a model's output on a perturbed and an unperturbed test set, and natural hallucinations, which come from noise in the training data. And the natural ones subdivide again, into detached, where the translation is simply semantically disconnected from the source, and oscillatory, where the output decouples and starts manifesting a repeating n-gram.
[S] Give me the oscillatory example, because I remember it being remarkable.
[G] The source is a German sentence about 1995 production volume reaching thirty million pizzas. The output is, roughly, the US, for example, has been in the past two decades, but has been in the same position as the US, and has been in the United States. It is not a mistranslation. The model has come off the rails entirely and is cycling.
[O] And none of that is intrinsic or extrinsic in any useful sense.
[G] That is the honest reading. You can force it into the split, and the survey does, but the informative structure is elsewhere. Other work in that section calls the same phenomenon over-translation, a repetitive appearance of words never in the source, against under-translation, skipping words that needed translating. There are also abrupt jumps to the end of a sequence and outputs that stay mostly in the source language.
[S] And here is the part that should worry anyone who evaluates translation systems.
[G] The survey states it directly. The appearance of hallucinations is found not to affect the BLEU score of the translated text. Your model can produce that pizza sentence and your leaderboard number barely moves.
[O] That is the same structural failure as the twenty-five percent summarization figure, in a different task.
[G] It is the through-line of the whole survey, and I think it is the real reason it landed. Six tasks, six local vocabularies, and in every one of them the standard metric was measuring something orthogonal to the failure people actually cared about.
[S] What about generative question answering, which you said had no standard definition?
[G] It is the least developed of the six, and the survey is candid about why. Hallucination there comes from two interlocking sources. The retriever pulls documents irrelevant to the answer, and the conditional generation model hallucinates on top of whatever it was given. Those are entangled, so attributing a given failure to one or the other is genuinely hard. That section is also where TruthfulQA first appears, eight hundred and seventeen questions across thirty-eight categories, with the finding that scaling the model up was less promising than fine-tuning it, because larger models learn the web's distribution better and produce more imitative falsehoods.
[O] Which is a 2022 result that reads like it was written about last year.
[S] Right. Now let us have the argument. My deflationary case is short. The two-way split is the reason this paper is famous, and by the paper's own account the field does not use it. There are four places where it says so.
[O] Name them.
[S] Section six point one, most existing hallucination metrics measure the two types together as a unified metric. Section seven point two, on summarization specifically, existing metrics evaluate both together in one metric because it is difficult to distinguish them automatically. Section six point two, most mitigation works do not distinguish between intrinsic and extrinsic. And the kicker from that same subsection, extrinsic hallucination has been somewhat overlooked as it is more challenging to reduce. So the category everyone quotes is the one nobody measures separately, and comparatively few even try to fix.
[O] All true, and I think it is the wrong test. You are grading a taxonomy on whether it has already been implemented. That is not what a taxonomy is for. Here is my case. In 2022 you had six literatures, summarization, dialogue, question answering, data-to-text, translation, captioning, each with a local word for the same failure and no ability to read each other's metrics. This survey gave them one coordinate system. The proof it worked is that the vocabulary is now ambient. You can say extrinsic at a workshop and nobody asks what you mean.
[S] The proof it worked is also that people say extrinsic and mean six different things by it.
[G] I will score this one honestly, because you are both partly right and the division is fairly clean. The split works as a diagnostic vocabulary, and it works as a research agenda. It names, precisely, something the field was not measuring, and section six point one lays out the two steps you would need. Locate the hallucinated substring first, then classify it. That is genuinely useful. What it does not do, as of this paper, is work as a measurement category, because nobody had built the localizer.
[O] So the survey is a specification rather than a result.
[G] That is a fair way to put it. And I will give the skeptic one more point that he did not take. A taxonomy where one branch contains both a helpful true elaboration and an unverifiable safety-relevant claim is under-specified, and the paper knows it. It asks for finer-grained categories in future work, and never delivers them. That is not a small gap. It is the gap that decides whether extrinsic hallucination is a bug or a feature in any particular deployment.
[S] Thank you. That is the one I actually care about.
[O] And I will concede something in return. If your taxonomy's own authors have to tell you the field is not applying it, then the taxonomy is doing less work than the citation count suggests. Those citations are for the map, not for the method.
[S] Now the structural question, and I think it is the most interesting thing about this paper as an object. Section thirteen. Bolted on, or folded in?
[G] Bolted on, and the paper does not hide it. It sits as a separate section at the end, after all six task sections, with its own footnote dating it to January 2024. And it does something substantive rather than decorative. Its first subsection broadens the definition. For large language models, the reference expands to include not just the input source but the training data, which in the paper's own framing pushes hallucination in these models more toward the extrinsic type.
[O] That is a real definitional move, not a cosmetic update.
[G] It is the most consequential paragraph in the addition. And it immediately concedes a problem with itself. The move only works if you assume the training data is factual, and the paper says plainly that this assumption is not always valid, since training sets contain non-factual and contradictory information, and that hallucination should therefore be distinguished from factuality.
[S] What else is in the section?
[G] A metrics taxonomy split into reference-dependent and reference-free. Reference-dependent means hallucination benchmarks, human-annotated ones like TruthfulQA, PopQA, Head-to-Tail and Med-HALT, plus automatically generated ones built from Wikipedia and reliable news text. Reference-free means uncertainty-based, aggregating token probabilities, and consistency-based, sampling several completions and checking whether they agree with each other. The paper is clear that consistency-based is the more reliable and more common approach in practice, because most models are served behind an API and the logits are simply not available.
[O] And mitigation?
[G] Pretraining data curation, with Llama 2 upsampling its most factual sources and Falcon showing that rigorous filtering and deduplication help. Instruction-tuning data curation. Reward-model data. Retrieval-augmented generation. Then decoding methods, DoLa contrasting logits from later against earlier layers, Context-Aware Decoding contrasting with and without context, Inference-Time Intervention shifting activations along truth-correlated directions. Chain-of-Verification. Chain of Natural Language Inference. Post-processing tools like PURR and CRITIC. And model ensembling and debate.
[S] That is a catalog, not a synthesis.
[G] That is a fair characterization and I would not fight it. It reads as an annotated list of 2023 techniques. What it does not do is go back and revise the sections it supersedes.
[S] Which brings me to the vision-language section.
[G] Section twelve, and this is the sharpest structural criticism available against this paper. It reviews object hallucination in image captioning using the CHAIR metric from Rohrbach and colleagues, which asks what proportion of the object words in a caption actually appear in the ground-truth captions, with a per-instance variant and a per-caption variant, computed against the eighty-object list from the MS COCO segmentation challenge. It states that captioning is, in the survey's words, the only vision-language task that has corresponding previous research works. For the other tasks it shows three question-answering examples and says there is currently no automatic metric for those hallucination types at all.
[O] And that is a 2022 picture of the world.
[G] It is. And the large-language-model addition knows it. The first future-direction subsection there is titled hallucination in large multimodal models, and it is not a throwaway. It runs about two hundred and sixty words across eleven sentences, call it half a page, and it is the longest of the five future-direction subsections in that part. It says models like GPT four V generate rich and detailed responses to visual inputs and are therefore inherently riskier than earlier vision-language models built for single-sentence captioning and short-answer question answering. It walks through the levels of information an image carries, CLIP's demonstrated weakness on things like object orientation, quantity and viewpoint, the vision-language alignment gap, the cost of annotation, and visual reinforcement learning from human feedback.
[S] So in January 2024 the authors wrote a considered account of why section twelve's framing is out of date, and left section twelve alone.
[G] That is accurate, and I want to be precise about what it does and does not imply. It does not mean section twelve is wrong. It describes the pre-multimodal-LLM literature accurately. It means the survey now contains two pictures of multimodal hallucination that do not talk to each other, one in the task section and one in future work.
[O] Is that actually a failure though? A survey is a snapshot. You cannot ask a 2022 paper to keep being a 2024 paper.
[S] You can if it keeps being republished as one. This is version seven. The authors were in the document, editing. They chose to append rather than revise.
[G] And there is a defensible reason for that choice, which I will offer as my read rather than the paper's. Revising section twelve properly would have meant re-surveying the field. Appending a new section let them ship the new material quickly, while it was still current. The cost is exactly what the skeptic named. The map has a new sheet taped to its edge instead of a redrawn region.
[O] And the field's answer was the market's answer. A separate dedicated survey of multimodal hallucination now exists to fill exactly that space. Which brings me to my last big question, and I think it matters most for anyone using this vocabulary today. Has the word hallucination drifted from what this paper standardized?
[G] Substantially, and you can locate the drift precisely using the paper's own terminology subsection. This survey's definition is source-relative. Hallucination is output that is unfaithful or nonsensical with respect to a provided source. Everyday usage now is closer to, the model said something false. In this paper's vocabulary that is factuality, not faithfulness. Different axes, and the survey is explicit about separating them.
[S] So the field kept the word and swapped the definition underneath it.
[G] Partly, and here is the uncomfortable part. The survey participates in the swap. Extending the reference to include the training data means that once your source is effectively the whole pretraining corpus, unsupported by the source becomes very close to false, and the intrinsic branch nearly empties out for open-ended generation. The paper is accommodating the drift rather than resisting it.
[O] And in fairness, it had already documented that the terminology was unstable before it tried to stabilize anything. The human-evaluation subsection lists the terms different papers use for what is basically the same judgment. Faithfulness, factual consistency, fidelity, factualness, factuality, hallucination, fact contradicting.
[G] Which is the honest summary of this paper's limits. It documented an inconsistency more successfully than it resolved one.
[S] One practical warning before we close, from the evaluation side, and it is not in the paper. The large-language-model section catalogs hallucination benchmarks and never asks whether those benchmarks leak into later training runs. The design of that benchmark family makes it acute. The paper says the primary focus of these benchmarks is long-tail, low-frequency knowledge, selected by rarity of appearance in pretraining corpora, or by low website traffic, or by recency. Publish that benchmark, let it get scraped, and the very property that made an item a good probe, its rarity, is the property you have just destroyed.
[G] That is a fair extension of the paper rather than something in it. The survey does not discuss contamination of the benchmarks it lists.
[O] The constructive half is that the survey already points at the alternative. Reference-free detection, uncertainty and self-consistency, has no static answer key to leak in the first place.
[S] It has other problems, but it does not have that one.
[O] Takeaways. Mine is that this paper's contribution is coordination, and coordination is chronically undervalued. Six literatures, one coordinate system, in one place, at the moment the field needed it. That is worth the citations it got.
[S] Mine is to cite the categories if you like, but read section six before you do. A taxonomy whose own authors report that most metrics and most mitigation methods do not apply it is a proposal, not a settled ontology. Treat it as a research agenda that is still mostly open.
