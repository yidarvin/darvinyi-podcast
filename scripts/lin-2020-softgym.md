[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio. Today's paper is SoftGym: Benchmarking Deep Reinforcement Learning for Deformable Object Manipulation, by Xingyu Lin, Yufei Wang, Jake Olkin and David Held, out of Carnegie Mellon, published at the Conference on Robot Learning in 2020.
[S] Two hundred and ninety two citations as of today. For an environment suite, that is the number that matters — it means people actually built on it rather than just citing the framing.
[O] And joining us is Soren, who has spent real time inside these environments and the deformable manipulation literature around them. Soren, welcome.
[G] Thank you. And I would sharpen your opening slightly. The paper's claim is not simply that reinforcement learning fails here. It is that the failure can be decomposed, and the decomposition is the contribution. Whether the decomposition is measured well is the argument you two are about to have.
[S] Set the scene first. 2020. What did reinforcement learning have to test on?
[G] Almost everything, as long as it was rigid. The Arcade Learning Environment, the MuJoCo suite, the DeepMind Control Suite, Meta-World, RLBench, SURREAL. A genuinely mature benchmarking culture. And nothing at all for deformable objects — no shared cloth environment, no shared rope environment, no shared fluid environment.
[O] And there was deformable manipulation work happening.
[G] A great deal of it, and that is the sharper version of the gap. The authors point at a cluster of papers doing learning-based cloth and rope manipulation, and their complaint is not that the work is bad. It is that each one evaluates on a different task variant with a different simulator or a different robot setup, which makes it impossible to compare them directly. Every group builds its own cloth sim, its own reward, its own success criterion, and reports a number that means nothing outside that one paper.
[S] That is a coordination failure, not a science failure. Why had nobody just built the shared thing?
[G] Because deformable objects break an assumption that almost every rigid-body benchmark quietly depends on, which is that there exists a low-dimensional state you can directly observe. A robot arm's state is a handful of joint angles and velocities. A piece of cloth's state, if you want it in full, is the position of every particle on its surface. In this simulator that is hundreds to thousands of dimensions, depending on how many particles you use.
[O] And unlike joint angles, you cannot read it off the robot.
[G] You cannot read it off anything. So a benchmark in this domain has to confront two things at once that rigid-body benchmarks get to sidestep. A state with genuinely high intrinsic dimensionality, and partial observability, because a real system only ever sees images or other indirect sensing, never the particle positions themselves. The authors' framing is that these were implicit difficulties everyone worked around differently, and the point of SoftGym is to make them measurable.
[O] Take me into the build, Soren. What is actually under the hood?
[G] Nvidia's FleX, which is a particle and position based physics engine. Every object is a set of particles plus constraints between them. Cloth is a grid of particles where each one is sprung to its eight neighbours — that is the stretching constraint — with an additional bending constraint between particles two steps apart to resist folding, and self-collision constraints on top. Rope is a chain of particles with springs between neighbours. Fluid is particles with a constant density constraint enforcing incompressibility.
[S] And the engine works by prediction and projection.
[G] Exactly that. It integrates velocity forward to a predicted position, then projects that prediction onto the feasible set defined by all the constraints. That is what makes it fast enough to matter — on a single 2080Ti, the authors report all SoftGym tasks running about four times faster than real time, with rendering.
[O] Give me the number that makes the case for simulation at all.
[G] A million simulation steps takes about six hours of wall clock time, and the authors note it would correspond to at least thirty-five hours of a real robot collecting data. That is the argument for the whole enterprise in one sentence.
[S] So they wrap FleX in a Gym API and that is the contribution?
[G] The API is more of the contribution than it sounds. If any codebase that already speaks Gym can point at these environments without adapting to a bespoke simulator or a bespoke reward format, you have removed the exact friction that made the prior literature incomparable. But the substance is the task design. It comes in three tiers.
[O] Walk them.
[G] SoftGym-Medium is six tasks, and it carries all the benchmarking. TransportWater — move a cup of water along a line to a target without spilling. PourWater — pour a cup into a target cup, and the reward is the fraction of water that lands in it. StraightenRope — straighten a rope from a random twisted configuration. SpreadCloth — maximize the top-down covered area of a crumpled cloth. FoldCloth — fold a flattened cloth in half. And DropCloth, which is the interesting one.
[S] Why is DropCloth the interesting one?
[G] Because the pickers are forbidden to go below a height threshold. The agent starts holding two corners of the cloth in the air and has to lay it flat, and it cannot simply lower it to the ground. So a swing-and-drop motion is required — if you just release it, the cloth lands crumpled. That is a task where the dynamics themselves have to be exploited rather than avoided.
[O] And the harder tier?
[G] SoftGym-Hard adds four. PourWaterAmount, which demands a specific target volume rather than as much as possible. FoldCrumpledCloth, where you have to spread and fold simultaneously. DropFoldCloth. And RopeConfiguration, where the rope has to match an arbitrary target shape, including letters of the alphabet, scored by minimum bipartite matching distance between particle positions.
[S] And the third tier.
[G] SoftGym-Robot, which swaps the abstract action space for a simulated Sawyer or Franka arm under Cartesian end-effector control. And I want to be precise, because this gets misread — that tier is simulated. There is a separate small real-robot experiment later, but SoftGym-Robot itself is a simulated arm. The paper also says that tier will only be released after obtaining permission from Nvidia.
[S] Which means the one tier that tests the harder setting is the one you cannot get. Hold that. What is the abstract action space it replaces?
[G] Pickers. A picker is a sphere that moves freely in space, and when activated it attaches the nearest particle to itself. For each picker the agent outputs four values — three displacements and one activation scalar, where above point five means grip and below means release. The authors say plainly why: they want to decouple low-level grasping from high-level planning.
[O] And that applies to the cloth and rope tasks.
[G] Seven of the ten tasks. The three fluid tasks directly actuate the cup instead.
[S] Fine. Now the part I actually care about. They benchmark a ladder of methods, not a flat list.
[G] This is the design idea worth taking from the paper. Each method is given a different amount of privileged information, so that the gaps between them localize where the difficulty lives. Top of the ladder is the Dynamics Oracle — the cross-entropy method planning against the ground-truth particle positions, velocities, and the ground-truth dynamics model itself. No learning at all. Pure trajectory optimization under model-predictive control.
[O] That is not a competitor. That is a ceiling.
[G] It is explicitly a ceiling, and the authors say so. It has information no real robot will ever have. Then two state oracles, both trained with Soft Actor-Critic. The Full State Oracle gets the raw positions of every particle, padded to a fixed maximum dimension since different cloth sizes produce different particle counts. That tests whether a policy can learn directly on the true high-dimensional state with no dynamics model.
[S] And the reduced one.
[G] The Reduced State Oracle gets a hand-designed summary instead. For every cloth task, the positions of the four corners. For rope, ten evenly-spaced keypoints. For the water tasks, cup dimensions, target position, water height and volume. That tests a different question — whether a smart low-dimensional abstraction, assuming you could perceive it perfectly, would be enough.
[O] And then the methods a real system would actually be stuck with.
[G] Pixels only. CURL-SAC, which uses a contrastive loss over randomly cropped images. DrQ, which is data augmentation plus regularization on standard reinforcement learning. And PlaNet, which learns a latent dynamics model and plans in it. Plus, on SpreadCloth and FoldCloth only, a comparison to Wu and colleagues from that same year, a model-free pick-and-place method with a task-specific action space and a cloth segmentation heuristic.
[S] Protocol. Seeds, steps, splits.
[G] Everything trains for a million environment steps, except PlaNet at five hundred thousand because of its compute cost — training PlaNet for a million steps takes around a hundred and twenty hours on their hardware. Five random seeds, and what is plotted is the median with a shaded band at the twenty-fifth and seventy-fifth percentiles. A thousand task variations are pre-sampled per task, split eight hundred for training and two hundred held out for evaluation — different cloth sizes, cup sizes, water volumes, initial configurations.
[O] And the normalization, because every number we are about to say depends on it.
[G] Per task they compute a lower bound, which is the performance of a policy that does nothing, and an upper bound, which is the task's best achievable performance. Then normalized score is raw score minus lower bound, over upper bound minus lower bound. So one is the ceiling, zero is doing nothing, and a policy that is actively worse than doing nothing goes negative. That last property matters later.
[O] Results. What does the figure say?
[G] The clearest pattern is that the Dynamics Oracle sits at or near the top of most panels. The paper's own language is that it performs the best and is able to solve most of the tasks — and I want to flag that hedge, because it is a real one. Most, not every.
[S] Where does it break?
[G] DropCloth. The Dynamics Oracle's line there is at point seven seven two, and the image-based DrQ ends near point eight four. DropCloth is the only one of the six panels where anything clears the oracle line. On PourWater, DrQ's curve reaches up to the oracle's line around seven hundred and twenty thousand steps and again near nine hundred thousand, without clearly exceeding it.
[O] Wait. Stop there. The pixel-only method beats the method that was handed the ground-truth physics engine?
[G] On that one task, at the end of training, yes. And I would not oversell it — the oracle's ceiling holds on the other five, and the authors note that even the Dynamics Oracle is limited by the exploration strategy the cross-entropy method uses. But the popular summary of this paper is that image-based reinforcement learning is uniformly far below the state-based methods, and that summary is wrong.
[S] Say more, because that is the summary I carry.
[G] The paper's actual claim is task-specific. Image-based methods are far below optimal on StraightenRope, SpreadCloth and FoldCloth, and there the authors read the flat curves as genuine plateaus — they say that even with more training, performance would still not improve. But the narrower comparison, the one against the Reduced State Oracle, is smaller than that list. The paper says image-based methods are much worse than the reduced-state oracle in certain tasks such as FoldCloth or StraightenRope. SpreadCloth is not in that sentence.
[O] Why is SpreadCloth excluded?
[G] Because on SpreadCloth the Reduced State Oracle collapses too — down into roughly the same low band, under about point two, as the image-based cluster. And the authors explain why themselves. The reduced state for cloth is the four corners, and the positions of four corners of a crumpled cloth are not sufficient to reason about its configuration.
[S] So the reduced-state oracle is not an oracle. It is a bet on a hand-picked feature set.
[G] That is exactly right, and it is one of the more useful findings in the paper. It performs well on StraightenRope, TransportWater and FoldCloth, where the summary happens to capture the task, and poorly where it does not. Its success is a property of how well its designer chose the abstraction, not a general property of low-dimensional states.
[O] And the Full State Oracle? That one gets everything.
[G] Performs poorly on all tasks. Which is the result I find most instructive. It has the true state, complete, and it fails everywhere, because concatenating every particle position into one unstructured variable-length vector forces the network to learn the object's geometric structure implicitly. The authors say that adds significantly to the complexity of the learning problem, and suggest a graph neural network would be the better architecture.
[S] So more information made it worse than less information.
[G] Structured less beat unstructured more. That is the sentence.
[O] What about the prior method, Wu and colleagues?
[G] On SpreadCloth it sits above the other five learned SoftGym methods after roughly the first hundred thousand steps — before that it is inside the same point zero five to point one seven cluster as everyone else. Still well below the Dynamics Oracle. The authors attribute the gain to the segmentation map and a better action space for exploration rather than a fundamentally better learner, and they say outright there is still large room for improvement. It also does not carry over — it does not perform very well on FoldCloth.
[S] There are two smaller experiments. Are they load-bearing?
[G] One is genuinely illuminating. Since PlaNet learns its own latent dynamics model, they feed it five real frames plus a planned action sequence and visualize what it predicts for the next thirty frames against the truth. PlaNet fails to predict the spilled water in PourWater and fails to predict the cloth's shape in SpreadCloth.
[O] Which is the mechanism behind the whole headline.
[G] It is direct evidence for it. The thing a latent dynamics model would need to predict here — complex, high-dimensional contact dynamics — is precisely the thing it cannot represent from pixels. The second experiment is a reality-gap check. They built a matched real-world cloth setup, a real Sawyer arm with a Weiss gripper, and ran the same pick-and-place action sequences in sim and in reality.
[S] And?
[G] Two matched rollouts, reported qualitatively — the simulated cloth shows similar behaviour to the real one. And the authors immediately add that direct sim-to-real transfer of learned policies is still expected to present a challenge. That is the correct scoping. It is evidence the dynamics model is a reasonable proxy, not evidence a policy would transfer.
[S] Then let me make the deflationary case, because I have been holding it for twenty minutes. The single most striking thing about evaluating this paper as an evaluation is how little of it is numeric for the methods that matter. Across eighteen pages there is no table of final scores and no per-task leaderboard for any learned method. Every comparative claim — performs the best, far below, outperforms the rest — is a read of curve position in a six-panel figure.
[O] With five seeds.
[S] With five seeds and visibly overlapping percentile bands. Look at the image-based cluster on PourWater and DropCloth. There is real room there for two methods to be described as meaningfully different when they are not, and there is no statistical test anywhere to settle it. And if you are a future paper reusing these environments, you have to eyeball a figure to know what you are beating.

[S] Which is standard for a from-scratch benchmark paper.
[G] Standard, and disclosed — full hyperparameter tables are published, which is the right mitigation. But it does not remove the effect, and the paper's own appendix demonstrates that better than any critic could. Appendix C is where the only real scalars in the paper live.

[G] From point nine five to minus four point nine nine. Nearly six normalized points, on the paper's best-performing baseline, from changing one hyperparameter. [O] So it is sensitivity, not selection.
[G] It is sensitivity, not selection, and the distinction matters. The finding is that the strongest baseline in this benchmark is fragile to a hyperparameter the benchmark's own authors chose, which is a real caution for anyone comparing against it. It is not a finding that anyone gamed anything.

[G] There is one loose end, and I have not seen it noted elsewhere. The hyperparameter table in Appendix B lists FoldCloth's reported planning horizon as thirty. [G] They cannot. And FoldCloth is the only one of the six tasks where this happens. [S] Which brings me back to my one real complaint, arrived at from a different direction.
[O] Let me give you the last piece of it, since I have been the optimist and I should name the thing that actually worries me. The picker abstraction. Seven of ten tasks use a free-floating sphere that grips whatever is nearest. The authors are open that this is deliberate — decouple grasping from planning. But it means most of SoftGym's measured difficulty is about state representation and dynamics, and comparatively little of it is about the grasping and contact problem that dominates real deformable manipulation.
[G] And the tier that would test the grasping-inclusive setting is SoftGym-Robot, which is the one the paper says cannot be released pending Nvidia's permission.
[S] So the hard version is scoped out and the tier that would restore it is unavailable. That is not a scandal. It is just a boundary on what the benchmark measures, and people citing it as the deformable manipulation benchmark should know where that boundary is.
[O] Implications, then. What would I want a benchmark paper in 2020 to have done differently, that I would want one today to do?
[G] Three things, and they are cheap. Print the table — final performance, mean and standard deviation, per task and per method. Run more than five seeds when the percentile bands visibly overlap. And measure the reality gap quantitatively rather than showing two matched rollouts.
[S] I would add a fourth, and it is the one with teeth. Build the reward-from-observation problem into the benchmark rather than declaring it out of scope. Every result here is conditional on a reward signal a real robot does not have. That is not a footnote, it is the load-bearing assumption.
[O] And I will add the one that cuts my way. Keep the ladder. The staged oracle design is why we can say something as specific as structured less beat unstructured more, or that the reduced-state oracle fails exactly where its features stop describing the object. A flat leaderboard of five methods would have told us none of that.
[G] There is a smaller methodological asymmetry worth naming too. The learned methods are evaluated on the two hundred held-out variations. The cross-entropy method, since it trains no parameters, is instead evaluated on ten variations randomly sampled from that set.
[S] Ten against two hundred.
[G] Ten against two hundred, for the method that defines the ceiling every other method is normalized against. The authors state it plainly in the setup, so it is disclosed, not hidden. But it is another reason to treat that oracle line as an estimate rather than a constant.
