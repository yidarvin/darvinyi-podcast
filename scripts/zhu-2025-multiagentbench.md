[O] Perdita, welcome. Give us the one-sentence version.
[G] The one-sentence version is that by twenty twenty five everyone was building multi-agent systems and nobody was measuring the agent-to-agent part. Every benchmark in use scored the final artifact. This paper tries to score the process and the artifact separately, in six different worlds.
[S] Set up the gap properly, because I want the prior work named.
[G] The paper names its targets directly. AgentBench, VisualAgentBench, GAIA, ToolBench, and HumanEval. Its charge is that these focus on isolated reasoning and generation and overlook the dynamics intrinsic to multi-agent interactions. And that charge is basically fair, because every one of those is a single-agent instrument.
[O] But the multi-agent work existed. MetaGPT, AutoGen, CAMEL, AgentVerse, Generative Agents.
[G] It existed as systems, not as measurement. And where multi-agent evaluation existed it was single-domain. One paper measures agents in Minecraft. Another measures agents writing software. There was no shared instrument spanning several, and more importantly no instrument that separated whether the coordination process was any good from whether the output was any good.
[S] Those really can come apart.
[G] They can, and that is the paper's whole bet. A team can stumble into the right answer through terrible communication. A team can communicate fluently and fail. A single success rate conflates the two, and the authors want to score both axes on every task.
[O] Let's build the machine. What is MARBLE?
[G] MARBLE is the harness, the Multi-agent Coordination Backbone with LLM Engine. At the centre is a Coordination Engine that takes in a configuration — task information, agent persona data, and a domain database — and constructs an Agent Graph. That graph is a set of agents plus typed relationship edges. An edge is a triple, agent i, relation, agent j, where the relation is something like collaborates, supervises, or negotiates.
[S] Why does the typing matter?
[G] Because the paper enforces it. Communication and coordination occur exclusively between agents with explicitly defined relationships. So who is even allowed to talk to whom is an inspectable data structure, not an implicit prompting convention buried in a system message. That is a real design decision and it is what makes the topology experiments possible at all.
[O] And each agent has some internal state.
[G] Each agent has a Cognitive Module holding its persona, its relationships to other agents, and its reasoning strategy. Behind that is a shared memory split two ways — a per-agent long-term memory retrieved with RAG, and a short-term memory. A Communication Module handles the message passing, an Action Module executes via function calling against the environment and a tool box, and every run terminates at an Evaluator that emits a Task Score and a Coordination Score.
[S] Perdita, the interesting part to me is that two things I would normally call implementation details are promoted to experimental variables here.
[G] That is right, and it is the most defensible novelty in the paper. The first variable is the coordination protocol. Centralized options are Star, where a single planner assigns tasks to all actors and consolidates their feedback, and Tree, where a top-level planner delegates to sub-planners who manage actors below them. Decentralized options are Graph-Mesh, a fully interconnected network of actors that plan concurrently, and Chain, where actors hand decisions down a line sequentially.
[O] And the second variable is how the planner thinks.
[G] The second is the planning prompt strategy, applied specifically to the centralized planner role. Four options. Vanilla is a zero-shot instruction to produce a plan. Chain-of-Thought feeds the planner the target task, the agent profiles including roles and expertise and historical performance, and summaries of previous subtasks. Group Discussion has multiple agents share insights and constraints and jointly refine the plan. And Cognitive Self-Evolving Planning, modeled explicitly on Reflexion, has the planner write down an expected outcome for each task, store it in memory, then compare the actual result against that expectation on the next iteration.
[S] So it accumulates a record of where its own predictions were wrong.
[G] Evolving experience is the paper's phrase. It is a per-run memory of expectation versus outcome that feeds the next plan.
[O] Six scenarios. Walk us through them, because the range is the pitch.
[G] Four have mutual goals and two have conflicting goals. On the mutual side. Research, where agents with complementary author profiles drawn from one hundred curated machine learning papers in the ResearchTown dataset co-author a proposal in a fixed five-question format — what is the problem, why is it interesting, why is it hard, why has it not been solved, and what is your approach. Thirty-three easy tasks, thirty-four medium, thirty-three hard. The agents have real tools, including a related-paper search and a fetch-by-arXiv-ID call.
[S] That one is at least grounded in a real corpus. Keep going.
[G] Minecraft, where agents jointly construct a target structure and are scored on a block-level hit rate against one hundred target structures. Database, which drops agents into a PostgreSQL instance running in Docker, seeded with one of five anomalies — fetching large data, inserting large data, lock contention, a redundant index, or vacuum problems — and the agents query system views like pg locks and pg stat statements to diagnose it. They get to predict two root causes when there is only one.
[O] Why two guesses?
[G] Because the authors are honest that the anomalies contaminate each other. If you fetch a large amount of data, you had to insert it first, so insert-large-data legitimately shows up as a symptom. And their anomaly queries hit the same tables from one hundred threads at once, so lock contention appears whether or not it is the cause. Allowing two predictions is an acknowledgment that the ground truth is fuzzy.
[S] I actually respect that. What is the fourth?
[G] Coding, adapted from the SRDD dataset, where agents with specialties — debugging, execution, test writing, review — build software across five topics: Education, Work, Life, Game, and Creation. Then the conflicting-goal pair. Bargaining is a multi-party negotiation over an item sampled from an Amazon products dataset. One of the running examples is a chair banner listed at fourteen dollars ninety-nine with a four point eight star rating.
[O] And Werewolf.
[G] Werewolf, the social deduction game, with villagers and werewolves plus special roles like Seer, Witch, and Guard. And this is where the setup gets structurally interesting. The authors prepared one hundred saved game archives, and run them two ways. Partial-day simulation loads a saved night state and simulates exactly one day-night cycle with explicit villager tasks — protect the seer for one point, exile a werewolf for two, rescue a villager for two, poison a werewolf for two. Full-game simulation starts from the end of the first night and runs the match to conclusion, with tasks demoted to suggestions.
[S] Hold that thought about the archives, because I want to come back to who generated them.
[O] Metrics. There are more than I expected.
[G] There are two families and you have to keep them apart or every claim in this paper becomes unreadable. Task Completion has two members. The KPI is milestone-based — a language model continuously monitors the interaction log for whether task-specific milestones have been reached, and credits every agent that contributed. Each agent's individual KPI is the number of milestones it contributed to divided by the total number of milestones, and the overall KPI averages that across agents.
[S] So KPI is a per-agent participation rate, not a success rate.
[G] Correct, and that distinction matters later. Separately there is a task-based score on final output quality — an LLM rubric for Research and Bargaining, and rule-based metrics for Minecraft, Werewolf, Database, and Coding. For Research the rubric has three axes: innovation, safety, and feasibility, each on a five-point scale.
[O] And the coordination family.
[G] Coordination is a Communication Score and a Planning Score. Both are LLM-judged on a five-point scale, taking as input the task description, the agent profiles, and the aggregated communication or planning transcript. Communication is zero if no communication occurs at all. The two are averaged into the overall Coordination Score. In the main table everything is multiplied by twenty so it reads on a zero to one hundred scale.
[S] So every coordination number in this paper is a language model rating a transcript.
[G] Every single one. Hold that.
