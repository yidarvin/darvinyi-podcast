[O] This is Litsearch Audio. Today's paper is MedExpQA: Multilingual Benchmarking of Large Language Models for Medical Question Answering, by Inigo Alonso, Maite Oronoz and Rodrigo Agerri at the HiTZ Center, University of the Basque Country, published in Artificial Intelligence in Medicine in 2024.
[S] It is on the docket for two reasons. It is the first medical exam QA benchmark to ship reference explanations written by doctors, and it is the first one that is not English-only.
[O] Tobias, welcome. You have spent real time with this one.
[G] Thank you. One framing note before we start, because it governs everything we say afterwards. Every number in this paper is exam multiple-choice accuracy — literally the first character the model emits after the phrase "The correct answer is". It is not a clinical evaluation, it does not measure safety, and the authors do not claim that it does.
[S] Start with the gap, then. What was broken about medical QA evaluation as of 2024?
[G] Two things, and the paper is unusually direct about both. First, the standard benchmarks — MedQA built on the USMLE, PubMedQA, MedMCQA, the MultiMedQA suite behind Med-PaLM, and the newer retrieval-focused MIRAGE — all score a single letter. None of them ship a reference explanation, so you can check whether a model was right and never why. Second, all of them are English. The authors describe multilingual evaluation of medical language models as, in their words, a totally neglected topic.
[O] And the reason the second one bites is that the headline claims — models earning passing marks on licensing exams — are all English claims.
[G] Exactly that. The passing-marks results from Singhal and colleagues, and the GPT-4 results from Nori and colleagues, are English multiple-choice results. Nothing in that literature tells you how the same model behaves for a resident reading the same case in Spanish.
[S] Fine, but I want to hold the first gap up to the light. A benchmark with gold explanations could mean two very different products. Either you score the model's generated explanation against the doctor's, or you feed the doctor's explanation in as context. Those are not the same thing.
[G] They are not, and this paper builds the second one. That distinction is the single most important thing to keep straight about MedExpQA, and we should come back to it, because the paper's own concluding remarks list scoring generated explanations as future work.
[O] Walk us through the data, then.
[G] The substrate is the Antidote CasiMedicos dataset, from Agerri and colleagues and Goenaga and colleagues. Every year the Spanish Ministry of Health releases the previous year's resident medical exams — Medico Interno Residente, the MIR, which the paper describes as similar to licensing exams like the USMLE. A volunteer project of practising and resident doctors then publishes written commentary on those exams, explaining why the correct option is correct and, usually, why each wrong option is wrong.
[S] Volunteer commentary. Say more about the quality control, because that is the load-bearing asset in this entire design.
[G] The paper is honest about it in the discussion. The explanations do not follow a repetitive formal structure, and they are not subject to a second auditor's review the way a textbook chapter would be. There is an example where a psychiatry resident spends the explanation musing about how easy the question was rather than explaining the medicine. And around five percent of the full explanations contain no explicit explanation of the correct answer at all. The authors deliberately left those in.
[O] How big is it?
[G] 622 documents per language, parallel across Spanish — the original — plus English, French and Italian. That is 2,488 documents in total, split 434 train, 63 validation, 125 test, per language.
[S] 125 test items. That is small.
[G] It is small, and it is worth holding onto, because every accuracy number we are about to quote comes out of 125. One item is point eight of a percentage point, which is why every per-cell score in the results tables is a multiple of point eight.
[O] What does one input actually look like?
[G] The clinical case, including the question, averages 137 tokens across the four languages. The multiple-choice options average 79.6 tokens, which is unusually long for a multiple-choice benchmark — the options range from a bare drug name to a full sentence of clinical claim. So call it roughly 217 tokens of base input. The doctor's full explanation averages 170.25 tokens on top of that.
[S] And the three gold conditions.
[G] Three ways of injecting the doctor's text. E is the full explanation covering both correct and incorrect options, which the paper treats as its upper bound. EI is only the explanation of the incorrect options, testing whether knowing what is wrong is enough to infer what is right. And H is the full explanation with the explicit answer-pointing phrases masked out — a phrase like "this option is false" replaced by a hidden token.
[S] Who does the masking?
[G] GPT-4, prompted, not fine-tuned. Specifically the 1106 preview model, with a rule set and in-context examples, applied across all 2,488 documents. They validated it against human annotation on 80 documents, 20 per language, and report an average F1 of point eight five with a standard deviation of point zero two.
[S] So the condition that is supposed to isolate reasoning from pattern-matching is itself produced by a model the authors measure at point eight five, not at one.
[G] That is a fair statement of it, and the paper does not hide the figure. Whether point eight five is good enough depends on whether the residual errors are random or systematic, and they do not test that.
[O] Now the automatic side.
[G] Two conditions. None, which is just the case and the options with no external knowledge. And MedRAG, from Xiong and colleagues — BM25 and MedCPT run as retrievers and fused with reciprocal rank fusion, over MedCorp, which is PubMed plus medical textbooks plus StatPearls plus Wikipedia. They run it at two depths, RAG-7 and RAG-32.
[O] Why seven?
[G] It is derived rather than arbitrary. They estimate that 85 percent of their prompts fit under 400 tokens, which leaves 1,648 tokens of a 2,048-token window for grounding, and that works out to about seven documents on average. RAG-32 is there because MedRAG's own paper got its best results at 32.
[S] And if 32 documents do not fit?
[G] They truncate the retrieved documents rather than the prompt. Which produces a confound I would like on the record. PMC-LLaMA has a 2,048-token context window, LLaMA-2 has 4,096, Mistral and BioMistral have 8,192. If you look at the tables, PMC-LLaMA's RAG-7 and RAG-32 rows are identical in all four languages, zero-shot and fine-tuned alike. For that model, RAG-32 is not a 32-document condition at all. It is the same seven documents under a different label.
[O] That is a good catch. What about the models themselves?
[G] Two pairs, chosen deliberately. LLaMA-2 13B against PMC-LLaMA 13B, which is LLaMA continued-pretrained on PubMed papers and medical textbooks. And Mistral 7B against BioMistral 7B, the DARE-merged variant, continued-pretrained on PubMed Central. So each medical-specialised model is matched against the general model it was built from.
[S] Training regime?
[G] Every model is run both zero-shot and after LoRA fine-tuning on CasiMedicos — rank 8, alpha 16, ten epochs, best checkpoint kept, one recipe for every model, on an A100. And crucially the fine-tuning is monolingual: each language is trained on its own data only. The authors say plainly that a per-model hyperparameter search exceeds the capacity of their lab.
[O] Give me the top-line table.
[G] Averaged over all four models and all four languages, zero-shot. Full gold explanation, E: 82.2 percent. Hidden-reference gold, H: 72.4. Incorrect-options-only gold, EI: 47.4. Then the automatic conditions. RAG-7: 37.9. RAG-32: 37.6. No grounding at all: 35.6.
[S] So the best automatic condition in this paper is RAG-7, not RAG-32.
[G] Correct, and it is worth being precise about, because RAG-32 is the one plotted in the paper's own summary figure. RAG-7 at 37.9 percent is the best automatic number. RAG-32 at 37.6 is simply the deeper retrieval setting, and on average it is slightly worse.
[O] And after fine-tuning?
[G] Everything rises and the ordering breaks. E goes to 91.7, H to 83.9, EI to 72.1. But no-grounding goes to 57.7 while RAG-32 goes to 54.4. Fine-tuned, retrieval is on average worse than nothing.
[O] I want to steelman that before we bury it. Fine-tuning on 434 in-language examples teaches the model the format and the register of these exams. Retrieved English PubMed snippets sitting inside a Spanish prompt are, at that point, mostly distraction.
[G] That is close to the authors' own reading. They put it as fine-tuning making RAG redundant. And they raise the language question directly in the discussion — MedCorp is English, three of the four prompt languages are not, and they explicitly leave open whether a translate-test approach would do better.
[S] Now the significance testing, because this is where I expect the paper to be strong and I want to verify it.
[G] They ran a McNemar test on every RAG-7 and RAG-32 score against its own None baseline, across both tables — 64 comparisons in total. Five are significant at alpha point zero five, and all five are zero-shot. Four of them are LLaMA-2: Spanish and Italian, at both k equals seven and k equals 32. The fifth is Mistral, English, at k equals 32 — 48.8 percent with no grounding up to 58.4 with RAG-32.
[S] Five out of 64. And I notice there is no significant Spanish Mistral cell, which is exactly the sort of thing people misremember.
[G] There is not. The significant set is those four LLaMA-2 cells plus that one Mistral English cell, and nothing else.
[S] Then let me push on the four LLaMA-2 cells, because I think they are weaker than they look. What is LLaMA-2's zero-shot Italian accuracy with no grounding?
[G] 12.8 percent.
[S] 12.8. On multiple-choice questions with four or five options. That is below random guessing.
[G] It is. And its Spanish no-grounding score is 18.4, which is also at or below chance. So the two baselines that retrieval significantly improves are the two baselines that were broken to begin with. Retrieval takes LLaMA-2's Italian from 12.8 to 32.8 at k equals 32. That is a real and significant improvement, over a floor that should not have been where it was.
[O] I do not think that fully deflates it. Something about the Italian prompt is failing for LLaMA-2 zero-shot, and adding context fixes it. That is a genuine effect.
[G] It is a genuine effect. My read is that it is more likely a repair to output behaviour than an injection of medical knowledge, but the paper does not run the analysis that would separate those two, so I will not push further than that.
[S] I will take it as a real finding with an unclear mechanism.
[O] Let's do the medical-specialisation question, Tobias, because I think this gets flattened in every summary I have read of this paper.
[G] It does, and it has to be split by model pair and by training regime, because the four cells genuinely disagree. Zero-shot, PMC-LLaMA 13B beats LLaMA-2 13B in all four languages on the per-model average: English 57.9 against 52.1, Spanish 46.8 against 43.2, Italian 46.9 against 42.7, French 46.1 against 45.2. The medical model wins that pair cleanly, four for four.
[O] So medical continued-pretraining does help.
[G] Zero-shot, for that pair, yes. Now fine-tune both and it reverses completely. PMC-LLaMA goes to 69.3, 58.7, 62.7 and 61.6 across English, Spanish, Italian and French. LLaMA-2 goes to 71.1, 67.6, 65.6 and 66.5. The general model now wins all four.
[S] And the other pair?
[G] The other pair points the other way throughout. BioMistral trails plain Mistral in all four languages zero-shot — 60.5 against 65.6 in English, 53.1 against 57.3 in Spanish, 50.7 against 56.3 in Italian, 53.7 against 57.2 in French. And after fine-tuning it trails in three of the four: Mistral takes English, Spanish and French, and BioMistral edges Italian, 70.5 against 70.1.
[O] So the honest statement is not that medical models lose.
[G] The honest statement is that it depends on which pair and whether you fine-tune. The paper's own claim — that Mistral is the best model overall, outscoring the medical-specific BioMistral — is true and well supported. Generalising that into medical pretraining not working is not supported by these tables, because the LLaMA pair says the opposite zero-shot.
[S] There is one more wrinkle in the fine-tuned table. The paper says BioMistral seems to get the best overall scores, but attributes that to its E column and says Mistral remains the best model everywhere else. Does the table bear that out?
[G] It does, and if anything the paper is being generous to BioMistral. BioMistral owns the best gold-explanation cells — 94.4 English, tied with Mistral, then 93.6 Spanish, 92.0 Italian, 93.6 French, the strongest E row in the table. But look at the per-language model averages across all six conditions. Mistral is ahead in English, Spanish and French — 79.5, 72.3 and 74.8, against BioMistral's 73.9, 70.3 and 72.5 — and BioMistral only edges Italian, 70.5 against 70.1. So that best-overall impression really is one column doing the work.
[O] Now the multilingual gap. How big is it, precisely?
[G] The abstract's framing is best results around 75 percent accuracy for English, with roughly a ten-point drop for other languages. The 75 is a specific cell: fine-tuned Mistral, English, RAG-32 — 75.2 percent, up from 68.0 with no grounding. That is the single highest non-gold score anywhere in the paper.
[S] And the same model, same condition, in the other three languages?
[G] All three fall relative to no grounding. Fine-tuned Mistral in Spanish goes from 63.2 with no grounding down to 55.2 with RAG-32. Italian, 56.8 down to 52.0. French, 66.4 down to 60.0. So the one configuration that produces the paper's headline English number actively hurts in the other three languages.
[O] That is the strongest version of the multilingual argument and I want to say it plainly, because it is the reason this benchmark exists. If MedExpQA had been English-only, the story from that row is: retrieval plus fine-tuning gets you to 75 percent, up seven points. Four languages in, the story is: it gets English to 75 and costs you roughly five to eight points everywhere else.
[S] I will give you that one entirely. That is the clearest demonstration in the paper that English-only evaluation reports a rosier picture, and it is not a small effect.
[G] And it holds more broadly than that one row. The authors state that performance for French, Italian and Spanish is substantially lower for every model in every evaluation setting.
[O] What about the reasoning question — the E versus H comparison?
[G] That is the cleverest measurement in the paper. E gives the model the doctor's explanation with the answer-pointing phrases intact. H gives the same explanation with those phrases masked. The drop between them is how much of the score was textual shortcut rather than medical content. On average, 82.2 down to 72.4, so about ten points of the gold-explanation performance is pattern-matching on the giveaway phrasing.
[S] Per language?
[G] The paper's claim is that the E-to-H gap is larger outside English, and for two of the four models it is stark. Zero-shot PMC-LLaMA drops 4.8 points in English, from 83.2 to 78.4, against 14.4 points in Spanish, from 77.6 to 63.2. Zero-shot Mistral drops 7.2 in English, 89.6 to 82.4, against 12.8 in Spanish, 88.0 to 75.2.
[S] Two of four. What do the other two do?
[G] LLaMA-2 drops 12.8 points in English and 12.8 in Spanish — identical — and its largest drop is Italian at 14.4. So the pattern is real on the average but not uniform per model, and I would not state it as a law.
[O] Noted, and that is the kind of thing that should be said out loud rather than averaged away.
[S] Let me make the deflationary case, and then you can both hit it. This paper runs four open models, two at 7B and two at 13B, on a 125-item test set, with one fine-tuning recipe nobody searched, and no frontier model is evaluated on the benchmark at all. GPT-4 appears only as a tool for building the masking condition. Every conclusion about how hard MedExpQA is, is a conclusion about small open models from early 2024.
[G] That is accurate and the authors would largely agree with the constraint. What I would add is that it limits the claims asymmetrically. The negative result about retrieval is fairly robust — RAG barely moves four different models across four languages and two training regimes. The claim that medical QA remains far from solved is much more fragile, because it is a claim about a model population that has been superseded several times over since.
[O] Here is my optimist case, and it is not about the models at all. The contribution is the measuring instrument. Nobody had a controllable knowledge dial for medical QA before this. You can now ask: with perfect knowledge, 82.2 percent; with perfect knowledge minus the giveaway phrasing, 72.4; with only the negative evidence, 47.4; with the best automatic retrieval anyone has built, 37.9. That decomposition tells you exactly where the engineering problem sits, and it is not in the model's reasoning.
[S] I think that is the paper's best argument and I will grant it. My objection is narrower. CasiMedicos is public commentary on public Spanish government exams that has been on the open web for years. The paper does not test contamination at all — no n-gram check, no decontamination pass, not even a paragraph.
[G] You are both partly right, and there is evidence in the tables. If gross memorisation of the exams were driving scores, Spanish should be the leak vector, since Spanish is the original language. Spanish is consistently among the lowest scores. That is evidence against gross contamination of the questions. But it says nothing about the explanations. If the explanation text itself was in pretraining, the E and H ceilings are inflated relative to what a genuinely novel explanation would produce, and the entire upper-bound framing shifts. Nobody has checked.
[O] Score that one to the skeptic?
[G] Score it as an open question the paper should have addressed and did not. It is also the most fixable item on the list.
[S] My second objection is about the name. It is called MedExpQA, explanation-based question answering, and the abstract says the lack of gold explanations means you cannot evaluate the reasoning behind a model's predictions. But this benchmark never scores an explanation. It manipulates which explanation goes in, and grades one character coming out.
[G] That is correct, and the paper concedes it in the concluding remarks — future work may involve evaluating models not only on accuracy but on the quality of the explanations they generate. So explanation-quality scoring does not exist in this release. What exists is explanation-as-input.
[O] I will concede the name overreaches. I will not concede the design is wrong. Explanation-as-input is what gives you the upper bound, and the upper bound is what makes the retrieval number interpretable at all. Without E at 82.2, RAG at 37.9 is just a low number with nothing to compare it against.
[S] That is fair. My complaint is about the claim, not the instrument.
[G] There is one more item I would put on the skeptic's side of the ledger, from the paper's own text. The results section says five of the 64 comparisons are significant, and Table 8 carries five asterisks. The discussion section then says the gains are not statistically significant in 61 out of 64 cases, which would imply three. Those two sentences do not reconcile. The tables are the authority and they show five.
[S] Minor, but the kind of thing that makes me read the rest more slowly.
[O] And on the other side of that ledger, credit where it is due. They ran the significance test at all, found that almost nothing clears it, and printed that in the results rather than burying it in an appendix. Most benchmark papers reporting a two-point retrieval gain would have called it an improvement and moved on.
[G] I agree. Reporting five out of 64 in your own results section is not the behaviour of a group trying to sell retrieval.
[O] So what changes if this holds?
[G] For evaluation practice, one clean lesson. If you are going to claim a retrieval system supplies medical knowledge, you now have a reference point for what actual knowledge is worth on the same items. The gap between the best automatic condition and the doctor-written condition is about 44 points zero-shot — 37.9 against 82.2. Any RAG paper reporting a few points of gain can be asked where it sits on that scale.
[S] And the multilingual lesson, which I think is the bigger one. If your benchmark is English-only, you are not measuring a smaller version of the multilingual result. In that fine-tuned Mistral RAG-32 row you are measuring the one language where the intervention helps, while it is hurting in the other three.
[O] My version of that: the fix is probably not more retrieval. The paper's depth sweep from zero up to 32 snippets flattens out around eight for most of these models, and PMC-LLaMA's retrieval scores sit below its own no-grounding scores in all four languages. More snippets is not the lever.
[G] Agreed, and the corpus-language question the authors raise is likely the more interesting lever. MedCorp is English, three of the four prompt languages are not, and nobody has run the translate-test comparison on this benchmark yet.

[G] That is right, and the authors frame the target as decision support for medical experts — a resident preparing for the licensing exam — not as clinical validation.
