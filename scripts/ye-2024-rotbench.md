[O] Take a weather tool. Leave its behaviour, its parameters, and its written description completely untouched. Change only its name, from Get underscore Weather to the three letters A B C. On this benchmark GPT-4's tool-selection accuracy falls from eighty to fifty-eight point one. Three human graders doing the identical items lose two point eight six points. That gap is the whole paper.
[S] Or the gap is an artifact of the ruler. Every stage is exact string match against one hand-labelled gold path, no partial credit, and each stage is gated on the one before it. Pick a functionally reasonable alternative tool and you score zero on all three. Some of that twenty-two-point drop is the scoring being brittle, not the model.
[O] The humans were graded with the same ruler and barely moved.
[S] The humans also weren't asked to emit a machine-parseable function call, and there are three of them, averaged. I want to know how much of that gap survives contact with the tables.
[G] Both of those are answerable from the tables, and the short version is that the gap is real, the ruler objection has real bite in one specific place, and the paper's own summary of which model is robust is wrong on its own numbers.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio, where an optimist, a skeptic, and a visiting scholar take one paper apart. Today it's RoTBench, a multi-level benchmark for evaluating the robustness of large language models in tool learning.
[S] Junjie Ye, Yilong Wu, Songyang Gao, Caishuang Huang and colleagues, all out of Fudan University. Main conference at EMNLP twenty twenty-four, first posted to arXiv in January of that year.
[O] Our guest is Imogen, who has been through this one carefully, tables and appendix included. Imogen, why is this on the docket?
[G] Because it isolates a variable that every tool-use benchmark before it was holding fixed without meaning to. In a curated benchmark environment, a tool's name is a faithful one-line summary of what the tool does. That is a property of the benchmark, not a property of the world, and nobody had checked how much of measured tool-use ability was riding on it.
[S] Set the prior work up properly. Who had done what?
[G] Two clusters. One evaluates the outcome of tool use — did the model pick a relevant tool, did the final answer serve the user. The other evaluates the process, stage by stage, and that includes T-Eval and the authors' own earlier system, ToolEyes. Both clusters run in stable, well-documented environments. Separately there's a robustness literature — PromptBench is the named exemplar — which perturbs the natural-language prompt. Nobody had perturbed the structured identifiers that a tool call actually resolves against.
[O] And why should the name matter at all when the description is right there?
[G] That is the authors' bet, and they state it explicitly in the limitations section. Their claim is that a model's comprehension of a tool leans primarily on the tool and parameter names rather than on a nuanced reading of the documentation. The whole benchmark is a test of that claim, and the results are the evidence for it.
[S] Which is a slightly circular way to design a benchmark, but a legitimate one if the effect size is large. Tell me how the data was built, because provenance matters here.
[G] It's layered, and the layering is worth stating precisely. The queries and the tool inventory come from ToolEyes — seven real-world application scenarios, fifteen randomly selected queries per scenario, a hundred and five queries total, against forty-one tool categories, ninety-five subcategories, and five hundred and sixty-eight individual tools. But ToolEyes did not ship standardised invocation paths. So the gold answer for every one of those hundred and five queries — the correct tool, the correct parameter set, the correct filled values — was manually labelled by the RoTBench authors.
[O] So the queries are inherited, the answer key is new.
[G] Exactly, and both halves of that matter. Inherited queries mean the Clean-level environment carries whatever contamination risk ToolEyes already carries. A hand-built answer key means the gold path is one team's judgment about what the right call is, applied once, with no adjudication procedure reported.
[S] Now the five environments. And I want them in the right order, because I have seen people get this wrong.
[G] Ascending noise: Clean, Slight, Medium, Heavy, Union. Union is the heaviest, not Heavy. Clean is the ToolEyes runtime as-is, a hundred and five cases. Slight applies insertion, omission, or character substitution to up to a third of a name's characters — half the tools get it in one batch, half the parameters in another, so two hundred and ten cases. Medium replaces names with reversals or short nonsense strings, again two hundred and ten. Heavy is the disruptive one.
[O] Disruptive how?
[G] Two mechanisms. Exchange shuffles all the tool names amongst each other. That's important — the name isn't degraded to noise, it's swapped for another tool's perfectly meaningful name, so the name is now actively misleading rather than merely uninformative. The second mechanism, addendum, bolts an extra mandatory parameter with a random name onto half the tools, which the model has to notice and fill from the description alone. Two hundred and ten cases. Then Union draws one tool-name method and one parameter method from those three levels and applies both simultaneously, a hundred and five cases.
[S] And the scoring.
[G] Three stages, cascading and gated. Tool selection is a plain indicator: is the chosen tool the required tool. Parameter identification multiplies that indicator by whether the identified parameter set exactly equals the required set. Content filling multiplies that by a product of indicators over every value filled. Higher is better on all three, and by construction content filling is less than or equal to parameter identification, which is less than or equal to tool selection, in every cell.
[O] So one wrong tool zeroes the entire row.
[G] It does. And I'd defend the design choice — in a real call, the wrong tool fails the interaction no matter how elegant the arguments are. But it does mean the three columns are not three independent measurements. They are one measurement read at three depths, and the deeper reads inherit every error from the shallower ones.
[S] Model roster?
[G] Four open-source and tool-tuned: two Tool Llama checkpoints, both fine-tuned from Llama two seven B on function-calling data, and two NexusRaven checkpoints, both fine-tuned from Code Llama thirteen B to emit nested function calls. Then GPT-3.5-turbo and GPT-4. Then a human reference, three university students on the same items, averaged. The GPT and Tool Llama families get a ReAct-style prompt, the NexusRaven family gets its own documented format. And critically, the main table grades only the model's first turn of tool use.
[O] Give me the headline.
[G] GPT-4's tool selection goes from eighty at Clean to fifty-eight point one at Union. The human average on the same items goes from eighty-eight point five seven to eighty-five point seven one. Two point eight six points. Averaged across all six models, tool selection drops about twenty point three two points Clean to Union, and I recomputed that one from the table cells — the six-model mean is seventy point one six at Clean and forty-nine point eight four at Union. It checks out exactly.
[S] Fine. That is a big effect and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. What's the part where the paper oversells?
[G] The part where GPT-4 becomes shorthand for the frontier and the frontier becomes shorthand for what's robust. Look at Heavy. NexusRaven thirteen B version two beats GPT-4 on all three stages there — sixty-two point three eight against sixty on tool selection, thirty-seven point six two against thirty-two point eight six on parameter identification, thirty against twenty-five point two four on content filling. A thirteen-billion-parameter open model, sweeping GPT-4 in an environment where the tool names have been deliberately swapped.
[O] So the open model is the robust one and the framing is backwards.
[G] No, and I want to stop that inference before it sets. Union is the heaviest environment, and under Union GPT-4 wins all three: fifty-eight point one against fifty-six point one nine, thirty-nine point zero five against thirty-seven point one four, thirty point four eight against twenty-two point eight six. Count the wins across all five environments and NexusRaven version two takes parameter identification in three of five, content filling in two of five, and tool selection in exactly one. A majority in exactly one stage.
[S] That is a more interesting result than either framing.
[G] It is, and it's the one I'd carry out of the paper. The ranking is not stable across noise levels. Which model is most robust depends on which noise you injected. That is a much sharper indictment of clean-environment benchmarking than "GPT-4 drops twenty-two points", because it says the leaderboard order you measured on a clean environment does not transfer.
[O] Is there anything non-monotonic in GPT-4's own curve?
[G] Yes, and it's stark. GPT-4's Medium score is above its own Clean score on all three stages — eighty-four point two nine against eighty on tool selection, fifty-three point eight one against fifty-two point three eight on parameters, forty-six point one nine against forty on content filling. The nominally more corrupted environment is the one where GPT-4 does its best work.
[S] Then the environments are not ordered by difficulty, they're ordered by the authors' intuition about how much they mangled the string. Those are different things and the paper treats them as the same thing.
[G] Agreed, and the paper does have a mechanism for the adjacent anomaly, which I think is the single best finding in the piece. The GPT family underperforms at Slight relative to Medium because of noise correction. Hand GPT-4 a tool whose name has been lightly corrupted — a stray inserted character, a capital in the wrong place — and it silently repairs the name to the clean form it expects, then calls that. The repaired name doesn't exist in the environment it was actually given. The model's competence at reading through typos is what breaks it.
[O] Which is a genuinely delightful failure mode.
[G] And they quantify it, carefully. In the Slight environment, thirty-three point seven two percent of GPT-3.5-turbo's tool-selection errors and thirty-three point eight five percent of its parameter-identification errors are attributable to that correction behaviour. For GPT-4, twenty-nine point one seven and twenty-two point eight three. Now — that is a share of each model's own errors, not a performance comparison. GPT-3.5's higher share does not make it worse overall, and I've seen that misread.
[S] What about tool names versus parameter names? Is the damage evenly split?
[G] Not remotely. Their figure three decomposes the average drop from Clean by whether the noise hit tool names or parameter names, and tool-name noise dominates at every stage. Parameter noise barely moves tool selection at all, which makes sense, and exerts less influence downstream too. There's a lovely corollary they flag: models are more robust under Union than under Heavy tool-name-only noise. Adding parameter corruption on top made things easier, because Union draws its tool-name method at random from all three levels, so on average it is milder than Heavy's guaranteed shuffle.
[O] They also run a significance test.
[G] Welch's ANOVA on the content-filling scores across the five environments, per model. Five of the six models come out significant at p below zero point zero five. The human row is F of zero point zero four, p of one — the cleanest statement of human stability in the paper.
[S] And the sixth model?
[G] NexusRaven version one, p of zero point five five five. Not significant. And the paper leaves that sitting next to the human result without comment, which I think is a real miss, because they mean opposite things. Look at that model's actual content-filling scores: across all five environments they range from zero point nine five to three point three three. It is pinned at the floor everywhere. Statistical stability at zero is not robustness. The humans are stable at around seventy percent; that model is stable at nothing.
[O] Point to the skeptic.
[S] I'll take it. What about giving the models more context — does interaction history help?
[G] They test that on GPT-4 only, content filling only. Give it the first two turns of a worked interaction and grade the third, and its average content-filling score rises by twenty-two point nine one points across the environments. But the standard deviation across environments rises from eight point one four to twelve point five six.
[O] So more capable, and no more consistent.
[G] Precisely. In-context examples buy accuracy, not robustness — and if anything they buy accuracy unevenly, widening the spread between the environment it handles well and the one it handles badly. That's a genuinely useful negative result for anyone who assumed few-shot examples were a robustness lever.
[S] All right, the second half. They propose a fix.
[G] RoTTuning, four phases. Phase one, self-instruct query expansion from the hundred and five seed queries out to four thousand and seventy-seven, deduplicated by dropping anything with Rouge L above zero point five five against its examples. Phase two, GPT-4's own function-calling feature generates tool-use trajectories for those queries, capped at nine turns, and each turn becomes a training point — twelve thousand two hundred and forty-seven of them. Phase three, environment augmentation: rewrite Clean trajectories into noisy variants, three thousand each for Slight, Medium and Heavy, fifteen hundred for Union, twenty-two thousand seven hundred and forty-seven trajectories in total. Phase four, LoRA fine-tune Llama two seven B base, context extended to eight thousand and ninety-six tokens by positional interpolation, five epochs. That gives you RoTLlama.
[O] And it's more stable?
[G] On its own numbers, clearly. RoTLlama's range across the five environments is twelve point three eight points on tool selection, sixteen point one nine on parameter identification, fourteen point seven six on content filling. All three are tighter than GPT-4's spread on the same stages. Its absolute ceiling is lower — seventy-six point one nine at Clean tool selection against GPT-4's eighty — but the curve is flatter.
[S] It was trained on the exact noise generators the benchmark tests. That's not robustness, that's studying the answer key's handwriting.
[G] That is the right objection and the paper half-answers it. Appendix E runs the version where you augment with data from only one environment, and it still outperforms the no-augmentation model in the other environments. So it is not memorising a single noise family. But your structural point stands: the noise families are shared between training and test by construction, and no held-out noise type — a type of corruption the training augmentation never saw — is ever evaluated. Half a point to you.
[O] What do the ablations say is actually doing the work?
[G] Environment augmentation, not the LoRA choice. Swapping LoRA for full-parameter fine-tuning costs a little mean performance and leaves the spread across environments largely unchanged. Removing augmentation drops the mean and visibly widens the spread. Removing both costs sixteen point one zero points. So the diversity of training environments is the lever, and the parameter-efficiency choice is close to incidental to robustness.
[S] Any result in there that undercuts their own model?
[G] One, and to their credit they name it themselves. They count tool hallucinations — calls to tools that don't exist — across all environments. RoTLlama has three, which is far below GPT-4's twenty-three and GPT-3.5's fifty, and enormously below the Tool Llama checkpoints at fifty-three and sixty-five. But NexusRaven version two has zero. The lowest hallucination count in the paper belongs to an off-the-shelf open model, not to theirs, and they say so and draw the reasonable inference — that a code-model base may be the right foundation for tool learning.
[O] Let me make the optimist case, because I think it's stronger than the skeptic's opening implied. This benchmark identifies a failure mode that is not hypothetical. Real tool catalogs are full of auto-generated identifiers, legacy endpoint names, inconsistent casing, and abbreviations that mean nothing outside one team. If a name change of no semantic consequence costs you twenty-two points of tool selection, then a large fraction of what we've been calling tool-use capability is name-reading capability wearing a costume. And the fix is cheap — LoRA on augmented trajectories, on a seven-billion-parameter base, buys you a materially flatter curve. That is a good day's work.
[S] My deflationary case has three parts. First, the ruler. One gold path per query, hand-labelled by the authors, exact match, no partial credit, hard gating. When a model picks a different but functionally valid tool it is scored identically to a model that picked nonsense. Second, the roster is a museum exhibit. This was submitted in January twenty twenty-four and revised in September twenty twenty-four, and it contains no model with native structured function calling from the generation that followed. Third, they cannot distinguish noise sensitivity from exact-match rigidity, and they don't try.
[G] Let me score those. Your ruler objection, I'd say confirmed and the paper hands you the receipt. Their appendix C reports that one Tool Llama checkpoint scores twenty at Clean and forty at Union in the application-manipulation scenario — it does better under heavy noise, because that scenario happened to leave it fewer viable tools to choose between. That is the scoring artifact showing through the results, in their own text.
[O] That's a small scenario, though.
[G] It is, and the aggregate effect is almost certainly modest. But it exists, it is uncontrolled, and no partial-credit or alternate-path check is reported anywhere. Skeptic wins that one. On the dated roster — confirmed, but I'd add that it cuts both ways. The benchmark's value is the methodology, and the methodology transfers to any model you like. The specific numbers are a snapshot; the environment construction is the artifact. Anyone can rerun it.
[S] And the third?
[G] Partly conceded by the authors themselves in the limitations, which is the honest place for it. They state two limits plainly: robustness is only assessed within a single tool-use round, and noise is only injected into names, never into the tool descriptions. So the criticism that they never test description-level noise is answered before you make it — they scoped it deliberately, and they say why.
[O] Is there anything that doesn't reconcile?
[G] There is one, and I'd hand it straight to the skeptic. In the RoTTuning results, they compare RoTLlama's extreme performance difference to GPT-4's, and they quote GPT-4 at twenty-one point nine for tool selection, twenty point nine five for parameter identification, and twenty point nine five for content filling. The last two are exactly maximum minus minimum across all five environments — Medium minus Heavy in both cases. I checked. The first one is not.
[S] What is it, then?
[G] GPT-4's true tool-selection range is Medium at eighty-four point two nine minus Union at fifty-eight point one, which is twenty-six point one nine. Twenty-one point nine is Clean minus Union — eighty minus fifty-eight point one. It's the abstract's headline subtraction, dropped into a sentence where the other two numbers were computed a different way. Two methods in one comparison.
[O] Does it change the conclusion?
[G] Not at all. RoTLlama at twelve point three eight is flatter than GPT-4 under either computation, and the honest number actually makes GPT-4 look worse, so nobody gained anything by it. It's sloppiness, not spin. But if you are reading this paper to reuse its numbers, use the table, not the prose sentence — and this is exactly why the reason to read a benchmark paper's tables yourself has not gone away.
[S] I'll take the finding and the general principle both.
[O] What changes downstream if this holds?
[G] Two things I'd watch. First, tool-catalog hygiene becomes an evaluation-relevant variable. If you are standing up a large tool surface for an agent — and a lot of people now are — the names in that catalog are load-bearing in a way the documentation is not, and that is a testable property of your deployment, not a research abstraction. Second, and this is my read going slightly beyond the paper: any leaderboard built on a curated tool environment is reporting a number that includes an unknown amount of name-reading. The ranking instability here is the evidence — the order of models changed with the noise level, within one benchmark, on identical queries.
[S] Which is the part that generalises past tool use, honestly. Every benchmark has some channel where the artifact is more legible than the world is.
[O] Takeaways. Mine: the effect is real and the intervention is cheap. Rename the tools and frontier tool selection falls twenty-two points where humans fall under three, and a LoRA fine-tune on noise-augmented trajectories flattens that curve materially without touching model scale.
[S] Mine: the number I trust least is the one I'd have quoted first. One hand-labelled gold path, exact match, hard gating, and an environment ordering that isn't a difficulty ordering — GPT-4 scores better under Medium noise than under no noise on all three stages. Read this as a strong existence proof, not a measurement.
[G] The paper's own: robustness to noise in tool and parameter names is a distinct capability from tool-use accuracy, it is not conferred by scale or by in-context examples, and it can be trained for directly by diversifying the environments a model sees. And the finding neither of them led with — the model ranking changes with the noise level, so which model is most robust is not a fixed fact about the models.
