[S] There is an experiment at the end of this paper that I think undoes a good deal of the front of it. [O] And they tell you exactly why, in the same paragraph. A ten-slot memory can only hold about two of these tasks at once. So the hard dataset collapses into the easy dataset, and the model starts winning the easy dataset. That is not a hole in the benchmark. That is the benchmark's design being confirmed from the other direction.
[S] Or it is a benchmark where the difficulty lives in how many storylines you cram into a memory buffer, and the theory-of-mind framing is decoration.
[G] Both of those are readings the paper can bear, and I would put one fact down before either of you builds on it. The headline claim here is not that any single model is bad. It is that four separate memory-augmented architectures, chosen because they were near state-of-the-art on bAbI, all fail to clear the success bar the authors set. And that phrase, near state-of-the-art, is the paper's own. It is doing more work than people remember.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio. Today's paper is Evaluating Theory of Mind in Question Answering, by Aida Nematzadeh, Kaylee Burns, Erin Grant, Alison Gopnik and Thomas Griffiths, published at E M N L P in twenty eighteen. It is the dataset most people now call Tom bAbI.
[S] And our guest is Blythe, who has spent more time with this one than is strictly healthy. Blythe, why is a synthetic story generator from twenty eighteen on the docket in a benchmarks show?
[G] Because it is one of the cleanest examples I know of a benchmark that imports its validity argument from outside machine learning. The control questions in here are not the authors' invention. They are lifted straight out of a developmental psychology paradigm, where they exist for exactly one purpose, which is to stop you crediting a child with understanding when the child guessed. Whether that transplant survives contact with a neural network is the interesting question, and the paper is more honest about the answer than its abstract lets on.
[O] Start with the gap. What was wrong with bAbI?
[G] Two things, and the paper states both in its first column. One, the tasks had gone stale. It cites results where existing models fail at only one or two of the twenty tasks. Two, and this is the sharper complaint, it cites Lee and colleagues for the finding that all but two of the twenty reasoning tasks only require transitive inference. So the suite was both saturated and narrower than its twenty-task presentation suggested.
[S] And nothing in those twenty asks a model what somebody else believes.
[G] Nothing. That is the actual gap. There had been one prior attempt at it, by Grant, Nematzadeh and Griffiths in twenty seventeen, which built a bAbI-style version of the Sally-Anne task and asked whether an end-to-end memory network could say where Sally will look for the milk when Sally's belief and reality have come apart. This paper is the same group, extending their own earlier work, and they name two limits of it.
[O] Which are?
[G] First, it only tested first-order beliefs. Sally's belief about the milk. People also reason about second-order beliefs, Anne's belief about Sally's belief about the milk, and that is a later and harder developmental milestone. Second, and this is the one I would underline, success in that earlier dataset was defined as correctly answering one question. The paper's own sentence is that this does not guarantee a model has an understanding of the state of the world.
[S] So the fix is to ask more questions.
[G] The fix is to ask the questions psychologists ask. In the Sally-Anne experiment, from Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith in nineteen eighty-five, a child watches Sally put a marble in her basket, watches Sally leave, watches Anne move the marble to her box, and then gets three questions. Where will Sally look for her marble. Where is the marble really. And where was the marble in the beginning. Only the first is about belief. The other two exist to confirm the child actually has the world modelled, so a correct belief answer is not luck.
[O] And the second-order half comes from the ice-cream van study.
[G] Perner and Wimmer, also nineteen eighty-five. Mary and John both see an ice-cream van in the park. Mary goes home. The van driver tells John he is moving to the church, then runs into Mary on the way and tells her too. John does not know Mary was told. So you ask, where does John think Mary goes to get ice cream. Perner and Wimmer found six- and seven-year-olds handle that, which is why it reads as the harder capacity.
[S] Blythe, I want to slow down on the translation step, because that is where these things usually go wrong. How does a four-panel puppet experiment become question answering?
[G] Through grammars. Every bAbI task can be written as a grammar, and the data is just derivations of that grammar. The authors follow their own earlier work and write grammars over entities, people and objects, and predicates, actions like enter, exit and move. So a story is five or six sentences. Anne entered the kitchen. Sally entered the kitchen. The milk is in the fridge. Sally exited the kitchen. Anne moved the milk to the pantry. That last one is the false-belief story.
[O] Three task types, right?
[G] Three. True belief, where the agent is present for the change, so her belief matches reality. False belief, where she is absent for the change, so her first-order belief is wrong. And second-order false belief, where Sally does observe the change, so Sally is right, but Anne does not know that Sally observed it, so Anne holds a false belief about Sally's belief. Those three, crossed with the four question types, memory, reality, first-order and second-order, give twelve task-question combinations. Table three in the paper is just the answer key for those twelve.
[S] Is that answer key as clean as it sounds? Because with two candidate locations and twelve cells, I am immediately worried about how much structure a model can memorise.
[G] You are right to worry, and the paper worries about it in the same place. For the true-belief and the false-belief tasks, the first-order and second-order questions happen to have the same correct answer. They only come apart on the second-order false-belief task, because that is the one where Anne has a false belief about Sally's belief. So the answer key has a genuine near-degeneracy in it, and the authors go on to show that a model exploits exactly that. We will get there.
[O] There is one more design decision I want on the record, because it is the kind of thing that gets skipped. The containers are transparent.
[G] Yes. The authors assume that whenever an agent enters a location, they become aware of the object's true location. It is stated as a deliberate choice to keep the tasks structurally similar, and specifically to prevent a model from learning to emit a fixed answer whenever a sentence like Sally looks inside the pantry shows up. It is a small thing that closes a real shortcut.
[S] And the two dataset variants.
[G] Tom easy, where a story contains exactly one task. And Tom, where tasks are randomly grouped into sets of five to form a story, which matches bAbI's story length. Training is ten thousand examples for each of the twelve combinations. And here is the detail that everyone should hold on to. In the Tom test set, each story contains four tasks, but there is only one question, at the end.
[O] One question per story. Not four.
[G] One. So the model has to work out which of four interleaved storylines the question is even about, before it does any belief reasoning. The paper's stated reason is positional. Questions that come earlier in a story have fewer distracting sentences after them, so they are easier, and putting every question at the end separates the difficulty of a question's position from the inherent difficulty of the question.
[S] That is a defensible reason and it also throws away the entire point of the control questions. The reason a psychologist asks where is the marble really is that they ask it of the same child about the same story, right after the belief question. If you ask your reality question of one generated story and your belief question of a different generated story, you have not cross-checked anything. You have two independent accuracy numbers.
[G] That is the strongest methodological criticism of this dataset and I do not think there is a rebuttal in the paper. You get per-combination accuracies, and the paper reasons about them jointly, but no single evaluated story ever has all four questions attached to it. Le and colleagues rebuilt this the following year, in the dataset called Tomi, and asking all the question types of the same story with a joint scoring rule is precisely one of the things they changed.
[O] Let us do the models, then the numbers.
[G] Four. The end-to-end memory network, Sukhbaatar and colleagues, twenty fifteen, which embeds sentences into memory slots and attends over them across multiple hops. The Multiple Observer model, Grant and colleagues, twenty seventeen, which is that memory network plus a separate memory module per agent, so an agent's memory only receives sentences for events that agent was present for, with an extra attention over the modules. The recurrent entity network, Henaff and colleagues, twenty seventeen, which is recurrent and keeps entity-level state in gated units rather than whole sentences. And the relation network, Santoro and colleagues, twenty seventeen, which scores every pair of sentences against the question.
[S] So the Multiple Observer model is the one with the theory of mind built into its wiring.
[G] It is the one with the architectural prior that matches the task, yes. It gets a separate memory per agent, which is a very direct encoding of the idea that different people saw different things.

The Multiple Observer model has no bAbI number here at all.
[S] So the setup is not four models that had solved bAbI now falling over on beliefs.
