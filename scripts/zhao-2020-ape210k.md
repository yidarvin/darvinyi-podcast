[S] Here is a sentence you almost never hear about a benchmark paper. The authors withdrew it, and the reason they gave was that their own experiments are, quoting the notice, meaningless and irreproducible.
[O] And here is the sentence you almost never hear right after that one. Years later people still cite the dataset by name, and you can download a copy of it this afternoon.
[S] Both of those are true at the same time. That is the whole reason this one is on the docket.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio. Today's paper is Ape two ten K, a large-scale and template-rich dataset of math word problems, by Wei Zhao, Mingyue Shang, Yang Liu, Liang Wang, and Jingming Liu, from the Yuanfudao AI Lab in Beijing with a co-author at Northwestern. Posted to arXiv on the twenty-fourth of September, twenty twenty.
[S] And withdrawn by its own authors fifteen days later. Our guest is Aubrey, who has spent real time with this one. Aubrey, welcome. Start with the withdrawal, because I suspect everything else depends on getting that right.
[G] It does, and it is easy to get wrong in two opposite directions, so let me be precise. On the ninth of October, twenty twenty, the arXiv listing was replaced by a withdrawal notice. The notice reads, verbatim, this paper has been withdrawn by Liang Wang. The comment field reads, verbatim, we decide to withdraw this paper, since the proposed Ape two ten K dataset is not going public, the experiments in this paper is meaningless and irreproducible without access to the dataset. That is the authors' own wording, grammar and all.
[O] So the trigger is a release that did not happen. The first version of the abstract says flatly that they make the dataset publicly available, on a GitHub repository under the Yuanfudao organization.
[G] Right, and that link is dead today. It returns a not-found. So the abstract's central promise was retracted by the people who made it.
[S] Then the honest thing to say on this show is that the dataset does not exist for us and we should all move on.
[G] No, and this is the first direction people get wrong. The dataset is obtainable. A full third-party copy has been public on GitHub since the thirteenth of October, twenty twenty. That is four days after the withdrawal. It has been redistributed on HuggingFace since twenty twenty-three. Anyone who wants Ape two ten K today can have it in about a minute.
[O] So the withdrawal removed the paper's own copy, and the community routed around it almost immediately.
[G] Correct. But now the second direction people get wrong, which is the more tempting one. It does not follow that the circulating copy is authentic, or sanctioned, or the thing the paper's numbers were measured on. There is no established chain of custody back to the original team.
[S] What can you actually check about it?
[G] Structure. The mirrored validation and test files each hold exactly five thousand problems, which matches the split the paper states. That is real structural agreement and it is worth something. It is not record-for-record identity. You cannot confirm that these are the same five thousand problems the reported accuracies were computed on.
[O] So the fair statement is, the data is available and unverifiable at the same time.
[G] That is exactly the fair statement. And two consequences follow, and people usually pick one and forget the other. First, contamination risk is live, not foreclosed. A widely mirrored Chinese-language math corpus sitting on GitHub and HuggingFace since twenty twenty is precisely the kind of thing a web-scale pretraining crawl ingests. Second, and separately, the provenance is unverifiable. Those are two different problems. The withdrawal does not protect you from leakage, and the mirror does not restore reproducibility.
[S] Fine. I will hold both. Let us talk about what the paper actually built, on the assumption it built what it says.
[G] The task is math word problem solving. You get a natural language description of an elementary school math problem, and the system has to produce an equation whose evaluation gives the right number. The paper's framing is that by twenty twenty the public datasets were compromised in two ways at once. Too small, and too narrow.
[O] Give me the landscape.
[G] Alg five fourteen has five hundred fourteen problems and twenty-eight equation templates. Dolphin eighteen seventy-eight has one thousand eight hundred seventy-eight problems. Dolphin eighteen K reaches eighteen thousand four hundred sixty problems with five thousand eight hundred seventy-one templates. And the workhorse of the field, Math twenty-three K, has twenty-three thousand one hundred sixty problems with only two thousand one hundred eighty-seven templates.
[S] Define template, because that word is doing a lot of work here.
[G] A template is the equation with the problem's own numbers replaced by symbols. If the problem gives you two numbers, they become d one and d two. Any number in the equation that does not appear in the problem text stays as itself. So the template is the shape of the reasoning, stripped of the particular quantities.
[O] Which means a dataset with few templates can be gamed by memorizing shapes.
[G] That is the authors' argument, and they put it sharply. Two thousand templates over twenty-three thousand problems raises, in their words, a serious question on the generalization capability of models trained on Math twenty-three K.
[S] And their fix is more data. Which is the least interesting possible fix.
[G] Their fix is more template diversity, and I would push back on you there, because the numbers are not marginal. Ape two ten K has two hundred ten thousand four hundred eighty-eight problems and fifty-six thousand five hundred thirty-two unique templates. Against Math twenty-three K that is about nine times the problems and roughly twenty-six to one on templates. The abstract rounds that to twenty-five times more.
[O] Nine times the size but twenty-six times the shapes. The diversity grows faster than the scale.
[G] That is the claim the paper is really making, yes. And there is a second axis they push on, which I think is the more interesting one. External constants.
[S] Numbers you need that the problem never gives you.
[G] Exactly. The paper's own worked example is a classic. Chickens and rabbits in the same cage, two hundred seventy-four feet in total, twenty-three more chickens than rabbits, how many chickens. The answer is sixty-one. But the equation needs a two and a four that appear nowhere in the text, because you have to know a chicken has two legs and a rabbit has four.
[O] That is commonsense knowledge smuggled into arithmetic.
[G] Or domain knowledge. Another of their examples needs you to know that an isosceles triangle has two equal sides and three edges in total. Another needs the volume relationship between a cone and a cylinder of equal base and height. None of those numbers are in the problem statement.
[S] And the headline percentage on that is what, exactly? Because I have seen this number quoted loosely.
[G] Good, because this is a place to be careful. Thirty-seven point seven percent for Ape two ten K, against zero point eight percent for Math twenty-three K. But that is a template-level figure. It is the share of unique equation templates that contain an external constant. It is not the share of individual problems.
[S] So what is the instance-level number?
[G] The paper reports it in a separate statistics table, and it is much lower. Seven point seven two percent of Ape two ten K's equations contain an external constant, against zero point one nine percent for Math twenty-three K. Same direction, same story, roughly forty to one either way, but you should never quote the thirty-seven point seven as if it described problems.
[O] That is a useful correction. The template-level number is the one everyone repeats.
[S] All right, how was this thing built? Because at two hundred thousand problems nobody hand-wrote equations.
[G] They did not. The raw pool is about nine hundred thousand Chinese elementary school math problems from Yuanfudao's own online production system, written by humans, with rationales and answers attributed to professional math teachers. Then they filter hard. Out go problems with no extractable answer, problems whose answer is not a number, problems with no numbers in the description, problems containing images or tables, and problems that ask multiple questions.
[O] How much does that last filter cost?
[G] About one-third of the raw pool is multi-question. The paper is explicit that it is scoping to single-unknown-variable problems and leaving the rest for future work. What survives is the two hundred ten thousand four hundred eighty-eight.
[S] And the equations themselves come from where?
[G] This is the part I would circle. The source data does not contain equations. It contains human-written rationales in natural language, the kind a teacher writes out. So the authors run a rule-based extraction pipeline. They find every equation in the rationale text that is consistent with the gold numeric answer, and then heuristics pick at most one. If there is exactly one candidate, keep it. If there are several, keep the longest, on the theory that short ones are intermediate calculations. If none can be extracted, substitute the trivial equation, x equals the answer.
[S] Stop there. That is a labeling function, not an annotation process.
[G] It is a labeling function, and the paper reports no inter-annotator agreement, no manual audit on a sample, and no estimate of how often the longest-candidate rule picks a wrong-but-numerically-consistent equation.
[O] To be fair to them, they do validate every extracted equation against the gold answer.
[G] They do, and that is a real constraint. It rules out equations that produce the wrong number. It does not rule out an equation that produces the right number by the wrong route, which is the failure mode you care about when your labels are supposed to be reasoning traces.
[S] And the fallback worries me more. If extraction fails, the label becomes x equals the answer. That is a template with no reasoning in it at all.
[G] Agreed, and the paper does not report how often that fallback fires. That is a genuine gap.
[O] There is preprocessing too, right?
[G] Yes, and some of it is unglamorous but load-bearing. They normalize unit strings, so kilogram and the various capitalizations of k g all collapse to one form. They convert full-width characters to half-width. They convert number words to numerals. And they apply equation normalization, which reorders the symbols in the equation so their order tracks the order the numbers appear in the problem text. That last one is a technique they credit to Wang and colleagues from the Math twenty-three K line of work, and it matters because the same equation has many equivalent written forms.
[O] Let us get to the model, because they also propose a baseline.
[G] They do, and its purpose is explicitly to show the dataset has headroom. It is a copy-augmented, feature-enriched sequence-to-sequence model, encoder-decoder with soft attention in the Bahdanau style. The embedding layer is the feature-enriched part. Five handcrafted per-position features, combined by a learned weighted sum. The character at that position. The part-of-speech tag of the word. The word itself. A flag for whether the character is a number. And a rank index, where you sort all the numbers in the problem in descending order and assign each an index.
[S] That last feature is essentially telling the model which number is biggest.
[G] Yes, and in an ablation footnote they say the part-of-speech feature is the most important of the four non-character features. The character and word embeddings are pretrained with word two vec on the problem description text itself. The encoder is a four-layer bidirectional LSTM, with a twist. The feature embeddings go through the first two layers, then the sum of those hidden states and the original input embeddings goes into the last two layers.
[O] A residual connection in the middle of the encoder.
[G] Effectively, yes. The decoder is a two-layer bidirectional LSTM with input feeding, so the attentional vector from one step is summed into the next step's input. And then the piece the paper is proudest of, the copying mechanism.
[S] Borrowed from summarization.
[G] Borrowed from pointer-generator summarization, and applied to math word problem solving, per the authors, for the first time. At each decoding step the output distribution is a mix. Alpha times a distribution over tokens copied straight out of the problem description, plus one minus alpha times the ordinary target vocabulary distribution, with alpha learned per step.
[O] And the reason that helps is that the equation is mostly made of things that appeared in the text.
[G] Precisely. The template symbols point at numbers in the problem. Copying gives the decoder a direct route to those tokens instead of forcing everything through a generation vocabulary.
[S] Fine. Numbers. And I want the two datasets kept strictly apart, because I have seen these merged.
[G] They must be kept apart, and the paper itself separates them into two tables with different protocols. Table one of the two is Math twenty-three K. Eight models, and most of them evaluated with five-fold cross-validation because the original test split is too small. Deep Neural Solver at sixty-four point seven. StackDecoder at sixty-five point eight. Group Attention at sixty-six point nine. TreeDecoder at sixty-nine point five. The previous best, the G T S model, at seventy-four point three. The paper's own approach at seventy-seven point five.
[O] So plus three point two over the previous best.
[G] Plus three point two, and that is the figure the abstract advertises. One caveat the paper flags itself. Two of the eight rows, the sequence-to-sequence expression-tree model and T-R-N-N, are marked with an asterisk because they were evaluated on the published test split rather than five-fold cross-validation. So those two are not protocol-matched to the rest.
[S] Now the other table.
[G] The other table is Ape two ten K's own held-out test set, and it has exactly three rows. Not eight. StackDecoder at fifty-two point two eight. The G T S model at fifty-six point five six. The paper's approach at seventy point two.
[S] Three rows. So only two prior systems were re-run on the new dataset.
[G] Only two. Both open-source, both chosen for that reason. It is important not to say that every prior model was evaluated on Ape two ten K, because six of the eight were not.
[O] Still, seventy point two against fifty-six point five six is not a small gap.
[G] It is not, and the StackDecoder number has a mechanical explanation the paper gives. StackDecoder structurally cannot represent external constants other than one and pi. On a dataset where thirty-seven point seven percent of templates need such a constant, that is a ceiling, not a tuning problem.
[S] Which cuts against the comparison being informative. You picked a dataset feature your baseline handles and one competitor cannot represent at all.
[G] That is a fair hit, and I would score it to you. The G T S comparison is the more meaningful one, and there the gap is about fourteen points.
[O] What about the cross-dataset story? The paper wants to say everything gets harder on Ape two ten K.
[G] It does say that, and the raw numbers point that way. StackDecoder goes from sixty-five point eight on Math twenty-three K to fifty-two point two eight on Ape two ten K. The G T S model goes from seventy-four point three to fifty-six point five six.
[S] Across different evaluation protocols.
[G] Across different evaluation protocols, yes. Five-fold cross-validation on one side, a single held-out test split on the other. So it is suggestive rather than clean. My read is that a drop of eighteen points is too large to be a protocol artifact, but the paper does not do the work to rule that out, and it should have.
[O] What does the ablation say about where the seventy point two comes from?
[G] Four components, removed one at a time, all on the Ape two ten K test set. Remove the copying mechanism and you lose two point nine two points. Remove the four handcrafted input features, keeping only the character feature, and you lose two point nine. Remove the pretrained embeddings and you lose one point two two. Remove equation normalization and you lose one point zero. Strip all four and you land at sixty-six point two two, down three point nine eight.
[S] Two point nine two and two point nine. Those are the same number.
[G] They are two-hundredths of a point apart, which is why I am giving you both and not ranking them. It would be a mistake to tell a listener that copying is the dominant component when the margin over the feature set is that thin, on a single test split, with no seeds reported.
[O] And the stripped-everything number is interesting on its own. Sixty-six point two two is better than the sum of the individual drops would suggest.
[G] It is, which tells you the components overlap in what they contribute. The paper does not comment on that, but it is visible in the table.
[S] Give me the error analysis, because that is usually where a dataset paper is most honest.
[G] It is, and this one is. They carve out three hard slices of the test set. Problems whose template is at least fifteen tokens long, which is about sixteen percent of the test set, and there accuracy falls to forty-seven point three seven. Problems whose template occurs exactly once in the whole dataset, which is forty-four point zero six percent of the test set, and there accuracy is forty-nine point one one. And problems whose equation contains an external constant, thirty-seven point six six percent of the test set, at fifty-two point eight nine.
[O] Forty-four percent of the test set has a template that appears once. That is a striking number.
[G] It is the number I would put on the poster, honestly. Nearly half the test set is a template the model has essentially never seen, and it gets under half of those right. That is the generalization pressure the whole dataset was built to create, and it works.
[S] And the flip side is the common templates.
[G] The flip side is in another table. The top ten most frequent templates in Math twenty-three K and Ape two ten K overlap on nine of ten. Same shapes, different frequency distribution. And on those Ape two ten K top-ten templates the model's accuracy is near or above ninety percent for nine of them.
[O] So the head of the distribution is solved and the tail is not.
[G] That is a clean summary of the paper's own findings.
[S] Let me make the deflationary case, and Aubrey can tell me where I overreach. The labels are produced by a heuristic with no reported audit. The headline model is the authors' own, on a dataset the authors built, against two competitors they selected, one of which structurally cannot do the task. The cross-dataset degradation claim mixes protocols. No human accuracy ceiling is reported anywhere, only a hand-wave that the gap is significant. And there is no statement of whether the train, validation, and test split is template-stratified or simply random, which on a dataset whose entire pitch is template diversity is the one methodological detail I most want.
[G] I will grant you almost all of that. The audit gap is real. The self-selected baseline is real. The missing human ceiling is real, and it matters because Math twenty-three K papers do report one. Judged as an existence proof that the dataset is not saturated, it does the job.
[O] Then let me make the case the other way. The construct here is genuinely better than what came before. Not just more rows, but more shapes, and a deliberate emphasis on problems that need knowledge the text does not give you. The error analysis is unusually candid for a dataset paper. [G] I will grant you the construct argument and the candor. I will qualify the influence one. They cite it in prose, as a comparison point. They do not run their models on it. And I think that is the tell.
[S] Because of the withdrawal?
[G] Partly, and partly because everything evaluated in this paper predates the language model era entirely. StackDecoder, the G T S model, and the paper's own baseline are all pre-trained-from-scratch neural architectures, per-dataset. None of these accuracy numbers tell you anything about how a modern model does on Ape two ten K. Seventy point two is a twenty-twenty number about a twenty-twenty architecture.
[O] So the dataset outlived its own leaderboard.
[G] The dataset outlived its own leaderboard, and it outlived its own paper. Which brings us back to where we started.
[S] Say the practical version for someone who might actually want to use this.
[G] Three things. One, it is a Chinese-language dataset of Chinese elementary school curriculum problems. That is easy to lose when you see it cited in a list next to GSM eight K or the MATH dataset. It measures something related but not identical, and cross-lingual transfer is not something this paper tests or claims. Two, if you evaluate a modern model on the circulating copy, you cannot assume the data is clean. It has been publicly mirrored since twenty twenty. Treat leakage as live. Three, whatever number you get, you cannot compare it to the paper's numbers with confidence, because you cannot establish that you are running on the same records.
[O] That third one is the strange part. Usually contamination and irreproducibility are the same worry. Here they are separate worries pointing in opposite directions.
[G] They are, and that is the genuinely instructive thing about this paper as a case study. The community's instinct when a resource is withdrawn is to mirror it, and the mirror preserves availability. It does not preserve provenance. You end up with a dataset that is simultaneously too available to be safe and too unverified to be authoritative.
