[O] Here is a paper that throws away ninety-one percent of its own data on purpose, and I think that is the most interesting design decision in Chinese dialogue modeling from that era.
[S] And here is the same paper from where I sit. It spends its entire argument on how rigorously it cleaned a corpus, and then evaluates every single model on a different corpus that it tells you, in its own introduction, is sixty percent contaminated with the exact junk it just spent ten pages removing.
[O] That is a fair description of what happened. I would just say the cleaning still shows up in the numbers.
[S] Some of the numbers. We are going to spend a while on which ones.
[O] This is Litsearch Audio. Today, A Large-Scale Chinese Short-Text Conversation Dataset, by Yida Wang and colleagues out of Tsinghua, published at NLPCC in 2020. The dataset is called LCCC, and it ships with pre-trained models under the name CDial-GPT.
[S] It is on the docket because it is one of the founding data artifacts for open-domain Chinese dialogue, and because its evaluation design has a hole in it that is worth being precise about rather than vague about.
[O] And to be precise about it we have Susanna with us, who has read this one closely. Welcome.
[G] Thank you. One framing note before we start, because almost every confusion about this paper comes from collapsing it. LCCC is two corpora, not one. LCCC-base is six point eight million dialogues, Weibo only. LCCC-large is twelve million dialogues, Weibo mixed with nine other public Chinese corpora and cleaned at a looser threshold. Nearly every number in the paper belongs to one or the other, never both.
[O] Set the scene for 2020. What did English have that Chinese did not?
[G] English had a full pre-training stack for dialogue. DialoGPT post-trained GPT-2 on one hundred forty-seven million Reddit conversations. Meena trained an Evolved Transformer with two point six billion parameters on roughly forty billion words of social media chat. PLATO was in the same wave. The paper's claim is that the bottleneck for Chinese was not modeling, because the Transformer recipe transferred fine. It was that no comparable Chinese corpus existed.
[S] There were Chinese dialogue datasets, though.
[G] There were, and the paper tabulates them. They fail in two opposite directions. The crowdsourced ones are clean and small. Wizard of Wikipedia, Document Grounded Conversations, PERSONA-CHAT, DailyDialog, and on the Chinese side DuConv at under thirty thousand dialogues. The scraped ones are large and noisy. STC, from Wang and colleagues in 2013, is four point four million Weibo post-response pairs, and that was the workhorse everybody used.
[O] So the gap is nothing that is both big enough to pre-train on and clean enough to be worth pre-training on.
[G] That is the pitch, and they put a number on the noise side of it. Using their own blacklist, they estimate that sixty percent of STC conversations contain dirty words, sensitive words, or special symbols.
[S] Start with where the raw data comes from, because for a scraped corpus that is half the quality story.
[G] The crawl is seeded rather than random, and I think this part is underrated. They manually select Weibo accounts belonging to professional news outlets. Then they treat the users who comment under those news posts as high-quality users, on the reasoning that spam and robot accounts do not generally bother engaging with daily news. From those seed users they collect posts plus the full comment trees underneath, and they run depth-first search over each tree, so any root-to-leaf path becomes one conversation session. That yields seventy-nine million raw sessions.
[O] So the anti-bot filter is sociological rather than statistical. You pick a population that spam does not frequent.
[G] Exactly. And it is a prior, not a guarantee. The paper never measures how many automated accounts it actually excluded.
[S] Then the cleaning itself.
[G] Two phases. Phase one is nine heuristic rules plus a five-category blacklist. The rules strip platform tags, the reply-to-at-somebody prefixes. Remove URLs. Split any conversation over thirty turns into shorter ones. Collapse a word or phrase repeated more than six times in a sentence down to a single copy. Drop dialogues whose response is too long or too short. Drop responses flagged as advertisements by the classifier from the original STC paper. Drop responses where ninety percent of the trigrams are high-frequency trigrams, a filter borrowed straight from DialoGPT. Drop specific generic response forms. And drop dialogues where the response is identical to the post.
[O] That last one is very Weibo. The comment that just quotes the post back at you.
[G] It is, and the platform-tag rule is the same flavor. A great deal of what they remove is not language at all, it is interface residue. Reply markers, sticker codes, advertisement boilerplate.
[S] And the blacklist.
[G] Five categories. Dirty words, sensitive words, and dialect. Special-topic words, and their published example is the antibiotic levofloxacin. Names, appellations, and unknown abbreviations. Special symbols and emoji. And platform signs, meaning words associated with ads, images, and videos. If a dialogue contains anything on the list, the whole dialogue is discarded.
[S] Stop there. Dialect is on the same list as obscenity?
[G] It is, in that grouping, as written. And I think it is the most consequential line in the cleaning section, because filtering dialect out of a national conversation corpus is a real cost, and the paper neither quantifies it nor defends it.
[O] To be fair, the rules are the crude pass. The classifiers are where the judgment happens.
[G] Phase two, yes, and it is two separately trained BERT classifiers. The first is trained on one hundred thousand manually labeled conversations, targeting what rules cannot see. Disfluent responses or serious typos. Incomplete information. Time-sensitive topics. References in the response to festivals, places, gender, or time that never appeared in the post. And post-response pairs that are simply irrelevant to each other. It reaches seventy-three point seven six percent accuracy on its test set.
[O] And the second one is doing something different.
[G] The second targets a specific social-media pathology, conversations that only make sense given context outside the visible text. Somebody replying to a photograph you cannot see, or to a shared external event. That classifier is trained on ten thousand manually labeled utterances and reaches seventy-seven point six zero percent accuracy. One hundred thousand plus ten thousand is where the abstract's one hundred ten thousand labeled examples comes from.
[S] Seventy-three and seventy-seven percent accuracy. Those are the gatekeepers deciding the fate of ninety percent of the corpus.
[G] They are. And to be exact about the disposal rate. The raw Weibo pool is seventy-nine point five million dialogues, about fifty-two point seven million single-turn and twenty-six point seven million multi-turn. What comes out the other side as LCCC-base is six point eight two million. That is eight point five eight percent surviving.
[O] And LCCC-large?
[G] Different pool, looser threshold. They mix the same seventy-nine million Weibo sessions with nine other public corpora. Six treated as single-turn, including the Chinese Chatterbot corpus, the PTT Gossiping corpus, a subtitle corpus, Xiaohuangji, Qingyun, and Tieba. Three treated as multi-turn, including Douban and an e-commerce conversation corpus. That combined pool is about ninety-one point four million, and twelve million survive, so thirteen point one percent. Same pipeline, relaxed classifier confidence. The paper's own words are extremely strict cleaning for base, and just strict cleaning for large.
[O] Which made LCCC the largest Chinese dialogue corpus available at the time.
[G] No, and this is worth correcting because it does get repeated. In the paper's own comparison table, PersonalDialog is the largest at twenty point eight three million dialogues, with fifty-six point two six million utterances and eight point four seven million user profiles. LCCC-large at twelve million is second. LCCC-base at six point eight million is third, ahead of STC, Douban, and DuConv. The claim the paper actually makes for itself is about cleaning, not about scale.
[S] Good. That is the honest version, and it is a better claim anyway.
[O] Then they train models on it.
[G] They do, and the model line is a decent ablation if you read it that way. The base model is GPTNovel, a twelve-layer GPT pre-trained for seventy epochs on about half a billion tokens of Chinese novels spanning comedy, romance, and mystery. CDialGPT-base is that same model post-trained for thirty epochs on LCCC-base. CDialGPT-large is post-trained on LCCC-large. And CDialGPT2-base is a GPT-2 style variant on LCCC-base. All around one hundred four million parameters, twelve heads, seven hundred sixty-eight dimensional embeddings, a thirteen thousand eighty-eight character vocabulary, trained on four RTX 2080 Ti cards.
[O] Four consumer graphics cards for the whole thing. A nice reminder of what 2020 actually looked like.
[S] Now the evaluation. Say exactly what they fine-tune and test on.
[G] Every model, including both LCCC-post-trained ones, is fine-tuned on STC. Four point four million conversation pairs, split into train plus a twenty thousand pair validation set and a disjoint twenty thousand pair test set. The baselines are GPT2-chitchat, an existing open Chinese GPT-2 pre-trained on five hundred thousand conversations, a six-layer vanilla Transformer, and a six-layer recurrent sequence-to-sequence model with Bahdanau attention, sized to be parameter-comparable.
[S] STC being the corpus they told us in the introduction is sixty percent noisy.
[G] The same one. Hold onto that, because the results only make sense with it in view.
[O] Give me the automatic numbers.
[G] Perplexity first, where LCCC looks best. GPTNovel is twenty-one point two seven. CDialGPT-base is eighteen point three eight. CDialGPT-large is eighteen point two three. Same architecture, same novel pre-training, the only thing changing is the dialogue post-training data. So that progression is a genuine ablation of LCCC's contribution, even though the paper never labels it as one.
[O] That is a real effect.
[G] It is. BLEU is messier. BLEU-2 runs five point nine six for GPTNovel, six point four eight for LCCC-base, six point six three for LCCC-large. Right direction. But the plain, non-pre-trained Transformer gets six point seven two, which beats all of them. BLEU-4 has the same shape, three point two zero for the best LCCC model against three point one four for the Transformer, so there LCCC only just edges it.
[S] So the pre-training does not buy you BLEU.
[G] The authors say so plainly, and cite DialoGPT and Li's 2020 investigation as prior cases of the same pattern. Their defense is Meena's finding that perplexity correlates strongly with human judgment, so they lean on perplexity.
[S] What about diversity? The distinct n-gram scores.
[G] GPT2-chitchat wins both, distinct-one at point zero one zero three and distinct-two at point one six two five, the highest in the table. And I want to be careful here, because this gets misattributed. The paper's explanation is not that GPT2-chitchat had a bigger or dirtier pre-training corpus. Their stated reason is the small scale of the model itself, eighty-eight million parameters against one hundred four million for GPTNovel and every CDialGPT checkpoint. They read the high distinct scores as an artifact of poor generation quality rather than genuine diversity.
[O] Which the human evaluation then supports.
[G] It does, and that is the cleanest cross-check in the paper. Human evaluation is two hundred samples per model, one thousand total, three judges, scoring two, one, or zero on fluency, relevance, and informativeness. CDialGPT-large scores one point two one seven. CDialGPT-base one point one five two. Transformer one point zero eight four. Sequence-to-sequence point seven five six. And GPT2-chitchat is last at point six seven seven, the same model with the best diversity numbers.
[S] So the diversity metric ranked the worst model first. That is a tidy little indictment of distinct-n as a standalone quality signal.
[O] Agreed, and I will score that as a point for the paper. Two evaluations that disagree, resolving cleanly.
[S] Except the human evaluation is missing the model I most want to see. Susanna, is GPTNovel in the human table?
[G] It is not. Five models were human-evaluated. Sequence-to-sequence, Transformer, GPT2-chitchat, and the two CDialGPT checkpoints. Two hundred times five is the one thousand samples. GPTNovel and CDialGPT2-base are both absent.
[S] That is the whole ballgame. GPTNovel is the ablation arm. It is the model that tells you what LCCC post-training actually bought you with humans in the loop. And the paper's defense of its weak BLEU is precisely that perplexity tracks human judgment, and then it declines to run the human judgment on the one comparison where that defense matters.
[G] That is a fair statement of the gap. The human evaluation shows LCCC-post-trained models beating non-pre-trained baselines. It does not isolate LCCC from the novel-corpus pre-training underneath it.
[O] I will concede that. I would still say the perplexity progression is evidence, just not human-validated evidence.
[S] And there is a second wrinkle. CDialGPT2-base has a perplexity of twenty-two point seven six. Worse than GPTNovel's twenty-one point two seven.
[G] Correct. Post-training that variant on LCCC made perplexity worse. The paper reports the number and does not discuss it. It is the one row that complicates a straight, LCCC-helps reading, and it is also excluded from the human evaluation.
[O] Let me make the optimist case properly, because I do not think it collapses. Somebody had to build this. Chinese open-domain dialogue had a four point four million pair noisy benchmark and essentially nothing else at pre-training scale. LCCC shipped twelve million cleaned dialogues, roughly thirty-three million utterances, plus released checkpoints, publicly, in 2020. It has around one hundred fifty citations. The artifact did the work the paper promised even where the evaluation section did not prove it.
[S] I will grant every word of that and still say the science does not close. Here is the structure of my objection. The thesis is that cleaning improves dialogue models. To test that you need a clean target to measure against. They fine-tune on STC and they test on STC. So every metric in the paper, perplexity, BLEU, greedy matching, embedding average, is scored against reference responses drawn from the sixty percent noisy corpus. A model that genuinely generates cleaner Chinese would be penalized by that reference set, not rewarded by it.
[G] That is the paper's central structural weakness, and I do not think there is a good rebuttal available. There is no evaluation on a held-out clean set, not even a slice of LCCC itself, which they had sitting right there. So the paper cannot separate fits STC's reference distribution slightly better from generates less noisy text. And the second one is the thing LCCC exists to deliver.
[O] What about the five-times claim? They do say an STC-trained model produces five times more blacklisted words than an LCCC-trained one.
[G] That claim is one sentence in section three point three. No table, no sample size, and no specification of which configuration counts as the model trained on STC. None of the seven rows in the automatic-metrics table is labeled that way. And it is measured with their own blacklist. Filter your data with a blacklist, train a model on what survives, then count blacklist hits in the output. That result is very close to guaranteed.
[S] Which is my objection in miniature. The cleaning is being graded by the cleaner.
[O] Fine. But does that make the corpus bad? No. It means the paper under-tested a corpus that was probably fine.
[G] I think that is right, and I would score it that way. The artifact is well-motivated and the pipeline is documented in unusual detail for 2020. It is the evidentiary chain from we cleaned it to cleaning improved generation that has a break in it.
[S] One more, and this is the one that actually moved me. LCCC-base is the cleaner corpus. Extremely strict, eight point six percent survival. LCCC-large is the looser one, thirteen point one percent survival. Which wins?
[G] Large. On perplexity, eighteen point two three against eighteen point three eight. On BLEU-2, six point six three against six point four eight. On the human score, one point two one seven against one point one five two. The less aggressively cleaned corpus wins every comparison the paper reports.
[O] That is genuinely awkward for the thesis.
[S] It is more than awkward. The headline is rigor of cleaning, and the paper's own experiment says the less rigorous version is better.
[G] I want to hold the confound, though, because it is a real one. LCCC-large differs from LCCC-base along three axes at once. It is nearly twice the size, it draws on nine additional source corpora with different domains, and it uses a relaxed classifier threshold. You cannot attribute the win to any one of those. It is entirely consistent with more and more diverse data beating marginally cleaner data, which is not a scandal, it is the standard scaling story. The paper simply does not run the experiment that would tell you.
[S] Which would be what, exactly?
[G] Same pool, two thresholds. Clean the seventy-nine million Weibo sessions strictly and loosely, train on both, compare. That is the one ablation that isolates cleaning intensity, and it is not in the paper.
[O] I will take that as the fair verdict. Not cleaning does not work. Cleaning intensity was never independently measured.
[S] Susanna, one thing I want to make sure we do not overclaim. None of us are evaluating the Chinese here.
[G] Correct, and it matters, because the interesting failure modes are language and platform specific. What I can speak to is what the paper documents and what it shows. It prints its own generated samples, including a self-play transcript between two copies of the LCCC-large model, and in that published example the model repeats itself and echoes the user's line back verbatim. The authors put that in the paper.
[O] I respect that quite a lot, actually. Showing your model looping, in your own case study.
[G] And on the cleaning side, the categories they target, platform reply markers, sticker codes, advertisement boilerplate, high-frequency trigram filler, are Weibo-shaped problems that a generic English pipeline would never catch. That part is real domain work. Whether the surviving eight point six percent reads as natural Chinese is a question the paper answers only through three annotators, with Fleiss kappa between point three nine and point four five.
[S] Which the authors themselves describe as fair to moderate agreement.
[G] They do, and there is no significance test on the human deltas. One point one five two against one point two one seven, at that agreement level, on two hundred samples per model, is not a gap I would build a claim on.
[O] So what does this mean for how we evaluate data interventions now, which is a very live question with synthetic data and filtering pipelines everywhere.
[S] For me it is a rule. If your contribution is a filter, you may not evaluate it with the filter. That is grading your own homework with your own answer key, and it produces a number that cannot fail.
[G] I would put it slightly differently. A data intervention needs a target distribution that is independent of the intervention. LCCC's evaluation inherits its reference set from the very corpus it is trying to replace, so the measurement is anchored to the thing being criticized. That failure mode is extremely common in modern data papers, and it usually goes unnoticed because the headline number goes up.
[O] And the flip side, which I will keep defending, is that the artifact outlived the evaluation. CDial-GPT and LCCC got used, widely. Which raises a contamination point for anyone reading this now.
[G] Yes. There is no classical leakage inside the paper, the STC test split is disjoint from training. But LCCC has been a public repository since 2020. Any modern Chinese model's numbers on LCCC or LCCC-derived checkpoints should be assumed pre-training contaminated.
[S] Good place to land it. Susanna, give us the takeaway.
[G] My one-sentence takeaway is the paper's own. LCCC is a carefully documented, aggressively filtered Chinese dialogue corpus in two variants, and its models do improve perplexity and human ratings over non-pre-trained baselines, but the cleaning that defines it is never measured against a clean held-out target.
[O] Mine is that building the artifact was the contribution. In 2020 somebody had to eat the cost of crawling seventy-nine million conversations and throwing ninety-one percent of them away, and the field got a usable Chinese pre-training corpus out of it.
[S] Mine is shorter. When a paper's whole claim is that it removed the noise, ask what it measured the result on. If the answer is the noisy dataset, the claim is untested, however good the artifact later turns out to be.
