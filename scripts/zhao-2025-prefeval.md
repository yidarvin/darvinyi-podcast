[O] Saskia, set the table. Why does this need a new benchmark? We already have a shelf full of long-context evaluations.
[G] Because the existing shelf measures a different thing. There are two families. One is personalization in the style of LaMP, where you condition a model on a user's writing history and score short-form generation. The other is the needle-in-a-haystack family, LongBench, Infinite Bench, L-Eval, BABILong, where you plant a fact deep in a document and check whether the model can find it again. Both are retrieval tests. You either hand the model the relevant context immediately before the question, or you ask it to recite a fact on demand.
[S] And the claim is that neither of those is the actual product failure.
[G] Right. The authors are after something they call proactive following. The preference is stated once, early. The later query is topically unrelated to the moment it was stated. Nobody prompts the model to remember. The question is whether the model recognizes, on its own, that this is the moment the preference applies, and then changes its behavior accordingly. Recitation is not the bar. Behavior change is the bar.
[O] That distinction feels right to me from a product standpoint. Nobody wants to append "remember I'm vegetarian" to every message.
[S] Sure, but I want to flag something early. The gap between "can retrieve" and "does apply" is real, and it's also exactly the kind of gap that could be entirely explained by ordinary long-context degradation. Hold that thought.

[O] So what's actually in the benchmark, Saskia?
[G] Three thousand manually curated preference-and-query pairs, spanning twenty topics across seven domains. Entertainment, Travel, Lifestyle, Shopping, Education, Pets, and Profession. The topics are weighted fairly evenly, roughly three to six percent of the benchmark each, so no single topic dominates.
[S] Seven domains, twenty topics. That's a reasonable spread for everyday chatbot use.
[G] And each pair comes in one of three forms of increasing subtlety. Form one, explicit: a single direct sentence, "I dislike eating seafood due to an allergy." Form two, implicit choice-based: a two-turn exchange where the assistant offers several options, some of which violate the preference, and the user accepts or rejects one. The preference is revealed only through the choice. Form three, implicit persona-driven: a four to eight turn conversation built around an assigned persona, where the preference comes up once in passing and is never what the conversation is actually about.
[O] So the third form is closest to how people actually leak their preferences.
[G] It is, and it's the hardest to construct honestly, which is worth noting. Then, to make it a conversation rather than an isolated probe, the authors interleave real multi-session turns sampled from the LMSYS Chat one-million dataset between the preference disclosure and the final query. They stretch that gap out to a hundred thousand tokens.
[S] Real conversation turns as filler. That's a nice touch. Synthetic filler would have been suspiciously easy to attend over.
[G] Each pair then supports two task types. The generation task has the model write a free-form response, scored by an LLM-as-a-judge pipeline. Four independent Claude 3 Sonnet evaluators each answer one binary question. Does the response violate the preference? Does it acknowledge it? Does it hallucinate a preference that was never stated? Is it a helpful response? Those four binaries get aggregated by a fixed rule table, the paper's Table 1, into one of four disjoint error types.
[O] And accuracy is defined how?
[G] As the absence of any of the four errors. A clean response. The four error types are preference-unaware violation, preference hallucination violation, inconsistency violation, and unhelpful response. That last one matters more than you'd expect, as we'll see.
[S] What's the second task type?
[G] Classification. Same query, but posed as a multiple choice question with four options, one of which is preference-aligned. Scored by ordinary exact-match accuracy. It's the cheap proxy, and the authors validate it rather than just asserting it. Generation accuracy and classification accuracy correlate at a coefficient of point seven three, across six models, five methods, and twelve dialogue lengths, with each point averaged over twenty topics.

[S] What are the four?
[G] Reminder, which is a single sentence appended before the model answers, telling it to take the earlier discussion into account.
