[G] Thank you. Worth flagging that it is a six-page short paper, because the compression is part of the story. There is a demolition of an existing benchmark, a replacement dataset, and a new evaluation protocol, all inside six pages.

[O] Beatrice, set the stage. This task did not start in machine learning.
[G] It did not. The Sally-Anne test comes from Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith in 1985. A child watches Sally put a marble in a basket, watches Sally leave the room, then watches Anne move the marble to a box. The question is where Sally will look for the marble, and the correct answer is the basket, the place the marble is not. You also ask two control questions: where is the marble really, and where was it in the beginning.
[S] And the controls exist so you know the child is not answering at chance or simply misremembering the story.
[G] Exactly. Those controls become important later, because this paper's second contribution is essentially about how you score them. Then in 2017, Grant, Nematzadeh and Griffiths cast the whole thing as bAbI-style question answering. Three or four lines of story, one question, a one-word answer, auto-generated so you can produce as many training examples as a neural network wants.
[O] And the second-order version came a year later.
[G] Nematzadeh and colleagues, 2018, added beliefs about beliefs. Where does John think Mary will go to get ice cream. That comes from Perner and Wimmer's 1985 experiments with children and an ice-cream van. This paper refers to that whole family, first and second order together, as ToM-babi.
[S] And the 2018 headline was that the memory-augmented models mostly failed, especially once you inserted irrelevant sentences at test time. That got read across the field as a genuine capability gap.
[G] It did. And what this paper attacks is not the models and not the 2018 experiments. It is the inference from those scores to that conclusion.
[S] So tell me what is actually broken in the generator.
[G] ToM-babi uses three separate generators, one for true-belief stories, one for false-belief, one for second-order false-belief. Each follows a strict line-by-line skeleton. The consequence is that the story type is legible right off the surface of the text. If the word "exited" never appears, it is a true-belief story. And because the skeleton is fixed, the answer sits in a predictable slot. The container the object starts in is always named in the same sentence as the object's first mention.
[O] In fairness to the original authors, they did guard against the crudest version of that. They insert an irrelevant sentence, the "Phone rang" line, at a random position, specifically so that nobody can win by writing "always output the location named on line four".
[G] They did, and this paper says so explicitly. The point is that randomizing where one distractor sentence lands defeats a positional rule but not an ordinal one. Algorithm 1 never says line four. It says the first occurrence of the object, or the last occurrence before an "exited" event. Sliding a phone call around the story does not perturb that at all.
[S] Walk me through the four branches precisely, because the first-order and second-order cases are exactly where the belief content is supposed to live.
[G] Each branch is keyed on a word in the question. "Beginning" means it is the memory question, so return the location of the object's first occurrence. "Really" is the reality question, so return the location of its last occurrence. "Look" is first-order belief: if an "exited" event appears in the story after the last "the object is in" statement, return the location of the last object occurrence before the last "exited"; otherwise just return the last occurrence.
[S] Before the last exit, not after it.
[G] Before. And "think" is second-order: same test on whether an exit follows the last placement, but then return the location of the last object occurrence before the first "exited". So the entire difference between reasoning about a belief and reasoning about a belief about a belief is last exit versus first exit.
[O] That is genuinely bleak as a characterization of the task.
[G] And the scores are one hundred point zero on ToM-easy and one hundred point zero on the harder ToM split. Not near-ceiling, not ninety nine. Perfect on both.
[S] Which retroactively reframes the 2018 finding entirely. If a four-branch string matcher is perfect, then the models scoring in the low nineties were not partially succeeding at theory of mind. They were partially succeeding at string matching.
[G] And the paper localizes where that partial failure came from. Table 2 breaks Memory Networks down by question type on the original data. First-order accuracy on ToM is ninety eight point six. Second-order is ninety five point three. Both essentially at ceiling. The number dragging the average down to ninety point seven is the memory question, at seventy one point nine, and the authors attribute that to ToM's stories being longer, so the object's first mention falls outside the model's memory capacity.
[O] So the hard part of the hard theory-of-mind split was remembering the beginning of a long story. Not belief at all.
[G] That is the paper's claim, and Table 2 is the evidence for it.
[S] I want to flag the methodological move here, and I mean it as a compliment that it is not novel. Ablate an input that the task supposedly requires, then check whether performance survives.
[G] The paper cites that lineage directly. Jabri and colleagues on visual question answering baselines that use only the candidate answers. Gururangan and colleagues, and Poliak and colleagues, on hypothesis-only baselines in natural language inference. Caglayan and colleagues showing multimodal machine translation benchmarks did not actually need the image. CLEVR as the diagnostic-dataset response in vision. And they open the section with Clever Hans, the horse.
[O] The principle they state is clean. If theory of mind means reasoning about another agent, then a benchmark for theory of mind should be impossible to solve without some input about the other agent. That is a testable property of a dataset, and almost nobody tests it.
[S] So they build the replacement. Beatrice, separate the changes, because I count two dataset changes and one protocol change, and I think they should be graded separately.
[G] That is the right split. First, one randomized generator instead of three templated ones. It can emit any of the three story types, it records internally which type it produced, and they sample from it with rejection to keep the dataset balanced across types. So balance no longer comes from generator identity, which means generator identity no longer leaks into the surface form.
[O] That is the elegant fix. The bias existed because balancing was implemented by switching generators.
[G] Second, distractors. Actions by agents unrelated to the queried belief, to decorrelate actions from answers. Distractor statements about extra locations and objects, so that the sheer number of times something is mentioned stops being informative. Randomization of the order of the exit, move, and re-entry actions. And randomization of which agent's belief is being queried.
[S] The example stories in Figure 1 are noticeably messier than the originals. A character who dislikes the kitchen. A third person wandering in. In one of them the exit happens before the object is ever placed.
[G] Third, and logically independent of the dataset, the protocol. For every story involving agents A and B and object O, they ask all six questions. Reality, memory, first-order belief for A, first-order belief for B, second-order belief for A, second-order belief for B. And then joint accuracy: a story counts as answered correctly only if every question about that story is correct.
[O] Why does that matter beyond making the headline number smaller, which is the cynical read.
[G] Because a correct false-belief answer is only meaningful if the model also has reality right on the same story. The old protocol asked one question per story, from separately generated stories, so you could never check whether one model on one story tracked both the world and the agent's belief simultaneously. The paper is explicit about the worry: accuracy on first-order belief questions might be associated with lower accuracy on reality questions, and single-question evaluation would hide that completely.
[S] I will grant that is a real conceptual contribution independent of ToMi itself. Any benchmark with control questions could adopt it tomorrow.
[O] Numbers. What happens.
[G] Table 1 reports average accuracy over all questions, plus joint accuracy on ToMi. Memory Networks: seventy seven point two average, forty four point three joint. So across the three models, joint accuracy runs from forty four point three to sixty six point eight.

[O] So what should someone building an evaluation today take from this.
[G] The ablated-input baseline, as a default step. Before you report that models fail at your task, write the solver that is denied the information your task is supposedly about, and run it. If it does well, your number is not measuring the competence you named.
[S] And run it against your own fix, and publish the result even when it comes back thirty six point five. That is the part of this paper I respect most.
[O] The joint scoring generalizes too. If your benchmark has a "does the model actually understand the situation" control question, scoring it on a differently sampled item than the hard question tells you very close to nothing.
[G] The authors are careful about the limits in their discussion. They note ToMi is still synthetic and linguistically simple, that real-world theory of mind would additionally require coreference resolution, handling polysemy and ambiguity, and common-sense reasoning, and that solving these tasks should be treated as a prerequisite rather than a demonstration. Later work took that up. FANToM moved false-belief tracking into conversation, and MMToM-QA moved it into a multimodal setting.
[G] The paper's takeaway: existing theory-of-mind question answering benchmarks were perfectly solvable by lexical and ordinal rules that never look at an agent, and once you remove those regularities and score all six question types jointly, memory-augmented models fail specifically and severely on false-belief pairs.
[O] Mine is that this is what a healthy field looks like. Someone writes the four rules, publishes the perfect score against the benchmark everyone was citing, and hands the community a better instrument in six pages.
[S] Mine is that diagnosing a dataset is much easier than designing one. These authors did the diagnosis brilliantly, built the replacement carefully, and their own agent-blind solver still cleared better than a third of it. Assume yours leaks too.
