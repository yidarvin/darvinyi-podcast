[G] The metric is called L L M Match, and it adapts an answer scoring protocol from M M Bench. You give a language model three things: the question, the human ground truth answer, and the agent's generated answer. It returns a similarity score on a one to five scale. One is incorrect, five is correct, and two through four cover degrees of partial similarity. The aggregate score subtracts one from each score, divides by four, averages over all questions, and multiplies by one hundred, so the whole thing lands on a zero to one hundred correctness axis.
[O] Which is why every number we are quoting is a percentage rather than an exact match rate.
[G] Right. Nothing in this paper is exact match. That is the point of the reformulation.
[S] And for the active setting, correctness alone is not enough, because an agent could wander the whole house and eventually stumble onto the answer.
[G] Which is why there is a second metric. It weights the same correctness term by the ratio of a ground truth exploration path length to the maximum of the agent's own path length and that ground truth length. It is modelled on S P L, success weighted by path length, a standard navigation metric. The authors note that the ground truth paths came from a scripted exploratory agent and are not necessarily optimal, so an efficiency score above one hundred percent is theoretically possible.
