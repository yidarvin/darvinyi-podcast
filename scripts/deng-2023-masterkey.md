# ---- context and problem ----
[G] That's exactly the right frame. Jailbreak prompts, meaning prompts engineered to talk a chatbot past its own usage policy, were already a well-documented phenomenon by mid-twenty twenty-three, but almost entirely documented against one target. Before testing anything, the authors first map the terrain: they pull the written usage policies of four providers, OpenAI, Google's Bard, Bing Chat, and Baidu's Ernie, across ten possible prohibited-content categories. Only four categories turn out to be both specified in policy and actually enforced by all four providers: illegal activity, harmful or abusive content, adult content, and privacy violations. Those four become the categories tested through the rest of the paper.
[S] So the scope of the whole study is set by where the four providers actually agree, not by whichever categories looked most dramatic.
[G] Right. With that scope fixed, they run their own empirical study: eighty-five jailbreak prompts, pulled from a public prompt-sharing site and an earlier academic study, fired ten times each at four commercial chatbots, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Bard, and Bing Chat, across those four categories. Sixty-eight thousand queries total.
[O] And the results were not close.
[G] Not remotely. GPT-3.5 fell for these jailbreaks twenty-one point one two percent of the time, averaged across the four categories. GPT-4 fell seven point one three percent of the time. Bard, zero point four percent. Bing Chat, zero point six three percent. Everything the community thought it knew about how broken jailbreak defenses were had effectively been measured on the two most vulnerable services.
[S] Which on its own is a decent little paper. Here's a measurement gap, go fix your evaluations.
[G] It would be, except they noticed a second pattern while running that first study. When GPT-3.5 or GPT-4 refuses a jailbreak, it tells you why, it names the specific policy you tripped. Bard and Bing Chat just return some version of "I can't help with that," no matter how hard you press for a reason. The authors read that as a hint: Bard and Bing Chat are probably running some extra, undisclosed moderation layer on top of the base model's own training.
[O] A generic refusal as evidence of an extra system bolted on.
[S] Which is an inference from silence, and a weak one on its own. A generic refusal could just as easily mean the model simply wasn't trained to explain itself, full stop, nothing extra involved.

# ---- method: the side channel ----
[O] So walk us through how you test a hypothesis about a system you're not allowed to look inside.
[G] The core insight is almost embarrassingly simple once you hear it. These chatbots generate text one token at a time, and the wall-clock time a response takes is strongly tied to how many tokens actually got generated, including on a jailbreak attempt that gets cut off partway through. If you can control roughly how long a response should take, and then watch how long it actually takes under different conditions, timing itself becomes a signal, even when the chatbot gives you nothing else to go on.
[S] Which is a real, named technique here, not a loose metaphor. This is time-based blind SQL injection, just pointed at a chatbot instead of a database.
[G] That's the paper's own comparison, explicitly. In blind SQL injection, you splice a conditional delay into a query. If the database takes five extra seconds to respond, the condition was true, no error message required, the delay itself leaks the answer. Here, the moderation pipeline plays the role of the database, and the delay leaks whether, and where, and how, it's stepping in.
[O] Before trusting a timing signal for anything, you'd want the underlying relationship to actually hold. Requested length really does predict generation time.
[G] They check that first, as a proof of concept. They ask each of the four services for responses of fifty, one hundred, one hundred fifty, and two hundred tokens, and measure both the tokens actually produced and the time taken. The Pearson correlation between requested length and generation time comes out positive and significant for every service, but the strength varies a lot: point five six seven for GPT-3.5, point eight three eight for GPT-4, point seven six two for Bard, and point four six five for Bing Chat, with p values of point oh oh nine or better across the board.
[S] Point four six five is not a strong correlation. Square it and you're explaining roughly twenty-two percent of the variance in response time from token count alone. And Bing Chat is one of exactly two services this entire side channel then goes on to reverse-engineer.
[G] That's a completely fair number to sit with, and I'd rather flag it than round past it. The relationship is real and significant everywhere, but on Bing specifically, timing is a noisier proxy than it is on GPT-4, and every downstream conclusion about Bing's defenses inherits that extra noise.
[O] Okay, the proxy is real but imperfect. What do they build on top of it?
[G] Four timing probes, each designed to localize where a defense intervenes. A baseline: two benign questions back to back, establishing that total time is roughly the sum of the two individual times when nothing gets blocked. Control one asks whether the input itself is filtered: a benign question followed by an overtly malicious one. If the input were screened before generation even starts, the response should come back almost instantly. For Bard and Bing Chat, the observed times were statistically indistinguishable from baseline, an average z of negative one point four six, p of point three four, not significant. That's evidence against input-side filtering.
[S] Meaning whatever's stopping these prompts, it isn't a pre-check reading your message before the model even starts responding.
[G] Right. Control two moves the malicious question to the front instead of the back, testing whether moderation watches the output stream live, token by token, versus only checking the finished response. Here the drop in time was large, a z of twenty-nine point four eight, p less than point oh one. That's strong evidence for real-time, streaming moderation, something watching generation as it happens and cutting it off mid-stream.
[O] That z-score is enormous. Whatever's happening there, it isn't noise.
[G] No, that's the cleanest result in the whole timing study. Control three is the subtlest: it asks the model to place a red-flag keyword at a specific position inside an otherwise benign two-hundred-token response, and checks whether elapsed time tracks that keyword's position rather than the baseline. The result there was a z of negative two point four five, p of point oh seven, which the authors themselves describe as falling just short of the conventional point oh five threshold, even while reading it as consistent with keyword-position tracking.
[S] So Control three is the one I'd push back on treating as a finding rather than a hint. Point oh seven is not significant by the standard the other two results are held to, and the paper's own text agrees with me there, to its credit, but it still gets folded into the headline conclusion about how these defenses work.
[G] That's a legitimate ding, and worth keeping separate in your head from Control two, which is unambiguous. Putting the three together, the authors conclude Bard and Bing Chat likely run dynamic, real-time output moderation combining keyword tracking with some semantic checking, rather than input-side filtering. And I want to be precise about what kind of claim that actually is.
[O] Go ahead, because I think this is the crux of tonight.
[G] A timing difference is direct, reproducible evidence that something changes response latency under specific prompt conditions. It is not direct evidence of the defense's actual architecture. The paper's Findings four through six, no input filtering, real-time monitoring, keyword-plus-semantic checking, are the authors' best inference from that timing signal, not a confirmed description of Bard's or Bing Chat's source code. The paper hedges this explicitly, more than once, saying its understanding may not perfectly mirror the actual defense design, that it offers a valuable approximation rather than ground truth.
[S] Which I respect, it's the honest way to write it up. My concern isn't that the authors overclaim on the page. It's what happens two steps downstream, once that inference becomes the seed for an entire generation pipeline.
[G] Which is exactly what happens next. From that inferred picture, the authors hand-craft one proof-of-concept prompt, a role-play framing around a character unbound by ordinary rules, with the actual trigger words spaced apart letter by letter to dodge keyword matching, and layered with code-block formatting on top. That single prompt works across all four services, and it becomes the seed for MASTERKEY itself.
# ---- method: the generator ----
[O] And this is where it stops being a one-off trick and becomes a system.
[G] Exactly, part two. Instead of hand-writing more prompts, they fine-tune Vicuna, the thirteen-billion-parameter open model, into a dedicated jailbreak-prompt generator, on eight A100 GPUs, through three stages. First, dataset building: starting from those eighty-five existing prompts plus the proof of concept, they strip out service-specific language, "ChatGPT" becomes "you," "OpenAI" becomes "developer," and expand the set by having ChatGPT itself rephrase each prompt inside a template that explicitly says rephrase this, don't execute it, which produces paraphrased variants that preserve intent.
[S] Using one chatbot to help generate attacks against other chatbots.
[G] It's a clever bit of jiu-jitsu, framing the task as translation rather than attack generation slips past ChatGPT's own guardrails. Second stage, continuous pre-training and task tuning: the base model first just reads the whole jailbreak corpus to absorb the style, then gets explicitly tuned on original-prompt-to-rephrased-prompt pairs to learn the rewriting behavior itself. Third stage, reward-ranked fine-tuning, RAFT: generated prompts get tested against the real chatbots, each successful jailbreak against a target is worth one point, summed across however many targets it beats, and the model is fine-tuned again on the resulting mix of high- and low-reward examples, so it learns to prefer prompts that generalize across services rather than overfitting to just one.
[O] So the reward signal is doing something specific: pushing the model toward prompts that transfer, not just prompts that work once.
[G] That's the intent. We'll see in a minute how much of the actual lift that step is responsible for, versus the fine-tuning stages before it.
# ---- results ----

[G] The main evaluation is large: eighty-five prompts, each rewritten into ten variants, tested against twenty prohibited questions, across four tested services, using four different prompt-generation models as the source of the prompts, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Vicuna, and MASTERKEY itself. Eighty-five times ten times twenty times four times four, two hundred seventy-two thousand queries in total.
[O] That's a genuinely large evaluation for an attack paper.
[G] It is, and the headline number is an average query success rate of twenty-one point five eight percent for MASTERKEY, against seven point three three percent for the original, un-rewritten prompts. It's not just an average that happens to look good, either: the ranking MASTERKEY beats GPT-4 beats GPT-3.5 beats Vicuna, as prompt-generation models, holds in all sixteen of the sixteen tested-model-by-category combinations. Not just on average, every single cell.
[S] Sixteen for sixteen is a genuinely clean result. I don't have a "the aggregate hides a mixed picture" objection there.
[G] You don't, and I checked that against the underlying table myself before tonight. Where I'd add texture rather than pushback: Vicuna, the un-fine-tuned baseline, is genuinely bad at the rewriting task itself. Given a template asking it to rephrase the bracketed text, it would sometimes just echo the literal placeholder back instead of substituting the real prompt in. That shows up numerically too, Vicuna-generated prompts actually underperform simply reusing the original, unmodified prompt in fourteen of those sixteen cells, and tie in a fifteenth.
[O] Meaning the fine-tuning-free baseline isn't losing because trained models are inherently better attackers in some deep sense. It's losing partly because Vicuna, out of the box, can't reliably do the rewrite at all.
[G] That's a fair read, and it's the authors' own qualitative explanation too, they show exactly that failure mode in the paper. The number the paper foregrounds hardest, though, is Bard and Bing Chat specifically: MASTERKEY reaches fourteen point five one percent against Bard and thirteen point six three percent against Bing Chat, which the authors describe as the first published successful jailbreaks against either service.
[S] Those are the same two services the initial study found were essentially unbreakable, zero point four percent and zero point six three percent with existing prompts.
[G] Right, going from under one percent to double digits, on the two hardest targets, is the paper's central claim to novelty. There's also a smaller supplementary experiment worth knowing about: they translate twenty sampled prompts into simplified Chinese and test them against Baidu's Ernie, reporting a six point four five percent average success rate. That one's explicitly scaled down, twenty prompts, one language, because repeated failed attempts on Ernie risked rate-limiting or account suspension, so I'd treat it as a transferability gesture rather than a fully powered result.
[O] Now the ablation, because that's where I want to know what's actually earning that twenty-one point five eight.
[G] This is the part I'd point people to first, honestly. They test two cut-down variants, MASTERKEY-no-finetune and MASTERKEY-no-reward. Removing fine-tuning entirely is catastrophic, costing between ninety-one and ninety-eight percent of the full model's success rate depending on the service. On GPT-3.5 specifically, the full pipeline's forty-four point two one percent collapses to three point two percent with no fine-tuning at all. Removing only the reward-ranking step, while keeping fine-tuning, is a real loss too, but much smaller, twenty-seven to forty-five percent, that same forty-four point two one percent on GPT-3.5 drops to thirty-two point two eight without reward-ranking.
[S] Those are not the same size of effect. Not close.
[G] They're not, and I'd say that more plainly than the paper itself does. The authors' own language is that both fine-tuning and reward-ranked feedback are indispensable, which is technically true, both cuts hurt. But "indispensable" implies comparable weight, and a ninety-plus percent collapse against a twenty-seven-to-forty-five percent loss is not comparable weight. Continuous pre-training and task-tuning is doing the overwhelming majority of the work of turning a general-purpose model into an effective jailbreak rewriter. Reward-ranking is a real, secondary refinement on top of that, not a co-equal pillar.
[O] Which is actually a more interesting engineering story than "both parts matter equally." It tells you where to invest if you were building on this.
[S] Or where to worry, if you're building a defense. The expensive part to reproduce is the fine-tuning corpus and process, not the reward signal.
# ---- the debate ----
[O] Let me make the strongest case for why this matters, before you two start picking it apart further. This paper does two things nobody had published before: it gives you a general, reusable methodology for inferring what an opaque safety system is doing, using nothing but a stopwatch, and it shows that methodology can be turned into an automated attack that breaks services previously considered resistant. Bard and Bing Chat go from essentially unbroken to double-digit success rates. And the disclosure story is clean: they say they didn't exploit any of this to cause harm, reported findings to the providers promptly, and withheld the actual working prompts and training data from publication.
[S] I'll take the other side, and I want to start with the word "actually." A timing difference is not the defense, it's a correlate of the defense, measured on a live, multi-tenant commercial API, with server load, caching, model routing across capacity pools, network path, and geography all sitting between the request and the timer. The paper states how many questions go into each timing cell, five for the token-time study, twenty for the main timing tests, but never states how many times each individual measurement was repeated, or whether conditions were interleaved to guard against drift. A z of twenty-nine is probably too large for ordinary load jitter to explain away. A z of negative two point four five, right at the edge of significance, is exactly the kind of result where I'd want repeat counts and randomization before I trust it.
[G] Both points land, and I'd rather adjudicate them separately than average them into "somewhat trustworthy." Control two, the real-time-monitoring result, is the strongest evidence in the paper, full stop, the effect size is large enough that ordinary infrastructure noise is an unlikely explanation. Control three is exactly what the paper's own words already flag it as, suggestive, not confirmed at conventional significance. So the finding that these services do something beyond input filtering, solid. The specific claim that it's keyword-position tracking, weaker, resting on a result that falls just short of significance.
[O] Fine, but even granting every one of Callum's caveats, the top-line achievement survives: first published jailbreaks against Bard and Bing Chat, using a technique grounded in an actual measurement, not a guess.
[S] It survives for the moment those measurements were taken. And that's my second objection, which is really about time, not statistics. Every number in this paper, twenty-one point five eight, seven point three three, zero point four for Bard, is a snapshot of specific deployed systems across roughly four months in twenty twenty-three. Bard doesn't even exist anymore under that name, it became Gemini in early twenty twenty-four. The seven point three three percent baseline was measured in that same narrow window, against those same services, so the comparison is fair at the time it was run, but the paper's own framing, that existing jailbreak approaches simply aren't effective, reads like a permanent verdict on a set of techniques when it's really a snapshot of one specific matchup.
[G] That's accurate, and it cuts in a direction worth naming explicitly: the same perishability critique the paper implicitly makes about Bard and Bing Chat's defenses, that whatever they were doing in twenty twenty-three has surely changed since, applies with equal force to MASTERKEY's own numbers. Nothing here is a durable claim about jailbreak robustness in general, or about these services today. It's a claim about this exact matchup, in this exact window.
[O] I'll take that adjustment. What I won't give up is that the methodology outlives the numbers. Even if every percentage in this paper is stale by now, here is how you time a black-box system to learn where it makes decisions is reusable against whatever comes next.
[S] Agreed, and that's actually where I'd put my own optimism, if you want to call it that. The side channel itself, well-controlled, is a genuinely useful auditing tool. My skepticism is about treating twenty-twenty-three's specific findings as settled facts about specific products, not about the technique's value in general.
[G] I'd add one more thing neither of you has raised: the ablation result is the part of this paper I'd bet on outlasting the headline numbers. Fine-tuning does the overwhelming majority of the work, reward-ranking is secondary, is a claim about a training recipe, not about a moving target's current defenses. That one I'd expect to still be roughly true.
# ---- implications ----
[O] Zoom out for a second, Callum. Where does this connect to how we evaluate systems more broadly, beyond jailbreaks specifically?
[G] It's a clean case study in measuring something you can't directly observe. The entire method rests on the idea that behavior you can measure from outside, response latency, stands in for a property you can't see directly, the internal structure of a safety system. That's the same basic move as using a benchmark score as a proxy for a capability you can't directly inspect, and it inherits the same discipline: state your proxy's assumptions, check them, and don't let the inferred picture harden into fact faster than the evidence supports.
[S] Which is exactly the discipline I'd want applied to red-teaming and safety auditing generally, not just to this one attack. If you're going to claim you've reverse-engineered a production system's defenses from the outside, repeat counts and interleaving matter as much as the headline z-score, because that's what tells a reader whether to trust the inference or just the correlation.
[O] And the disclosure model is worth other security researchers copying, regardless of where you land on the statistics: report promptly, withhold the actual working prompts, work with the vendor rather than around them.
