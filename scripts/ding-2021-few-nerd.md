[G] Sixty-six thousand paragraphs, averaging sixty-one point three tokens each. And the stated reason for going through a dictionary at all is class balance — raw Wikipedia entity distributions are wildly skewed, and a few-shot benchmark with a severely uneven type distribution is not much use.

[G] It is entirely human, and the protocol is unusually well documented. Seventy trained annotators plus ten experienced experts. [S] Fine. Now the benchmark settings, because there are three and they are not interchangeable.
[G] Three tasks on one corpus. The first is Few-NERD S U P, an ordinary supervised split — seventy percent train, ten dev, twenty test, with all sixty-six types present in every split. That measures instance-level generalization. Roughly one hundred thirty-two thousand training sentences.
[O] And the two few-shot settings partition the types instead of the sentences.
[G] Correct, and this is the mechanically interesting bit. They split the sixty-six fine types into three disjoint sets, and then — because a single sentence can contain entities from several types — they blank out any entity whose type is not in the active split, replacing its label with O. So a training sentence containing a test-set entity type does not leak that type; the mention is simply relabelled as non-entity.
[S] That is the only honest way to do it, and it is also a little lossy.
[G] It is lossy, and they acknowledge it by saying they tried to design the partitions to replace as few entities as possible. Now, the two settings differ in how the partition is drawn. Few-NERD INTRA splits by coarse type. Every fine type under Person, Miscellaneous, Art and Product goes to train. Event and Building go to dev. Organization and Location go to test.
[O] So train and test share no coarse structure whatsoever.
[G] Essentially none. Few-NERD INTER instead splits within each coarse type — roughly sixty percent of each coarse type's fine subtypes to train, twenty percent to dev, twenty percent to test. So all eight coarse categories appear in every split. INTRA is asking whether you can generalize to a new region of the type space. INTER is asking whether coarse-level knowledge you already have helps you pick up new fine types underneath it.
[S] Was the INTRA design a guess, or did they justify it?
[G] They justified it, and I think this is the best small experiment in the paper. They take a Bert Tagger trained on seventy percent of the data, use it to embed entity mentions from the held-out twenty percent, sample a hundred instances per fine type, and compute the average pairwise dot product between every pair of the sixty-six types. What comes out is a heat map that blocks along the diagonal — types sharing a coarse parent are mutually much more similar than types across coarse boundaries.
[O] So they measured the correlation structure and then designed a split to destroy it.
[G] That is exactly what they did. INTRA is engineered to be hard, and the heat map is the evidence that it should be.
[S] One more mechanical thing before results. The sampling. Because N-way K-shot on a sequence labelling task is not the same problem as N-way K-shot on classification.
[G] It is genuinely harder, and the paper explains why with a clean example. Say you are doing five-way five-shot and your support set already has four classes with five examples and one class with four. The next sentence you accept must contain exactly one entity, of exactly that one remaining class. On a densely annotated corpus, such a sentence may barely exist. Greedy samplers from prior work get stuck at the end of every episode.
[O] So what is the fix?
[G] They relax the exactness. It becomes N-way K to two K shot. You keep adding sentences as long as no class exceeds a cap of two K, and you stop when every class has at least K. Each class ends up somewhere between K and two K examples rather than exactly K. It is a small change and it makes sentence-level sampling tractable on dense text.
[S] And it means every number in this paper is on a slightly different task than the exact-K numbers in the papers that preceded it.
