[S] The paper is the Baby Intuitions Benchmark, BIB, from Kanishk Gandhi, Gala Stojnic, Brenden Lake, and Moira Dillon, all at New York University. NeurIPS twenty twenty one, first posted February twenty twenty one, and funded under the DARPA Machine Common Sense program.
[O] Ruth, welcome. And I want to flag something for our listener up front, because this one sits differently from most of what we cover.
[G] It does, and it is worth being blunt about it. There is no language model anywhere in this paper. There is no prompting, no chat template, no judge. BIB is a set of silent videos of shapes moving around a grid, and the models being tested are small networks trained from scratch on tens of thousands of those videos. If you walk in with benchmark instincts from the language world, half of them will mislead you here.
[S] So set the problem up properly. Why does an infant-cognition paradigm end up at NeurIPS.
[G] Because of a gap the authors argue is structural rather than incremental. By the end of their first year, human infants already reason about other agents in ways that are abstract and flexible. They expect agents to have goals that attach to objects rather than to locations. They expect preferences to belong to particular individuals. They expect solid obstacles to constrain what an agent can reach. They expect an agent to take a detour when the detour is the only route to the goal. And they expect agents to move efficiently.
[O] And that is not folk wisdom, that is a documented literature.
[G] Decades of it, and the paper is careful to cite the specific studies each task is built from. Woodward in nineteen ninety eight for the object-versus-location result. Buresh and Woodward in two thousand seven for binding a preference to a specific actor. Scott and Baillargeon in twenty thirteen for accessibility. Sommerville and Woodward in two thousand five for instrumental action. Gergely and colleagues in nineteen ninety five for efficiency. These are among the most replicated findings in developmental psychology.
[S] And the machine-learning side has its own literature on this. Inverse reinforcement learning, Bayesian theory of mind, the ToMnet line.
[G] It does, and the paper's complaint is not that those approaches are wrong. It is that nobody had evaluated them on anything that would reveal whether the reasoning generalizes. Three specific failures. First, the Bayesian theory-of-mind evaluations, the Baker and Tenenbaum line, offered fewer than one hundred sample episodes. That is plenty for a hand-built Bayesian model and useless for anything you need to train.
[O] Second?
[G] Second, Rabinowitz and colleagues' ToMnet, which is the most direct neural ancestor of everything tested here, drew training and test episodes from the same distribution. So a high score genuinely cannot distinguish abstract reasoning from having memorized the distribution.
[S] And the third is the one I suspect the authors care most about.
[G] It is. None of those evaluations were translatable back to the paradigms actually run with infants. So there was no way to look at a model's score and say anything at all about whether the machine was failing where the baby succeeds.
[O] Which is the move BIB makes. It does not just borrow the spirit of the infant work, it imports the measurement instrument.
[G] Right, and the instrument is violation of expectation. In an infant lab, you show a baby the same event eight times, then show them an outcome that either fits the pattern or breaks it, and you measure how long they look. Infants look longer at the outcome that violates what they expected. BIB keeps that structure exactly. Every episode is nine trials. Eight familiarization trials that establish a pattern, then one test trial, presented as a matched pair of videos, one expected and one unexpected.
[S] And a model does not have looking time. So what is the substitute.
[G] Prediction error. The model watches the eight familiarization trials, forms whatever internal representation it forms, and then predicts forward through each of the two test videos. Whichever video it predicts worse is the one it was more surprised by. An episode counts as correct if the model's error is higher on the unexpected video than on its paired expected video. Chance is fifty percent by construction.
[O] Now this is the design detail I wanted, because there is a subtlety in how the pairs are built that most people skip.
[G] And it is the whole trick. The unexpected outcome is deliberately made perceptually more similar to the familiarization trials, while being conceptually implausible. The expected outcome looks perceptually different but violates nothing. So the pair is rigged against low-level pattern matching. A model that is genuinely tracking what the agent wants gets it right. A model running on surface similarity does not merely get it wrong at chance. It gets it systematically backwards.
[O] Which is my answer to your opening objection. Twenty six point three percent is not a broken benchmark. It is the instrument reporting that the model is locked onto the perceptual heuristic that the pair was designed to catch.
[S] I will grant that the design is deliberate. I am not yet granting that a single below-chance cell tells us which heuristic. Ruth, does the paper actually identify it, or is that an inference we are making on its behalf?
[G] The paper identifies it, and differently for different models, which is the part I find genuinely useful. Walk through the concrete task. Familiarization shows an agent repeatedly moving to the same object, and critically, that object sits in roughly the same place each time, so identity and location are correlated. At test the two objects swap places. The expected video has the agent follow its preferred object to the new location. The unexpected video has the agent go to the old location, where the other object now sits.
[S] So a model that learned location wins on the unexpected video every time.
[G] Every time. And that is exactly what the paper says the MLP behavior-cloning model did. It encoded a preference for a goal location rather than a goal object. The two recurrent models failed differently. They fell back on predicting the agent will approach whichever object is spatially closer, and the paper quantifies that: roughly seventy percent of trials.
[O] Let us lay out the five families properly, because the range matters for what comes later.
[G] Five evaluation families, each with one thousand episodes, so two thousand videos each. Family one, preference for a goal object rather than a goal location, the one we just did. Family two, binding that preference to a specific agent. Familiarize on one agent's choices, then at test a new agent appears, and the model should have weak or no expectations about the newcomer. Family three, inaccessible goals. The model should expect the agent to settle for its nonpreferred object only when the preferred one is walled off, not when it is still reachable.
[S] And the two harder ones.
[G] Family four is instrumental action, and it is the most structured. There is a goal object, a key shaped like a triangle, a lock, and a green removable barrier. During familiarization the agent fetches the key, inserts it into the lock, the barrier vanishes, and it goes to the object. At test there are three conditions: no barrier at all, a barrier that is present but not blocking anything, and a barrier that actually blocks the goal. The model should expect the key detour only in that third case.
[O] And family five is efficiency.
[G] Efficiency, with the crucial twist. A rational agent that has been taking efficient paths around a barrier should be expected to take a new efficient path once the barrier moves. But an agent that was moving inefficiently from the start, an irrational agent, should generate no such expectation. That second half is BIB extending the infant literature rather than just reproducing it.
[S] What is the world these live in, physically.
[G] A ten by ten grid world, overhead view, populated with simple geometric shapes standing in for agents, objects, keys, and barriers. Videos are two hundred by two hundred pixels. There is also a matched three-dimensional rendering, built with the AI2-THOR simulator, at a three-quarters viewing angle, provided to vary perceptual difficulty.
[O] And the training side, which I think is the most underrated design decision in the paper.
[G] Four background-training tasks, and every one is structurally simpler than the evaluation task it feeds. A single-object navigation task, ten thousand episodes, where the model learns how agents start, move, and are deflected by walls. A no-navigation preference task, another ten thousand, where two objects sit right next to the agent so it learns that agents have consistent preferences without learning any navigation. A single-object multi-agent task, four thousand episodes, teaching that agents can be swapped mid-episode. And an agent-blocked instrumental action task, four thousand, teaching the key-and-lock mechanic.
[S] So no background task is a rehearsal for any evaluation task.
[G] By design. To succeed on the preference evaluation, a model has to fuse navigation from one training task with preference from a completely different one, in a configuration it has never seen. The authors contrast this explicitly with AGENT, the contemporaneous benchmark from Shu and colleagues, which trains across many leave-out splits with relatively minor differences between train and test. BIB commits to one canonical split, chosen to be as demanding as they could make it.
[O] Before results, one more mechanical detail, because I think it does real work.
[G] The scoring uses the single most surprising step, not the average across the trial. Maximum prediction error, not mean. The authors are candid about why: averaging consistently produced weaker discrimination between the expected and unexpected videos. That is an empirical choice, made because it worked better.
[S] Which I want on the record as a degree of freedom. Choosing max over mean because max separates your conditions better is a defensible engineering call and it is also a knob that was turned in the direction of a bigger effect.
[G] That is fair, and the paper does not hide it. It states the alternative and states why it was rejected.
[O] Give us the models, Ruth, then the table.
[G] Three baselines in the main text, all trained purely by passive observation. Two are behavior cloning: they predict the agent's next action, a two-dimensional displacement, from a state encoder pretrained with augmented temporal contrast, plus a context encoder over the eight familiarization trials that is either an MLP or a bidirectional LSTM. That is the MLP baseline and the RNN baseline. The third is a video model, directly inspired by ToMnet. A convolutional network plus a bidirectional LSTM compresses each familiarization trial into a characteristic embedding, those are averaged across the eight trials, the result is tiled out spatially to sixty four by sixty four and stapled onto a test frame, and a U-Net decodes that into a prediction of the next frame under pixel-space mean squared error.
[O] And they all learned the training tasks.
[G] Comfortably. The video model's validation error on the background tasks runs from about two point four times ten to the minus four up to nine times ten to the minus four. [S] The training-evaluation mismatch.
[G] Yes, and it is real and specific. In the background training for instrumental action, the green barrier confines the agent. In the evaluation, it confines the object. [O] So where does this leave someone citing BIB today?
[G] Cite it for what it demonstrably is. A carefully built, large, procedurally generated diagnostic that catches three theory-of-mind-inspired architectures running on nearest-object and go-straight-to-the-goal heuristics, with the specific heuristic named in each case. Do not cite it as a measured human-machine gap, because the human side was never measured.
