[S] Right. Set the scene. Why did anyone need a new benchmark? Systems people have been benchmarking systems since forever.
[G] Because the paper argues the standard playbook does not transfer, and it names four specific reasons. The first is that throughput optimizations can quietly destroy the thing you were training for. Their words: an effect only observable by running an entire training session. Their example is precision. The accuracy gap between single-precision and lower-precision training, they say, only emerges in later epochs, and some numerical representations never match full-precision validation error at all.
[O] So if you benchmark on throughput, low precision wins every time and you never learn that it cost you a point of accuracy.
[G] Exactly. The second reason is subtler and I think it is the best paragraph in the paper. Scale does not merely change how fast the computation runs. It changes how much computation there is. Their own reference numbers: version zero point five Res Net fifty takes about sixty-four epochs to reach the seventy-four point nine percent top one target at a minibatch size of four K, whereas a minibatch size of sixteen K can require more than eighty epochs to reach that same accuracy, increasing computation by thirty percent.
[S] So a bigger machine can be less efficient in total work while still finishing sooner in wall clock.
[G] Yes, and that tension is precisely what the benchmark has to price. Third, training is stochastic. Fix your hyperparameters, fix your seed, and time-to-target still moves. Their figure on MiniGo is blunt about it. They observed, and I am quoting, considerable variability across runs even when fixing the random seed.
[O] Even the seed. So it is floating point non-determinism and asynchrony underneath.
[G] That is the implication, yes. And fourth, frameworks are not computationally identical even when they claim the same algorithm. The paper writes out two different update rules for stochastic gradient descent with momentum and points out that Caffe implements one while PyTorch and TensorFlow implement the other. Those differ mathematically the moment your learning rate changes during training, which is to say, always.
[S] That last one is a lovely piece of pedantry with real teeth. Same hyperparameters does not mean same computation.
[O] What existed before this?

DAWNBench's flexibility, they write, also made it difficult to draw fair comparisons between hardware and software platforms.

[S] And the clock?
[G] It starts, quoting the timing rules, when the system touches any training or validation data, and it stops when the system achieves the defined quality target on the validation data set. Three things are carved out of that window. One-time system initialization, because at large scale that is a function of cluster administration rather than training capability. [O] The twenty-minute compile exclusion is interesting. Why cap it rather than exclude it entirely?
[G] Because MLPerf uses public data sets, which are much smaller than industrial ones, so a large distributed system can finish some of these benchmarks in minutes and compilation would swamp the measurement. The cap is a compromise. It stops compile time from dominating, but it also, in their words, discourages submissions with compilation approaches that are too computationally and operationally expensive to use in practice. And on the reformatting exclusion, they explicitly bar you from smuggling training-time augmentation into that free stage.
[S] What about the variance problem you raised earlier? A single run of a stochastic process is not a measurement.
[G] They require repeats, and the counts are empirically derived. Vision tasks require five runs, chosen to ensure ninety percent of entries from the same system are within five percent. [O] A trimmed mean. Sensible.

[O] Tell me about the thresholds themselves. Who picks seventy-four point nine?

[S] And the reference implementations. Are those the thing being timed?

[O] So how do submissions get compared without turning into a hyperparameter arms race?
[G] Divisions. The closed division requires equivalence to the reference across model, initialization, optimizer, schedule and data processing, with only a listed subset of hyperparameters tunable. The open division allows different model architectures, optimization procedures and data augmentations, to encourage innovative solutions and hardware-software co-design. Orthogonally there are three categories. Available, meaning you can genuinely buy it or rent it in a cloud, with versioned and supported software. Preview, meaning it meets those criteria within sixty days or by the next submission cycle. And research, for prototypes and for configurations larger in scale than anything available. There is also a system type label, on premises or cloud.
