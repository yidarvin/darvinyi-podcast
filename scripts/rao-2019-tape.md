[S] Noted. That is a thing I would like more benchmark papers to be forced to declare on the front page.
[O] Start with the gap, Tristan. Why does protein representation learning need a benchmark in twenty nineteen?
[G] Because unlabelled protein sequence had become nearly free and labels had not. The paper opens on exactly this. The total number of known sequences was doubling every two years. Pfam, the database they pretrain on, held thirty-one million protein domains. Meanwhile, determining a protein's structure experimentally means cryo-electron microscopy or X-ray crystallography, which version one's appendix says can take between weeks and years and cost over two hundred thousand dollars.
[S] That is the standard semi-supervised setup. Cheap unlabelled, expensive labelled.
[G] It is, and the authors say so directly. They frame the question as whether the NLP trick transfers. Proteins are chains of amino acids, discrete tokens in a sequence, so next-token prediction and masked-token prediction are both mechanically available. What is not obvious is whether the structure being learned resembles linguistic structure at all, because the thing generating protein sequences is evolution and biophysics, not grammar.
[O] But the real gap in the paper is not scientific, it is sociological.
[G] Correct, and this is the sharpest paragraph in the paper. Several groups had already built self-supervised protein embeddings. Bepler and Berger. Alley and colleagues with UniRep. Rives and colleagues. The complaint is precise: Rives and Bepler both report transfer results on secondary structure and contact prediction, but they, quote, differ significantly in test set creation and data-splitting strategies. Alley and Yang report protein engineering results, but on different tasks and different datasets. Nothing was comparable to anything.
[S] And the one shared benchmark that did exist?
[G] CASP, the structure prediction competition, packaged for machine learning as ProteinNet. The authors take contact prediction straight from it, and then say explicitly that structure prediction alone is not a sufficient benchmark for protein models. So they add tasks CASP does not cover.
[O] Walk us through the pretraining corpus and those splits.
[G] Pfam clusters its thirty-one million domains into evolutionarily related groups called families. The authors exploit that structure directly. They hold out entire families, about one percent of the data, as an out-of-distribution test set, and split the remainder ninety-five to five for an in-distribution random test set. Version one's appendix goes further and explains the clan holdout. A clan is a cluster of families that Pfam's maintainers group by shared function or evolutionary origin. They chose holdout clans and families in pairs, where a clan of novel function is held out together with a family that is similar in sequence but different evolutionarily or functionally.
[S] Which is a genuinely sharp design. You are testing whether the model can tell apart two things that look alike and are not related.
[G] Right. And here is a small thing I want on the record. In the camera-ready, the heldout-clans column exists in the table but is never explained in the prose. Section four point one describes only the family holdout, and the results text says they report metrics on the random split and the fully heldout families. The pointer to the appendix section that would explain clans points at an appendix the camera-ready does not contain.
[O] So the column is orphaned in the paper of record.
[G] Orphaned in the paper of record, explained only in the preprint that does not contain the column. That is a version-control failure rather than a scientific one, but it is real, and it is the kind of thing that only surfaces when you sit both documents side by side.
[S] Five tasks. Go.
[G] They group into three areas. Structure prediction is two tasks. Secondary structure is sequence tagging: every amino acid position gets one of three labels, helix, strand, or other, using the standard DSSP labelling system. The data are from Klausen and colleagues, filtered so no two proteins share more than twenty-five percent sequence identity, and the headline metric is per-amino-acid accuracy on the C B five thirteen test set.
[O] And sequence identity is what, exactly?
[G] The percentage of exactly matching positions between two aligned proteins. It is the field's standard proxy for these two are near-duplicates. Filtering the split at twenty-five percent means no test protein is closely related to anything in training. That is the leakage control, and the important thing is that it is baked into the split rather than reported after the fact.
[S] Second structure task.
[G] Contact prediction, and it is pairwise. For every pair of positions in the sequence, predict whether those two amino acids sit within eight angstroms of each other in the folded structure. The data are ProteinNet, which is itself built from CASP, filtered at thirty percent identity. The metric is precision on the L over five most confident predicted contacts, where L is the protein's length, and critically it is restricted to medium- and long-range pairs, on the CASP twelve test set. The paper is explicit that the medium and long range ones are what matter, because those are the contacts that constrain the global fold rather than just the local backbone.
[O] Then evolutionary understanding.
[G] Remote homology detection. Classify a protein into one of one thousand one hundred and ninety-five structural folds. The data are Hou and colleagues' splits, derived from SCOP, and the split holds out entire evolutionary groups, so at the hardest level the model must recognise a fold it has never seen a close relative of. The paper's framing is that homologous proteins can have very different sequences if they diverged in the distant past, so this measures detecting structural similarity across sequence dissimilarity.
[S] And the two engineering tasks, which I gather are the ones they actually built themselves.
[G] Both of them, and only those two. Those are the only splits TAPE constructs from scratch. Fluorescence is a regression: predict the log-fluorescence intensity of a green fluorescent protein variant, data from Sarkisyan and colleagues. Train on the Hamming-distance-three neighbourhood of the parent GFP, meaning every variant within three amino acid substitutions, and test on variants four or more mutations away. You train local and test further out.
[O] And stability inverts it.
[G] It inverts it deliberately. The paper's definition is unusual enough that I will keep their wording. The label measures the most extreme circumstances in which the protein maintains its fold above a concentration threshold, which they describe as a proxy for intrinsic stability. Data from Rocklin and colleagues. Train on a broad sample across four rounds of experimental design, then test on Hamming-distance-one neighbourhoods around the top candidates. Broad to narrow, where fluorescence was narrow to broad. Both scored by Spearman's rho.
[S] Why would anyone care about ranking single mutations?
[G] The paper's own answer is combinatorial. For a protein of length L, the number of sequences m mutations away is order L to the m, which is unsearchable experimentally even for modest m. And they flag epistasis, which is their word for second- and higher-order interactions between mutations at different positions. That is why greedy one-at-a-time optimisation is unlikely to work, and why a model that ranks variants well is directly useful to a protein designer.
[O] Models.
[G] Three trained from scratch. A twelve-layer Transformer, five hundred and twelve hidden units, eight attention heads, thirty-eight million parameters, trained with masked-token prediction. An LSTM in the ELMo style, two three-layer LSTMs with one thousand twenty-four hidden units running forward and backward and concatenated, trained with next-token prediction in both directions. And a dilated ResNet, thirty-five residual blocks, two hundred and fifty-six filters, kernel size nine, dilation rate two, masked-token prediction. All of them trained on Pfam for one week on four V one hundred GPUs.
[S] And the two prior models. Taken from their original papers, or retrained?
[G] Retrained here, which matters. Bepler and colleagues' structure-supervised bidirectional LSTM, and UniRep, which is Alley and colleagues' unidirectional multiplicative LSTM with nineteen hundred hidden units. Both re-pretrained on Pfam by the TAPE authors under the same self-supervised protocol. So those rows are not the numbers the original papers reported.
[O] And the baselines.
[G] Two, and they bracket the comparison. A one-hot encoding of the amino acid sequence as the floor. And as the ceiling, alignment-based features concatenated to that one-hot: hidden-Markov-model state-transition probabilities for secondary structure, position-specific scoring matrices for contact and homology. Note that the alignment features vary per task, picked to match whatever each task's own state of the art used. And they are not used for the two engineering tasks at all, for a reason the paper gives plainly: proteins there differ by a single amino acid, so an alignment search returns identical features for every one of them.

[S] Then let me put the leakage question. Thirty-one million Pfam sequences of pretraining. Was any of that filtered against the five downstream test sets?
