[S] It is also not one effect. That range is doing an enormous amount of work, and by the end of this I think you will agree that the top of it belongs to exactly one of the four tasks.
[O] I will grant you the spread. What I will not grant is that the spread makes the benchmark less useful. The spread is the finding.
[G] You are closer to each other than you sound. The same benchmark, with the same framework, reports a gain of one hundred sixty-nine percent on one task and under one percent on another. Both of those are in the paper.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio. Today we are on LongLaMP, a benchmark for personalized long-form text generation, from Ishita Kumar and colleagues at UMass Amherst, Adobe Research, and the University of Oregon, posted to arXiv in 2024.
[S] And with us is Verity, who has spent real time inside this one. Verity, welcome.
[G] Thank you. This is a benchmark I think is genuinely useful and frequently mis-cited, which is a combination I enjoy.
[O] Why this paper, today?
[S] Because personalization evaluation is one of the weakest corners of eval methodology, and this is the paper that tried to drag it from headlines to paragraphs.
[G] The reference point is LaMP, from Salemi and colleagues in 2023, which shares a senior author with this paper, Hamed Zamani. LaMP established the retrieval-augmented personalization setup and a benchmark to go with it, but almost all of its outputs are short: generate a news headline, generate a scholarly paper title, paraphrase a tweet. LongLaMP is explicitly the long-form extension of that line. Worth flagging that LaMP itself is not yet a node on the atlas, so if you know the short-form work, this is the sequel to it.
[O] And the argument for going long is basically that nobody's real use case is a headline.
[G] That is the paper's framing. The introduction argues the practically important cases — write the email, write the review, write the abstract — need paragraphs, and that long outputs raise failure modes a title-length task cannot expose at all. Holding a voice across sentences. Avoiding topic drift. Staying coherent over an extended passage.
[S] I want to flag the load-bearing assumption right there. Long-form is more useful is asserted, not measured. Nobody in this paper asks a human whether the long outputs are any good.
[G] Correct, and hold that, because it becomes the central weakness later.
[O] Verity, walk us through the benchmark itself.
[G] Four tasks, each from a different real corpus. LongLaMP-1 is personalized email completion: you get a subject line and part of an email body, and you complete it, conditioned on that user's previously written subject-and-email pairs. It is built on the Avocado Research Email Collection, a private corpus held under a confidentiality agreement. LongLaMP-2 is personalized abstract generation: given a title and keywords, write the abstract, conditioned on that author's previous abstracts, built from the Citation Network dataset, the AMiner data. LongLaMP-3 is personalized review writing: given a product description, the user's star rating, and a short summary of their experience, write the full review, from Amazon Reviews. LongLaMP-4 is personalized topic writing: given a post summary, write the Reddit post body, from the Reddit TL;DR corpus.
[S] And the splits. This is where I have seen people get it wrong.
[G] Two settings per task. The user setting holds out entire users, with no user appearing in both train and test, so it is a cold start for an unseen person. The temporal setting keeps the same users but orders their documents chronologically: the most recent document goes to test, the penultimate to validation, the third most recent to train, and everything earlier becomes the profile. One exception, and it matters. For topic writing, Reddit posts carry no reliable temporal signal, so that task's second split assigns documents randomly rather than chronologically. Table one in the paper labels it random, not temporal.
[O] So four tasks, eight task-and-setting combinations.
[G] Eight rows in table one, yes.
[S] How big are they?
[G] They vary a lot, and the variation matters. Email completion in the user setting is the smallest: three thousand two hundred eighty-six training examples, nine hundred fifty-eight validation, eight hundred twenty-three test, with an average output length of ninety-two point six tokens. Product review writing in the user setting is fourteen thousand seven hundred forty-five train and one thousand eight hundred twenty-two test, with an average output length of three hundred four point five tokens. So the target texts differ by more than a factor of three in length across the benchmark. The user pools differ too. The Avocado collection gave them two hundred seventy-nine users after filtering. Review writing filtered down to roughly twenty-four and a half thousand.
[O] And the method being evaluated on all that.
[G] Retrieval-augmented generation, deliberately not per-user fine-tuning. A query function turns the input into a retrieval query, a retriever pulls the top k entries from that user's own profile, and a prompt-construction function concatenates the task instruction, the input, and the retrieved entries into the final prompt. The paper's argument against per-user fine-tuning and latent user representations is compute cost, storage cost, and privacy risk.
[S] Which retrievers?
[G] The main tables — two, three, and four — use two. BM25, classical term matching, and Contriever, a pretrained dense retriever. And I want to be precise here, because this is the claim I most often see overstated. The paper does test more than two, just not in the main tables. Section four point four adds a Recency retriever for the temporal setting, which simply takes each user's most temporally recent profile entries. It also adds two random-retrieval baselines: Non-Personalized Random, which retrieves a random profile from the pooled profiles of all users, and Personalized Untuned, which retrieves randomly from the target user's own profile. Those results live in appendix tables eleven through fifteen, and they cover only part of the task-and-setting matrix.
[O] And the models?
[G] Three. Zero-shot with LLaMA-2 seven billion chat, zero-shot with GPT-3.5-turbo through the Azure OpenAI API, and fine-tuned with FLAN-T5 base. One important asymmetry: GPT-3.5 is never run on email completion at all. The paper says so directly in a footnote — because that task is built on a private dataset, they did not send it through an external API.
[S] That is a defensible choice and a real hole in the comparison at the same time.
[G] Both things are true. And the scoring is ROUGE one, ROUGE-L, and METEOR against the text the user actually wrote. That is the entire evaluation apparatus.
[O] Let us do numbers. Start with the headline.
[G] There are two headline framings, and they sit in different places in the paper, which matters for how people quote it. The introduction says the framework achieves an improvement between five point seven percent and one hundred twenty-eight percent across various metrics, against non-personalized baselines. The conclusion reports an average across all tasks of thirty point two one percent on ROUGE one and forty-seven point five percent on ROUGE-L. Neither of those is in the abstract. The abstract contains no numbers at all.
[S] Fine. Now the per-task picture.
[G] Take the zero-shot user setting, table two. Personalized email writing with LLaMA-2 and Contriever at k equals four: ROUGE one goes from point one seven seven three to point three four nine zero. ROUGE-L goes from point one one one one to point two nine nine three. METEOR from point one six zero five to point three four nine five. The gain table for that setting, table sixteen in the appendix, puts the ROUGE-L gain at one hundred sixty-nine point four percent. That is the largest gain anywhere in the paper's gain tables.
[O] And the smallest?
[G] Review writing. Same table, same setting: LLaMA-2 with Contriever at k equals two moves ROUGE one from point two seven six six to point two eight six six, which table sixteen calls a three point six two percent gain on that metric for that model. Now go to the temporal setting — that is table three, with gains in table seventeen — and LLaMA-2's ROUGE one gain on review writing is zero point two four percent, and its ROUGE-L gain is negative two point five eight percent.
[S] Negative. Personalization made it worse.
[G] On that cell, in that setting, for that model, yes, on a single run. And there are other negative cells. GPT-3.5 on topic writing loses two point zero eight percent of ROUGE-L in the user setting and three point five three percent in the temporal setting. LLaMA-2 on abstract generation loses three point four seven percent of METEOR in the temporal setting.
[O] Let me steelman before you pile on. Topic writing's METEOR gain in the user setting is twenty-eight point nine two percent across both models. Abstract generation's ROUGE-L gain in the user setting is twelve point zero nine percent. Those are real, and neither of them is email.
[G] They are real, and that is the fair reading. The ordering is consistent across both settings, though: email far out in front, abstract and topic writing solidly positive on most metrics, review writing hovering near zero.
[S] Verity, one thing bothers me about that range in the introduction. One hundred sixty-nine point four is larger than one hundred twenty-eight.
[G] It is, and that is a genuine oddity. I cannot find a cell equal to one hundred twenty-eight percent in tables sixteen, seventeen, or eighteen, and the maximum reported in those tables, one hundred sixty-nine point four percent, sits above the stated ceiling. The paper never says which numbers generate the range. My read, going a step beyond the text: the low end, five point seven, lines up neatly with topic writing's overall ROUGE-L gain in the user setting, five point six eight percent. The high end I cannot reconcile.
[O] So the headline range does not bracket their own best result.
[G] On the gain tables as printed, no.
[S] What happens under fine-tuning?
[G] The pattern narrows but survives. Table eighteen reports the FLAN-T5 base gains. Email completion in the user setting gains one hundred one point eight percent on ROUGE-L, and eighty-six point two percent in the temporal setting. Abstract generation in the user setting gains zero point four four percent on ROUGE one and zero point six seven percent on METEOR, and loses zero point zero eight percent on ROUGE-L. That is noise around zero.
[O] So a fine-tuned model already knows how to write an abstract, and the author's profile adds essentially nothing.
[G] That is the natural reading of that row.
[S] Now the retrieval-depth result, because I think that is the most practically useful thing in the paper.
[G] Agreed. More retrieved profile entries is not monotonically better. Section four point four reports that for topic writing, ROUGE scores increase from k equals zero to k equals one and then decline after. Abstract generation keeps climbing but with clear diminishing returns. Review writing benefits from more profiles in the user setting, but plateaus or declines past a certain count in the temporal setting. In the fine-tuned table four it is sharper still: abstract generation in the user setting sits at point three six six six with no personalization, around point three six eight at k equals two, and falls to point three two zero at k equals four with either retriever. That is well below not personalizing at all.
[O] So there is a real capacity cost to stuffing profile into the prompt.
[G] The paper links it to the lost-in-the-middle work from Liu and colleagues in 2023, and concludes that a one-size-fits-all profile count is ineffective. The best retriever flips too. Contriever is selected for abstract generation, topic writing, and email writing, but BM25 is the selected retriever for review writing in the temporal zero-shot setting, even though Contriever wins review writing in the user setting.
[S] Verity, the question I actually care about. Is there any evidence anywhere that these ROUGE gains correspond to a human noticing better personalization?
[G] No. There is no human evaluation in this paper. There is no LLM-as-a-judge check either. The limitations section concedes that the standard metrics have limitations in fully capturing the complexities of long text generation and personalization, and argues they are widely recognized and provide a consistent framework, but no validation study is run.
[S] On outputs averaging between ninety-two and three hundred four tokens.
[G] Correct.
[O] Let me make the optimist case properly, then. First, the gap is real. Before this, personalization eval was headlines and titles, and a headline cannot test voice consistency across paragraphs. Second, the design is careful in ways benchmarks often are not: four genuinely different domains, two complementary splits each, and the retriever and k selected on validation performance rather than test. Third, the private email corpus is a deliberate contamination defence, which is rare. And fourth, the negative cells are printed. They published the places where personalization hurts.
[S] I accept all four. Here is the deflationary case. The number people will carry away is thirty percent, or a hundred twenty-eight percent, and neither describes what happens on three of the four tasks. The largest gain comes from the one task whose non-personalized baseline is weakest by construction — a private corpus chosen precisely because the model has never seen it. A large relative jump off a floor is not the same phenomenon as personalization helping. And every table is one run. No seeds, no confidence intervals, no bootstrap. A zero point two four percent gain and a negative two point five eight percent loss are indistinguishable from decoding noise at temperature zero point eight.
[G] Let me score that. Point to the skeptic on variance. The experimental setup says, in one sentence, that all the results are reported on one run, and zero-shot decoding is nucleus sampling at temperature zero point eight. Small deltas are simply not interpretable under that. Point to the skeptic on the aggregate. The introduction's range and the conclusion's averages both collapse a spread that runs from one hundred sixty-nine percent down to negative three percent, and the per-task breakdown lives only in appendix tables.
[O] And on my side?
[G] Point to the optimist on the contamination design, with a caveat that cuts both ways. Using Avocado does defend against pretraining exposure, and the limitations section says that is exactly why they chose it. But it also means the email task is not directly comparable to the other three, which use public Amazon, Reddit, and AMiner data the models very likely saw. The paper's strongest contamination defence is the same design choice that makes its headline number unrepresentative.
[S] That is a nice trap to walk into.
[G] It is an honest one. And a second point to the optimist: the appendix baselines are more informative than their placement suggests. In table fourteen — user setting, LLaMA-2, k equals one — abstract generation scores point three five zero eight with no personalization, point three zero nine four when you retrieve a random profile from the pool of all users, point three one nine three when you retrieve randomly from the target user's own profile, and point three five one one with real retrieval. Random retrieval from other people actively hurts. That is evidence the retriever is doing something, not merely that longer prompts help.
[O] Although point three five one one against point three five zero eight is not exactly a triumph.
[G] No. That row is close to flat, and I would not sell it as more. The real abstract-generation gain in the main table comes at k equals four with GPT-3.5, not at k equals one with LLaMA-2.
[S] What would change my mind?
[G] Three things, and the paper does none of them. A human study, or at minimum a judge study, on a sample of generations, correlating ROUGE deltas with perceived personalization. Confidence intervals on the small gains. And a comparison against a published personalization-specific competitor. The related work discusses prompt rewriting from Li and colleagues, summarization plus retrieval from Richardson and colleagues, and reinforcement-learned retrievers from Salemi and colleagues, but none of them is run as a baseline here. The appendix does add two prompt-construction variants of the authors' own — a summarization-only prompt and a stylistic-extraction prompt — but those are their own ablations, on validation sets, with GPT-3.5.
[O] So where does this leave the field?
[G] With a usable, extensible benchmark and an unfinished measurement problem. The tasks and the splits are the durable contribution. The scoring is the part that has to be replaced.
[S] Which is the general lesson, and it is not specific to personalization. Every long-form generation benchmark hits the same wall. The thing you care about is not string overlap, and the thing you can compute cheaply is. Personalization sharpens it, because two texts can be equally in someone's voice and share almost no n-grams.
[O] And the counterpoint is that you cannot fix the metric before you have the tasks. Somebody has to build the corpora first. My read is that LongLaMP's value in five years is its four datasets and its two split designs, not any number in its tables.
[G] I would sign that, with one addition. If you are building a reward model or a judge for personalized writing, this benchmark hands you eight task-and-setting combinations with real ground truth written by real people. That is exactly the substrate a better metric needs. The paper did not build that metric. It built the place to test one.
[O] Verity, one sentence each to close. You first.
[G] The paper's takeaway: personalized long-form generation is a distinct problem from personalized short-form generation, retrieval-augmented personalization improves the aggregate on all four tasks, and how much it improves is strongly task-dependent and not monotonic in how much profile you retrieve.
