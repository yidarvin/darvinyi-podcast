[O] The abstract of this paper says it covers over two hundred fifty languages. The conclusion of the same paper says more than one thousand languages. Both sentences were written by the same authors about the same benchmark.
[S] And that is where I start, because if a benchmark cannot state its own headline breadth consistently, I am not sure what the headline is measuring.
[O] I read that as a units problem, not a credibility problem. There is a reading where both numbers are exactly right.
[G] There is, and the paper supplies it — in one sentence, buried in the related work section. But the resolution is not the one most people reach for, and getting it wrong is the single easiest mistake to make when citing this work.
[S] Welcome to Litsearch Audio. Today it is M-M-T-E-B, the Massive Multilingual Text Embedding Benchmark, from Kenneth Enevoldsen and a very large number of colleagues, published at I-C-L-R twenty twenty-five. Auden, thanks for coming in.
[G] Happy to be here. This is a paper I would describe as unusually honest about its methods and unusually careless about its own arithmetic, which is a strange combination to hold in your head at once.
[O] It is on the docket because it is one of the largest community-built benchmarks in the evaluation literature, and because the way it counts things is genuinely instructive. Auden, set the stage. What was wrong with what came before?
[G] The predecessor is M-T-E-B, from Muennighoff and colleagues in twenty twenty-three, which had already done the hard unifying work — one harness, eight task categories, and by this paper's own related-work description, fifty-eight tasks across one hundred twelve languages. Before that, every embedding paper chose its own slice of evaluations and nothing was comparable.
[S] So the harness existed. What was thin about it?
[G] Two things. First, the multilinguality was shallow in a specific way — this paper says M-T-E-B's non-English coverage came mainly from machine-translated tasks or from bitext mining, rather than from tasks natively written in those languages. Second, the eight categories did not cover things that had become live research areas by twenty twenty-four: instruction-following retrieval, long-document retrieval, code retrieval, reasoning as retrieval.
[O] Were there alternatives that went broader?
[G] Two narrower ones, and the paper names both. MIRACL covers eighteen languages, but monolingual retrieval only. MINERS reaches more than two hundred languages, but only classification and bitext mining. Neither has the task diversity of M-T-E-B. So there was a genuine hole: nothing had both the breadth of languages and the breadth of task types.
[S] And the second half of the problem is compute, which I actually think is the more interesting motivation.
[G] It is. The paper cites two figures for the cost of comprehensive evaluation. Evaluating a single seven-B language model on HELM consumes over four thousand G-P-U hours. And running a moderately sized model through the English M-T-E-B — which they rename M-T-E-B English version one — takes up to two days on a single A one hundred.
[O] And that cost lands hardest on exactly the people this benchmark is supposed to serve.
[G] Precisely, and the paper has a name for it, borrowed from Ahia and colleagues in twenty twenty-one — the low-resource double bind. The communities whose languages have the least benchmark coverage are also the ones with the least compute to run a big benchmark. So the goal here is two-sided and slightly contradictory on its face: make the benchmark much bigger, and simultaneously make it much cheaper to run.
[S] All right. How was it actually built? Because the authorship structure is the first thing you notice.
[G] It was coordinated entirely through GitHub, with an explicit point system for authorship credit, modeled on the SEA-Crowd effort from Lovenia and colleagues. Two points for implementing a task. Four points for each new language a dataset introduces. One point per full metadata annotation. Two to ten points for fixes. One point per model run. And two points for a pull-request review — with the detail I like: if a pull request had several reviewers, two points went to each of them, not two points split between them.
[O] That is a real design choice. It removes the incentive to be the only reviewer.
[G] It does, and the paper is candid that the review award was crude — it says two points was sometimes too low for a lengthy review and sometimes too high for a perfect pull request, but that on average they thought it was fair.
[S] And the byline that came out the other end?
[G] Eighty-seven names in the printed version of the P-D-F. And a small discrepancy worth knowing: arXiv's structured author metadata and Semantic Scholar's both list eighty-six. The name that is in the printed byline but missing from both indexing services is Saksham Thakur. Of the eighty-seven, eleven carry a double-dagger marking them as the managing team, and one carries the correspondence mark — Enevoldsen.
[O] So it is not eighty-seven equal co-authors, it is a coordinated core with a very wide contributor base.
[G] Right, and I think flattening that to eighty-seven authors misses what the paper is demonstrating. The point system is the contribution as much as the datasets are.
[S] Quality control. A benchmark assembled from hundreds of community pull requests is exactly where I expect the floor to fall out.
[G] They put a gate on it. Every submitted task needed at least one reviewer from the main contributors, and required metadata — annotation source, license, dialect, citation. Then a sanity check: two reference models, multilingual e-five small and MiniLM L twelve, had to be run on the task. A task got flagged for further scrutiny if either model landed within a two percent margin of a random baseline, or scored near-perfectly, or if the two models scored roughly the same as each other.
[O] Why is the two-models-agree case a red flag?
[G] Because these two models differ substantially in capability. If they cannot be separated on your task, the task probably is not measuring anything. And importantly, flagged did not mean rejected — the paper says a task could still be kept if a low score reflected genuine difficulty rather than a broken implementation.
[S] Good. Now the funnel, because the task counts are where this paper gets slippery.
[G] Three stages, applied identically to each derived benchmark. Initial scope: everything matching the benchmark's language and domain goal. Refined scope: drop machine-translated tasks, drop under-specified licenses, drop overly narrow domains like code retrieval. Then task selection and review. Table one gives all four benchmarks. M-T-E-B Multilingual goes from over five hundred tasks, to three hundred forty-three, to one hundred thirty-two. M-T-E-B Europe: four hundred twenty, to two hundred twenty-eight, to seventy-four. M-T-E-B Indic: fifty-five, to forty-four, to twenty-three. And M-T-E-B English version two: fifty-six, to fifty-four, to forty-one.
[O] That last stage is a big cut. What is the criterion?
[G] This is the cleverest mechanical idea in the paper. They treat task selection as feature reduction. For each held-out task, fit a linear regression on all the other tasks' scores to predict that task's score across models. The task whose score is most predictable from the others is the most redundant, so it gets dropped. Repeat. Stop when the most predictable remaining task falls below a Spearman correlation of point eight between predicted and observed — point nine for the stricter English version two.
[S] And the guardrail?
[G] They never remove a task if removing it would eliminate a whole language or a whole task category from the collection. So redundancy pruning cannot silently delete your only Amharic task.
[O] I want the efficiency work, because that is the half of the goal that sounds hardest.
[G] Three separate interventions. Clustering first. In M-T-E-B, clustering scores a v-measure over k-means on embeddings, repeated across multiple distinct sets, which means encoding a very large number of documents. Instead, they encode a four percent subsample of the corpus once, draw ten sets from it without replacement, and cluster each. For some tasks that cuts documents encoded by a factor of one hundred.
[S] And the validation, because a four percent subsample is a big claim.
[G] Nine English clustering tasks, thirteen models of various sizes. The appendix table gives an average speedup of sixteen point one one times, at an average Spearman rank correlation of point nine six three four between the fast rankings and the original rankings. And that average re-derives — the nine printed per-task speedups average to sixteen point one zero, which matches to rounding.
[O] Point nine six on model ranking for a sixteen-times speedup is a very good trade.
[G] It is. Second intervention: retrieval. They use TREC pooling — aggregate rankings from three models, B-M twenty-five, e-five multilingual large, and e-five Mistral Instruct seven B, and keep the top two hundred fifty documents per query. Then, for datasets with more than a thousand queries, sample down to a thousand queries. Two hundred fifty documents times one thousand queries gives a ceiling of two hundred fifty thousand documents, which is exactly the maximum the paper states, down from over five million in the largest original datasets.
[S] Why two hundred fifty documents specifically?
[G] This is the part I want to praise, because the paper shows its work and the answer is uncomfortable. They validated on two English retrieval sets with deliberately opposite judgment densities. Natural Questions is sparse — roughly one relevant document per query. TREC-COVID is dense — five hundred or more relevant documents per query. On Natural Questions the model ranking stays stable even down to one hard negative. On TREC-COVID, in the paper's own words, the ranking becomes unstable starting at one hundred hard negatives and keeps changing as you go lower.
[O] So two hundred fifty is a margin above where it visibly breaks.
[G] That is the reasoning, and they call the choice conservative. Third intervention is the cheapest and cleverest: bitext mining caching. Datasets like Flores share the same source sentences across every language pair — the English side appears in every English-to-X pair. Cache those embeddings once instead of re-encoding them per pair. That takes Flores' English side from four hundred ten thousand documents in M-T-E-B English version one down to one thousand and twelve.
[S] Right. Results. Who is being evaluated?
[G] Twelve embedding models, spanning twenty-two million parameters at the bottom — all-MiniLM L six — up to two seven-B models built on Mistral, Grit-L-M seven B and e-five Mistral seven B instruct. Everything zero-shot. Ranked by Borda count across tasks.
[O] Give me the headline.
[G] On M-T-E-B Multilingual, which is one hundred thirty-two tasks, the top model is multilingual e-five large instruct, with a Borda score of one thousand three hundred seventy-five and an average across all tasks of sixty-three point two. Grit-L-M seven B is second at one thousand two hundred fifty-eight and sixty point nine. E-five Mistral seven B instruct is third at one thousand two hundred thirty-three and sixty point three.
[S] And the parameter counts on those.
[G] Five hundred sixty million for the winner, roughly seven billion for the two behind it. So a model something like thirteen times smaller is beating both.
[O] This is the result I care about most, and I want to say why. The prevailing intuition in twenty twenty-four was that embedding quality was going to follow the same scaling story as generation — bolt an embedding head onto a bigger decoder and win. This says the axis that matters for multilingual embedding is what you pretrained on, not how big you are.
[S] I will grant the direction, but I want the other two benchmarks before I grant the framing.
[G] You should, because the ranking is not stable. On M-T-E-B Indic, twenty-three tasks, multilingual e-five large instruct wins again at seventy point two. But Grit-L-M and e-five Mistral fall all the way to fifth and sixth, at sixty point two and sixty point zero. That is a much bigger collapse than on the other benchmarks. And on M-T-E-B Europe, seventy-four tasks, the order flips — Grit-L-M seven B is first, Borda seven hundred fifty-seven, average sixty-three point zero, and multilingual e-five large instruct is second at seven hundred thirty-two and sixty-two point two.
[S] So the sentence "the small model wins" is benchmark-dependent.
[G] It is, and the paper's own explanation is the right one: the Mistral-based models have narrower, more European-centric pretraining, which disproportionately helps them on Europe's language set and hurts them on genuinely low-resource Indic languages. The appendix extends that — Grit-L-M is the top model on M-T-E-B Code, Borda eighty-eight, average seventy-three point six, against multilingual e-five large instruct's rank three at sixty-five point zero.
[O] So the honest claim is not "small beats large", it is "highly multilingual pretraining beats scale, on the language sets where the large models were not trained".
[G] That is the claim the tables support. One precision note, because it is easy to overstate. The paper says Grit-L-M beats multilingual e-five large instruct on Code and on English version two — true on both, sixty-six point four against sixty-five point two on the latter. But Grit-L-M is not the top model on English version two. E-five Mistral seven B instruct is, at Borda three hundred ninety-three. The paper's claim is correctly scoped to a pairwise comparison; a careless citation turns it into a leaderboard win.
[S] Now the English version two result, which is the efficiency payoff.
[G] They rerun M-T-E-B English version one's fifty-six tasks through the optimizations, drop tasks like M-S MARCO and Natural Questions because they are common embedding fine-tuning targets, and apply task selection. The rebuilt benchmark correlates with the original at a Spearman of point nine zero and a Pearson of point nine six, both with p below point zero zero zero one. And before task selection — just swapping in the optimized task variants — the correlation with the original is point nine seven Spearman, point nine nine Pearson.
[O] And the runtime.
[G] From up to two days for a moderately sized model, down to three point one one hours on an H one hundred for the seven-B model, and point eight one hours for all-MiniLM L twelve. Using, per the abstract, only two percent of the original documents and six percent of the original characters.
[S] Is there a systematic casualty?
[G] One, and the paper names it. The three smaller English models — all-MiniLM L twelve, all-MiniLM L six, all-mpnet-base — score worse on version two specifically because M-S MARCO and Natural Questions were removed, and those were their training targets. So part of what the old benchmark was measuring was familiarity.
[O] Which is an argument for the new benchmark, not against it.
[S] Agreed, and I will say that plainly — removing your community's two favourite fine-tuning targets to restore zero-shot status is the single most disciplined thing in this paper. Now let me spend my capital. Auden, the language count. Is this benchmark two hundred fifty languages or one thousand languages?
[G] Both numbers are in the paper, and they are attached to different things. The abstract, section one, and the limitations section all say over two hundred fifty languages. The conclusion says more than one thousand. The related work section is the only place that reconciles them, in a single parenthetical: the number of languages extends to over one thousand, two hundred fifty excluding bitext-mining tasks. And appendix D point two gives the precise total — one thousand fifty-one distinct I-S-O six three nine dash three codes.
[S] So the thousand is inflated by counting both sides of every bitext pair.
[G] No, and this is the wrong explanation that I want to kill on air, because it is intuitive and it is arithmetically impossible. Figure one credits bitext mining with one thousand forty-three languages across twenty-one tasks. Twenty-one tasks with two sides each would be forty-two. It is not pair doubling.
[O] Then where do a thousand languages come from?
[G] From individual datasets that are themselves massively multilingual. The clearest case is the Bible N-L-P bitext mining task, which lists dozens upon dozens of I-S-O codes — a-a-i, a-a-k, a-a-u, and so on — drawn from Bible-translation corpora. So the real arithmetic is subtraction of the paper's own two figures: one thousand fifty-one total, minus the two hundred fifty reachable outside bitext mining, leaves roughly eight hundred languages that enter this benchmark only through bitext-mining tasks.
[S] Then my objection changes shape, and I think it gets sharper. It is not that the number is fake. It is that for eight hundred of those languages you have exactly one task type — and bitext mining is about the shallowest signal an embedding benchmark offers. Aligning translation pairs tells you almost nothing about whether the model can cluster, classify, or retrieve in that language.
[G] That is the correct critique and I would score it fully to you. The count is real; the depth behind it is not uniform. Roughly two hundred fifty languages get cross-category coverage, and roughly eight hundred get one shallow task type.
[O] I will concede the depth point and still defend the headline. Eight hundred languages with a shallow eval is strictly more than eight hundred languages with no eval, and it is exactly the population the low-resource double bind is about. That is a first rung on a ladder.
[S] It is, as long as nobody reads "one thousand languages" as "one thousand languages evaluated the way English is". Auden, is there a place where the counts are cleanly stated?
[G] Not entirely, and this is where I turn on the paper. Figure one, which is the overview graphic, prints its own headline trio — one thousand ninety languages, five hundred fifty tasks, seventeen domains. The body text says over five hundred tasks, over two hundred fifty languages, ten task categories. The appendix says one thousand fifty-one language codes. Those are three different sets of numbers for the same benchmark in the same P-D-F.
[O] Is that drift or error? Because a live GitHub repository does keep growing.
[G] I think it is drift, and the paper half-acknowledges it — a footnote points readers to the live documentation for the current task list. But there is a subtler trap in that same figure. The ten per-category language counts sum to one thousand seven hundred seventy-nine, which is well past the figure's own one thousand ninety headline. So those per-category numbers are not additive components of the total. The same language appears under several categories.
[S] So if anyone adds up the boxes in that graphic, they get a number that is meaningless.
[G] Correct, and it is worth saying out loud because that graphic is the thing people screenshot.
[O] Are the task counts at least stable?
[G] They are not, in three separate places, and this is my main methodological complaint. English version two: table one says forty-one tasks, section two point four says forty-one, and section four says a reduction from fifty-six to forty. M-T-E-B Multilingual: section two point four says a final selection of one hundred thirty-one diverse tasks, while table one and the results table's own header both print one hundred thirty-two. And M-T-E-B Europe: appendix H point one says a final selection of ninety-six tasks, while table one and the results table both print seventy-four.
[S] Ninety-six against seventy-four is not a typo. That is a twenty-two task gap on a flagship benchmark.
[G] It is the largest of the three and I cannot account for it from the text. My read is that the prose in the appendix describes an earlier state of the selection pipeline and was not updated. But the paper does not say that, so a reader has to pick, and the tables are the more defensible pick — two independent tables agree on seventy-four.
[O] Let me try the steelman on the whole units problem, because I think there is one. This paper is fifty-seven pages, assembled from hundreds of community pull requests, over a live repository that kept growing during review. Every one of those inconsistencies is a count that moved between drafts. None of them is a result that moved.
[S] That is fair and I will take it partly. But there is a version of the same slippage in the expansion claim itself, and that one matters more. The like-for-like sentence in related work is that they expand the fifty-eight datasets to over four hundred datasets. That is datasets to datasets. The five hundred plus figure is tasks, which is a different unit — and the same related-work paragraph two sentences earlier describes the predecessor as fifty-eight tasks. The paper uses "fifty-eight tasks" and "fifty-eight datasets" for the same number.
[G] That is accurate, and it is the cleanest illustration of the whole problem. Languages, tasks, task categories, datasets, and models are five different units here, and this benchmark multiplies all of them. If you take one number away from this paper, take the datasets one — fifty-eight to over four hundred datasets — because that is the only comparison where both sides are the same unit.
[O] All right. My turn to press on something other than counting. Auden, how good is the efficiency validation?
[G] Thin outside English, and I think this is the sharpest technical criticism available. The clustering bootstrap — sixteen point one one times, Spearman point nine six three four — is validated on nine tasks, all English: Biorxiv, Medrxiv, Reddit, StackExchange, and TwentyNewsgroups. The retrieval downsampling is validated on exactly two English sets, Natural Questions and TREC-COVID. Neither technique is revalidated on a single non-English or non-Latin-script task before the same thresholds are applied uniformly across the whole benchmark.
[S] And they already know one of those two English tasks destabilises below one hundred documents.
[G] They do, and they say so. TREC-COVID is an unusually dense-judgment set, so it is a hard case rather than a typical one. But the argument that it is atypical does not rule out similar instability on some of the many low-resource retrieval tasks that get the identical two hundred fifty document cap.
[O] Is there any non-English check at all?
[G] One, and it is not for the speedups — it is for a construction method. For the new multilingual Wikipedia retrieval and reranking tasks, they generated synthetic queries with a G-P-T four class model, and compared against gold German question-answering data. Reported Spearman, point nine three. But the ninety-five percent confidence interval is point six nine to one point zero.
[S] That interval is enormous. Point six nine is a meaningfully different world from point nine three.
[G] It is, and it is consistent with a small evaluation sample. And it covers one of the seventeen languages that construction method was used to build tasks in.
[O] I will concede that one outright. Validating a multilingual benchmark's cost-cutting machinery almost entirely in English is the weakest part of the paper, and it is weak in exactly the direction that would embarrass the paper's own thesis.
[S] Which two limitations does the paper own itself?
[G] Both are stated plainly, which I credit. First, the language distribution is skewed toward high-resource languages. English backs two hundred ninety tasks by the figure six caption — and the appendix's own tally for English is three hundred three, which is another small internal disagreement, both numbers from the paper. The next highest are German at fifty-eight tasks and French at fifty-seven. That is a factor of five below English, with a long tail of the other listed languages in the single digits to a few dozen.
[O] So the top hundred languages in that appendix table are already the top hundred out of one thousand fifty-one.
[G] Exactly, and figure six removes English from the plot for readability, which visually flattens the very skew the limitation section is describing. Second limitation: they filter machine-translated datasets but permit human-translated ones, and they note this can leak English-centric labelling into supposedly non-English tasks. Their own example is a clustering task where labels derived from English samples transfer onto the translations.
[S] What about contamination proper?
[G] Not directly addressed. There is no train or pretraining leakage analysis. The one contamination-adjacent design decision is building English version two as zero-shot by removing M-S MARCO and Natural Questions. That is real, but it is one benchmark of the four.
[O] And prompting?
[G] Every model is run zero-shot with generic prompts per task category, not tuned per task. The paper itself notes this may understate what the instruction-tuned models can do. So the headline that a five hundred sixty million parameter model beats the seven-B models has an asterisk the authors put there themselves.
[S] Let me try to state where I land, because I have moved. My opening position was that a paper contradicting itself on its headline breadth cannot be trusted on breadth. I now think that is too strong. The contradictions are all in prose describing counts, and the counts have a coherent reconstruction from the tables and the appendix.
[O] And I have moved too. I came in wanting to say this is a thousand-language benchmark. It is not, in any sense a practitioner should use. It is a two-hundred-fifty-language benchmark with genuine cross-category depth, plus an eight-hundred-language bitext-mining frontier that is real coverage but one task type deep.
[G] I would accept both of those as fair readings. And to score the debate directly: the optimist wins on the efficiency contributions, which are quantified rather than asserted — every shortcut in this paper comes with a correlation number attached, which is rarer than it should be. The skeptic wins on validation scope, because that quantification is almost entirely English. And they split the language count — the number is honest, the depth behind it is not uniform, and the paper communicates it badly.
[O] Auden, what should someone take as genuinely new here versus inherited from M-T-E-B?
[G] Inherited: the harness, the leaderboard, and eight of the ten task categories. New: the point-based community construction and the authorship system that goes with it, the scale — fifty-eight datasets to over four hundred datasets — two new task categories in multi-label classification and instruction retrieval, the regional benchmarks for Europe and Indic languages, the redundancy-based task selection procedure, and the three efficiency methods with their reported correlations. The finding about model size is a result of the benchmark, not a property of it.
[S] And what would raise your confidence?
[G] Three things, none of which require new science. Rerun the clustering and retrieval downsampling validation on at least one non-Latin-script task per category. Report the per-language task distribution against the headline rather than only the top hundred. And reconcile the figure's numbers with the body text explicitly instead of pointing at a live repository.
[O] Takeaway from me: this is the strongest available evidence that multilingual pretraining, not parameter count, is what determines embedding quality outside English — and the efficiency work makes that finding reproducible by people without an H one hundred farm, which is the actual contribution.
[S] Mine: cite the datasets number, fifty-eight to over four hundred, and never cite a language count from this paper without saying which task types back it. Two hundred fifty languages have depth, roughly eight hundred more have bitext mining, and the paper's own abstract and conclusion will each hand you a different number.
[G] And the paper's own: a large, reviewed, community-built benchmark can be made both broader and dramatically cheaper at once — the rebuilt English benchmark reproduces the original's model ranking at a Spearman of point nine zero using two percent of the documents — but the cost-cutting machinery in this particular paper was validated almost entirely in English before being applied to everything else.
