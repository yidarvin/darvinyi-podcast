[S] Oh, we are absolutely coming back to that. But set the scene first. 2021. Where was factuality evaluation?
[G] Fragmented. There was a small pile of purpose-built factuality metrics — FactCC, DAE, FEQA, QAGS — all proposed within about a year of each other, and each evaluated on its own private slice of data with its own protocol. None of them could be compared to any other, because no shared benchmark existed to compare them on.
[O] And all of them treated factuality as a binary. Factual, or not factual.
[G] That is the second problem, and the authors argue it is the deeper one. A binary label throws away exactly what a system builder needs, which is which kind of mistake the model made and how many. And it rests on a judgment humans are demonstrably bad at making consistently. Falke and colleagues, in 2019, found crowd workers agreed with expert factuality labels at a kappa of about point six five, and at most point seven four even when they put more than nine workers on each item.
[O] So the move is: do not collect a bigger binary dataset. Change the question you ask the annotator.
[G] Exactly that. The claim is that if you decompose "is this factual" into linguistically motivated subcategories, the final binary decision becomes more objective as a byproduct. Better-defined subquestions, better agreement.
[S] Give me the typology. Concretely.
[G] Three levels. The first is semantic frame errors, grounded in frame semantics — a predicate plus its arguments. A predicate error is where the relation itself is wrong: the vaccine was rejected by the regulator when the article says approved. An entity error is where a core argument is wrong or swapped — the right relation, the wrong entity, and that includes agent-patient reversals. A circumstance error is a non-core modifier: right relation, right entities, wrong time or place.
[O] Frame, core slot, non-core slot. That is clean.
[G] The second level is discourse errors, and this level only exists because summaries have more than one sentence. A coreference error is a pronoun with a wrong antecedent, or no antecedent at all. A discourse link error is where the temporal or causal connector between two statements is wrong — both statements can be individually true and the link between them still false.
[S] And the third level?
[G] Content verifiability errors. Out of article error, which is information simply not present in the source — what Maynez and colleagues call extrinsic hallucination. And grammatical error, where the sentence is broken enough that you cannot check it against anything at all. Then two catch-alls: other, and not an error.
[O] What is the evidence the typology is complete rather than just convenient?
[G] Weak but real: the "other" bucket absorbs about one percent of errors overall. That is the authors' own completeness argument, and it is the right test to run. Though it only tells you annotators could always find a box, not that the boxes carve at the joints.
[S] Now the data collection, because this is a meta-evaluation benchmark. If the human labels are noise, everything downstream is noise.
[G] Two hundred and fifty articles per dataset, on CNN Daily Mail and X-Sum. They pulled publicly available outputs from five CNN Daily Mail systems — an LSTM sequence-to-sequence model, a pointer-generator network, Bottom-Up Summarization, BERTSum, and BART — and four X-Sum systems, including a topic-aware convolutional model and a BERT-initialized encoder-decoder. That is one thousand two hundred and fifty CNN Daily Mail outputs and one thousand X-Sum outputs. Two thousand two hundred and fifty summaries in total, four thousand nine hundred and forty-two annotated sentences, three independent annotators on every sentence.
[O] Sentence level, not summary level.
[G] Sentence level, but each sentence is shown inside the full summary, so a discourse error spanning two sentences is still visible. And a sentence can carry more than one category.
[S] What stops this from being the usual crowdsourcing mush?
[G] The quality engineering, which is genuinely the strongest part of the paper. A qualification test with an eighty-five percent bar before you can annotate at all. Continuous evaluation against artificially inserted errors — negated predicates, swapped pronouns, a sentence lifted from a different article, scrambled word order, one for each error family they cared about — with workers blocked below fifty percent on those checks. A per-article probe asking the worker to pick out an entity that was not in the article, to confirm they actually read it. And pay fifty percent above the American minimum wage, plus quality bonuses. They report the bonuses alone dropped the blocked-worker rate from about thirty percent to under ten.
[O] And the agreement numbers?
[G] Here you have to be careful, because three different numbers get quoted as though they were one. On the full four thousand nine hundred and forty-two sentences, the binary factual-or-not judgment gets a Fleiss kappa of point five eight, with ninety-one percent of annotators agreeing with the majority. On the finer eight-category split, restricted to sentences all three agreed were non-factual, kappa is point three nine. The paper calls that moderate, and it is.
[S] And the point eight six that everybody cites?
[G] That is a different measurement entirely: crowd majority against a single domain expert, on two hundred and one datapoints, drawn from ten CNN Daily Mail summaries and ten X-Sum summaries. Cohen's kappa of point eight six. It is a real result, and it does beat the point six five from Falke and colleagues. But it is a spot check on a twenty-summary slice against one expert, not a large-sample estimate of expert agreement.
[S] So the headline validation number is computed on about four percent of the sentences, against a single rater.
[G] Yes. I would call it a quality audit rather than a validation study.
[O] Let us get to the metric benchmark, because that is the half people actually cite.
[G] Nine metrics across four families. N-gram: BLEU, ROUGE one, two and L, and METEOR. Embedding: BERTScore precision, recall and F one, computed between the summary and the article, not the reference summary. A simple Open Information Extraction triple-matching baseline. Two entailment metrics, FactCC and DAE. And two question-generation-and-answering metrics, FEQA and QAGS.
[S] And they do not report a plain correlation with the human labels.
[G] No, and this is the methodological move I would most want people to copy. They report partial correlations, controlling for which system-and-dataset pair a summary came from. Figure 3 is the argument. Fit one regression line across all the data for FEQA and FactCC and it looks steep. Fit a separate line inside each model-and-dataset partition, and for FEQA those lines are nearly flat.
[O] Because the pooled line is really measuring "this summary came from a weak model," not "this summary is unfaithful."
[G] Precisely. System identity predicts both the metric score and the human score, so pooling manufactures correlation. They regress that categorical variable out of both sides and correlate the residuals instead.
[S] I want to push on that. Is it possible they over-corrected? If a metric is genuinely good at ranking systems, then partialling out system identity throws away the signal you actually wanted.
[G] That is a fair challenge and the paper does not answer it. It never reports the unpartialled correlations alongside the partial ones, so a reader cannot see how much the correction moves any metric's ranking, or whether any pairwise ordering would flip under the naive statistic. And their significance testing, the Hotelling-Williams comparisons in Table 4, is computed on the partial correlations as well — so it compounds the choice rather than checking it.
[O] Small note for anyone reimplementing this: the appendix equation for the partial correlation has a typo. It defines the human residual using the metric's scores rather than the human scores. Obvious from context, but it will bite you.
[S] Numbers. Which systems are actually factual?
[G] Pooled across both datasets, sixty percent of the two thousand two hundred and fifty summaries contain at least one factual error. But the pooled number hides the real story, which is that the two datasets barely belong in the same table. At the dataset level it is forty-three percent of CNN Daily Mail summaries wrong, versus ninety-two percent of X-Sum summaries.

[S] What about the dataset split? I suspect that is where the ranking falls apart.
[G] It does. FactCC gets point three six on CNN Daily Mail and point zero seven on X-Sum. DAE, point two five and point zero four. QAGS, point one three and negative point zero two. [O] Is that the only explanation available?
[G] No, and this is a genuine gap. FactCC is trained on CNN Daily Mail itself, augmented with four kinds of artificially inserted mistakes. [S] And DAE?
[G] DAE has its own asterisk. The appendix says it was trained with a maximum length of one hundred and twenty-eight tokens, after concatenating the article and the summary. [O] Meanwhile the n-gram metrics do what on X-Sum?

[O] The ablation is my favourite part of this paper. Walk us through it.
[G] They drop each broad error category from the evaluation set in turn and re-measure the correlation. A bigger drop means that category was carrying more of that metric's signal. And each metric turns out to have a distinct fingerprint. ROUGE L's correlation comes almost entirely from content verifiability errors — it can spot spans that are missing or added, and it has negative correlation with semantic frame errors, because swapping two entities barely changes n-gram overlap at all.
[O] So ROUGE is not measuring factuality, it is measuring whether text appeared from nowhere.
[G] That is a fair reading. The Open Information Extraction baseline is comparatively better on semantic frame errors, which makes sense since it explicitly matches subject-relation-object triples. BERTScore precision's tallest bar is content verifiability and its smallest is semantic frame — so its point two seven overall is mostly about catching content that cannot be checked against the source, not about judging whether a predicate is right.
[S] And the entailment metrics?
[G] FactCC is the best of all metrics tested on semantic frame errors, and nearly uncorrelated with discourse errors. The paper's explanation is that FactCC's synthetic training perturbations never included a cross-sentence error, so it never learned to look for one. DAE has the highest correlation with discourse errors of any metric tested, which the paper attributes to entailment operating at the dependency level. And both question-answering metrics are negatively correlated with discourse errors.
[O] That is the actionable finding for me. Factuality is not a scalar skill. These things are detectors with different receptive fields, and you should choose one based on which errors your model actually makes.
[S] Which you can only know if you already have annotations like these. Circular, but usefully circular.
[G] There is one more analysis worth knowing about, on annotator confusion. They looked at sentences where two annotators agreed on a category and the third picked something else. Most categories get confused with "not an error," which the authors read as ordinary annotation noise. But coreference error is a dramatic outlier: when coreference error is the majority label, the dissenting annotator says "not an error" sixty-nine point seven percent of the time.
[O] Why?
[G] The authors' explanation, and I find it convincing, is a specific cognitive blind spot. You have just read the source article. The summary uses a pronoun with no antecedent anywhere in the summary. Your brain silently supplies the antecedent from the article you just read, and you never notice it was missing. They say it persisted even after they called it out explicitly in the instructions.
[S] So the category with the highest annotator failure rate is also the one that only shows up on CNN Daily Mail — and it is thirty-nine percent of the pointer-generator's errors and thirty-three percent of BART's.
[G] Yes. Which means those coreference numbers are probably undercounts.
[O] Let me make the optimist case, and it is not about the numbers at all. FRANK's contribution is procedural. It said: stop asking humans a question they answer inconsistently, decompose it into questions they answer well, and the aggregate judgment gets better for free. And it said: stop evaluating your metric on your own private slice with your own protocol. That template is why fine-grained factuality evaluation exists downstream — BEGIN and FActScore both build on meta-evaluating metrics against fine-grained human labels rather than chasing a leaderboard number.
[S] And my deflationary case is that the specific results have a shelf life measured in about eighteen months. Every model in this pool — sequence-to-sequence from 2015, pointer-generator from 2017, through BART in 2020 — is an encoder-decoder summarizer from an era that has ended. None of them resemble an instruction-tuned decoder-only model. The error distribution FRANK measured is that generation's failure modes, not a law of nature.
[G] I would score the typology to the optimist and the correlation table to the skeptic, and I think the paper would agree with both. The typology has generalized. The numbers describe one dated model pool. What I would resist is the intermediate claim, which people do make — that FRANK establishes a ceiling on how well any automatic factuality metric can work. It establishes that these nine, on those nine systems, all correlated weakly.
[O] I will also concede the skeptic's strongest point, which is that the paper's own error analysis undercuts its own top metrics. FactCC goes from about point four four on non-pretrained down to point one six on pretrained.
[S] Which is the worst possible direction for that trend. The metric works best on the models you have stopped using.

[S] So what changes if you take this seriously in 2026?
[G] Three things on my read. First, the meta-evaluation stance itself. If you are proposing an evaluator — a factuality metric, an LLM judge, a reward model — then "how well does it correlate with a human, on a shared dataset, controlling for which system produced the output" is the right question, and it is still badly under-asked.
[S] The confound point especially. Modern judge evaluations pool across model families constantly, and if your judge partly recognizes which model wrote the text, your pooled correlation is inflated for exactly the reason Figure 3 shows.
[G] Second, the fingerprint result. If your evaluator's correlation comes almost entirely from one error family, it will fail silently on a distribution where a different family dominates. That is precisely the CNN Daily Mail to X-Sum collapse, and there is no reason to think a modern judge is immune to it.
[O] And third, for me, the annotation design. The qualification test, the continuous adversarial checks, the entity probe, paying people properly. Those bought a workable dataset in a task where the field's prior attempts landed at kappa point six five. That is transferable to every human-preference collection anyone is running right now.
[S] With the caveat that even their well-engineered protocol got a Fleiss kappa of point five eight on the binary question. Well-engineered bought them moderate agreement, not good agreement. Nobody should read this paper and conclude the human label problem is solved.
