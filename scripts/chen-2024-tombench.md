[S] Now take the same model and the same false belief stories, and require that every question attached to a given story be answered correctly before that story counts as passed. Ninety point eight becomes fifty nine point zero.
[O] That is a large drop and it is not obviously a damning one. Requiring several coin flips to all land heads will crater any accuracy number you like.
[S] Here is why I do not think it is only arithmetic. The humans took the same hit on that task and landed at exactly fifty nine point zero as well. The drop is real, it is shared, and I want to know which part of it is the test being hard and which part is the scoring being multiplicative.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio, where an optimist, a skeptic, and a visiting scholar take one paper apart. Our guest today is Sylvie.
[S] The paper is ToMBench, benchmarking theory of mind in large language models. Zhuang Chen, Jincenzi Wu, Jinfeng Zhou, Bosi Wen and colleagues, out of the CoAI group at Tsinghua, with collaborators at Chinese University of Hong Kong, Tianjin, Nankai and elsewhere. ACL twenty twenty four, with a revised version posted in December of that year.
[O] Sylvie, welcome. We have covered ToMi on this show, and FANToM, and AGENT, and MMToM-QA. That is four theory of mind benchmarks already. Make the case that this one needed to exist.
[G] The case is coverage plus provenance. ToMi is templated false belief question answering over short narratives, so it probes belief tracking and essentially nothing else. FANToM puts the test inside a multiparty conversation with information asymmetry, which is a different and sharper instrument, but again it is centered on what an absent character missed. AGENT is not language at all, it is animated trajectories scored by violation of expectation. MMToM-QA is multimodal, video plus text. ToMBench is text and multiple choice like ToMi, but it is trying to span the whole psychological construct rather than one slice of it, and it is built so that nothing in it existed before the models did.
[S] Start with the failure it is reacting to, because the literature it enters is a mess.
[G] It is a genuine contradiction, and the paper lays it out in the introduction. Kosinski, twenty twenty three, reported that instruction-tuned models performed comparably to children aged seven and ten on false belief tests, on forty samples. Bubeck and colleagues found GPT-4 doing impressively on the Sally-Anne test, on ten samples. Van Duijn and colleagues found GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 beating students aged seven to ten on early inventories.
[O] And then the counterpunch.
[G] Ullman, twenty twenty three, and Shapira and colleagues showed that trivial alterations to those same test items collapse the performance. The paper names the phenomenon: Clever Hans. The model is riding a spurious correlation rather than tracking a belief.
[S] So the field has two camps with incompatible headlines, and the paper's diagnosis is that the evaluations themselves are broken.
[G] Three specific ways. Constrained scope, subjective judgment, and unintended contamination. Scope is that existing evaluations each probe one or two dimensions in isolation. Subjective judgment is that the classic psychometric inventories use open-ended questions requiring expert scoring, which does not scale to benchmarking ten models and introduces annotator inconsistency.
[O] And the third is the one that actually indicts everybody.
[G] It is, and the paper does something I appreciate: it demonstrates it rather than asserting it. Appendix A, Table four. They prompt two models with, quote, give a Sally-Anne test example and its answer, with no context at all. GPT-3.5 Turbo oh-six-one-three returns Sally putting a marble in her basket, leaving, Anne moving it to her own box, and the question of where Sally will look. GPT-4 oh-six-one-three returns essentially the same scenario with the same answer.
[S] Which is exactly what you would expect from a psychology instrument published in nineteen eighty three and reproduced in every textbook since.
[G] Right. The inventories predate the pretraining corpora by decades. So a benchmark assembled by recombining published ToM inventories is running an open-book exam and calling it a closed-book one.
[O] So the response is to write everything from scratch. Walk us through the framework, because there are two views of the same corpus and that is the part people get wrong when they cite this.
[G] Two orthogonal views over one inventory. The task view groups items into eight theory of mind tasks taken from the psychology literature, each with its own citation. Unexpected outcome test, scalar implicature task, persuasion story task, false belief task, ambiguous story task, hinting test, strange story task, and faux-pas recognition test. The false belief task traces to Wimmer and Perner, nineteen eighty three. The faux-pas test to Baron-Cohen and colleagues, nineteen ninety nine.
[S] And the ability view.
[G] The ability view regroups the same construct into thirty one fine-grained abilities across six dimensions, adapted from a framework called ATOMS, from Beaudoin and colleagues, twenty twenty. ATOMS was built to catalogue theory of mind measures for young children. It originally has seven dimensions and thirty nine abilities. The authors drop the Percept dimension because it requires visual cues, and drop some mixed abilities, leaving six dimensions and thirty one abilities. Emotion has seven, desire four, intention four, knowledge four, belief six, and non-literal communication six.
[O] Why bother with both views? That sounds like reporting the same numbers twice.
[G] Because they do not line up, and the mismatch is informative. The eight tasks between them only touch nineteen of the thirty one abilities. So the authors go back to the original psychology literature and write extra standalone items for the remaining twelve, purely to complete ability coverage. Knowledge-pretend play links, for instance, has no dedicated task behind it. That ability exists in the benchmark only because they wrote items for it directly.
[S] Give me the item format and the construction procedure, because from-scratch is a claim about labor.
[G] Every item is a story, a question, and options. Stories are original social vignettes, inspired by posts on Reddit, Twitter, Zhihu and Weibo, spanning nine primary topics — family, workplace, school, neighborhood, recreation, friendship, romantic, technology, civic — with dozens of subtopics under each. Questions are either yes-no or four-option. The wrong options are deliberately written to be plausible rather than easily dismissed.
[O] And the workers are the authors themselves.
[G] All of them trained by psychology experts first, and all of them having reviewed the underlying literature. Then two rounds of validation. In round one, worker A completes every sample created by worker B, and disagreements get discussed and revised. In round two, anything still unresolved goes to a third worker C. Final average agreement, ninety nine point four percent.
[S] I want to be precise about what that ninety nine point four measures, because it gets quoted loosely.
[G] Good instinct. It is agreement among the item constructors about the ground truth answer. It is not a measure of how consistently the human test takers answered. Those are different quantities and the paper reports only the first.
[O] Sizes.
[G] Twenty four hundred seventy samples from the eight-task construction phase, over nine hundred thirty four stories, with at least one hundred samples per task and at least twenty per ability within a task. Then the extra items for the twelve uncovered abilities bring the total to two thousand eight hundred sixty questions. That total is the headline number for the benchmark.
[S] And the ability view reports fifteen hundred eighty four stories against those twenty eight sixty questions, which is more stories than the task view has but fewer questions per story. That does not obviously reconcile.
[G] It does not, and the paper does not spell it out. My read, going beyond the text, is that the ability-view story count is summed per dimension, so a story that serves both an emotion item and a belief item is counted under both. The per-dimension rows do add to fifteen eighty four exactly, and there is no other arithmetic I can make work. Worth flagging rather than quoting that number as unique stories.
[O] And the bilingual half.
[G] Written in Chinese first, then translated to English with GPT-4 oh-six-one-three, with the correct answer withheld from the translation prompt so the translation step cannot leak the label, then manually checked. And the story lengths are not symmetric. Across the task view, average story length is sixty one point two words in English against ninety seven point seven in Chinese. On the ambiguous story task specifically, one hundred two point six in English against one hundred sixty four point one in Chinese.
[S] So the two language halves are not calibrated to identical difficulty. They are a source and a translation.
[G] Correct, and the paper's own significance testing supports treating them separately. They run a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, find neither view's results are normally distributed, then use a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the language difference. P equals zero point zero one nine for the task view and zero point zero one six for the ability view. Both under zero point zero five. The averages look similar across languages, but the difference is statistically significant.
[O] Take us to the evaluation setup and then the numbers.
[G] Ten models. GPT-4 eleven-oh-six, GPT-4 oh-six-one-three, GPT-3.5 Turbo eleven-oh-six, GPT-3.5 Turbo oh-six-one-three, Qwen fourteen B Chat, ChatGLM3 six B, LLaMA-2 thirteen B Chat, Baichuan2 thirteen B Chat, Mistral seven B version zero point two, and Mixtral eight by seven B Instruct. Two prompting conditions, vanilla and chain of thought. Accuracy is the metric.
[S] Option order bias?
[G] Handled, and asymmetrically. For every model except the GPT-4 pair, they shuffle the option order five times and take the most frequently selected option. For the two GPT-4 models, pilot experiments showed very consistent answers across orders, so those are single-shot.
[S] That asymmetry is defensible and I would still like to have seen the pilot numbers.
[G] They are not reported. The human baseline is twenty native Chinese-speaking graduate students, each paid fifteen dollars, completing the Chinese half with no tutorials or examples. That single Chinese score is then reused as the human baseline for the English results, justified by citing Bradford and colleagues, twenty eighteen, that native-English and native-Chinese speakers do not differ significantly on theory of mind tasks.
[O] Give me the headline.
[G] Two headlines, and they come from different models under different conditions, so keep them separate. In the task view, the smallest human-model gap is ten point one points: humans at eighty five point four average, against GPT-4 eleven-oh-six at seventy five point three in Chinese. In the ability view the smallest gap is ten point eight points: humans at eighty six point one, against GPT-4 oh-six-one-three with chain of thought at seventy five point three in Chinese.
[S] So the abstract's "over ten percent points" is two different comparisons stapled together.
[G] It is two comparisons that happen to agree in direction and rough magnitude. Which is a reasonable thing to summarize, but you should not attribute both to one model's line.
[O] What about the ordering below GPT-4?
[G] The GPT-4 pair is clearly the top tier. The paper says it clears the GPT-3.5 Turbo tier by more than ten points, and on most cells that holds comfortably. Effectively matched.
[S] The claim I have seen repeated from this paper is that the mixture-of-experts model loses to the smaller dense one. That is an interesting claim about scale and I want it checked.
[G] Then let me be careful, because the paper's sentence and the popular paraphrase are not the same sentence. On the task view average, Mixtral leads in both languages — fifty five point one against forty seven point five in Chinese, fifty three point three against fifty one point five in English. On the ability view Chinese average, Mixtral leads again, fifty two point five against forty five point eight. Mistral leads in exactly one of the four average columns: ability view English, fifty one point three against fifty point eight.
[O] So one cell out of four.
[G] One cell out of four, and the paper builds a claim about mixture-of-experts and theory of mind on top of it without reconciling the other three. That is the weakest inference in the results section and it is quotable enough that it has traveled.
[S] Noted and flagged. What about the task that inverts the whole story?
[G] The false belief task. GPT-4 eleven-oh-six scores ninety point eight in Chinese and eighty eight point two in English, both above the human eighty six point eight. The authors do not read that as human-level belief tracking. They read it as structure. False belief stories are heavily templated — someone moves an object while someone else is not looking, then you ask about the second person's belief — and existing inventories contain many of them. It generalizes well after training on the pattern.
[O] Which is the same argument the paper makes against contamination, applied to itself.
[G] It is, and consistently. Their line is that surpassing humans on a specific task is normal and should not license a conclusion about general theory of mind. The criterion they propose is robustness across tasks and abilities.
[S] Where is it worst?
[G] Scalar implicature, by grand mean across all ten models: thirty six point four in Chinese and thirty five point two in English. That task requires reading a quantifier and doing arithmetic on it — the example in the paper is a football team of eighteen players with almost one third as goalkeepers, how many goalkeepers. Pragmatics plus mathematics, a known weak spot. Humans are also relatively weak there, at seventy five point five, their second-lowest task.
[O] And their lowest?
[G] Persuasion story, at seventy point zero. Which is worth noting, because GPT-4 eleven-oh-six gets sixty one point zero Chinese and sixty five point zero English there. That is one of the narrowest human-model gaps on the board, and it comes from humans doing badly rather than models doing well.
[S] Give me the ability-dimension picture.
[G] Knowledge is far and away the bottom. Grand mean thirty four point four Chinese, thirty four point zero English, against a human eighty two point two. And knowledge here does not mean the model's own world knowledge. It tests whether the model understands how information propagates between characters — who could have learned what. ChatGLM3 six B scores sixteen point eight in Chinese on that dimension.
[O] That is under a four-option guessing floor.
[G] It is under twenty five, yes, but I would hedge the phrasing. The dimension mixes four-option and yes-no items and the paper does not break out the option counts per dimension, so I would not state a clean chance baseline. What I will say is that it is far below anything you would call competence. The strongest dimensions are non-literal communication and emotion, which track the easier tasks.
[S] Chain of thought. Everyone assumes it helps.
[G] The paper's summary sentence is that chain of thought almost never improves theory of mind performance and can even cause declines. The task view supports that plainly — the ten-model grand mean falls from fifty six point nine to fifty four point eight in Chinese and from fifty five point eight to fifty three point seven in English. LLaMA-2 thirteen B Chat drops from forty one point zero to thirty nine point eight in Chinese.
[O] And the ability view?
[G] The ability view is essentially flat, and slightly positive: fifty five point three to fifty five point seven in Chinese, fifty four point four to fifty four point six in English. So "almost never improves" is a fair reading of the task view and a slight overstatement of the ability view.
[S] Is anything hiding under that flat average?
[G] Yes, and the paper does not dwell on it. Knowledge, the worst dimension, improves substantially with chain of thought — thirty four point four to forty point seven in Chinese, thirty four point zero to forty point eight in English. Roughly six points on the dimension where models are weakest. The authors' explanation for why chain of thought generally fails is that decomposition only helps if the reasoning it decomposes into is itself sound. That is a reasonable story, and it does not explain the knowledge result.
[O] Then let us get to the piece that I think is the actual paper. The coherent test.
[G] The paper's phrasing is that for all tasks except persuasion story, most stories are associated with multiple questions probing different facets of the same scenario. So the authors add a story-level scoring rule: a model fails a story if it answers any question about that story incorrectly. They credit Kim and colleagues, twenty twenty three, for the idea, which is the FANToM lineage.
[S] And the result.
[G] Under coherent scoring, every one of the ten models drops by at least twenty points on the averaged figures. GPT-4 eleven-oh-six drops the least among models, twenty point six. GPT-3.5 Turbo oh-six-one-three drops the most, twenty seven point one. Humans drop thirteen point six. And the top-model-to-human gap widens from ten point one points to sixteen point two.
[O] Sixteen point two being what against what, precisely.
[G] Chinese coherent scores. Humans at seventy one point eight, GPT-4 eleven-oh-six at fifty five point six.
[S] This is the number I want to stress-test, because a story with four questions is scored under a product and a story with one question is not. That is a scoring artifact, not a finding.
[G] That objection is correct in general and the per-task breakdown is how you answer it. On persuasion story, which has exactly one question per story, the drop is zero point zero for every subject including humans. On the hinting test, ninety three stories and one hundred three questions, so nearly one question each, GPT-4 eleven-oh-six drops one point two in Chinese and one point nine in English. The coherent test bites precisely where stories carry many questions and does nothing where they do not.
[O] So the mechanism is visible and behaves as you would predict.
[G] It does. Which means the interesting quantity is not the raw drop, it is the differential between humans and models on the same story structures. Humans face the same product. They lose thirteen point six on average; the models lose at least twenty.

[G] The false belief task, which is exactly where the models looked best. On top of that, they ran the ablation that undercuts their own headline number and published it. That is the behavior you want from benchmark authors and it is rare.
[S] My deflationary case is about the measuring apparatus around the good idea. [O] What does the paper concede itself?
[G] More than most. Answering theory of mind questions correctly is not the same as deploying theory of mind in an interaction.
[S] Which is the boundary between this and SOTOPIA, or any interactive social agent evaluation.
[G] Exactly the boundary, and they name it rather than blur it.
[O] Sylvie, the case study at the end. It is one ability and it is doing a lot of rhetorical work.

[O] That is an existence proof, not a population result.
[G] Correct, one model, one item. I would not generalize it. What it does usefully is convert an abstract claim — models ride semantic association — into something you can look at.
[S] So where does this leave evaluation practice, which is the part I actually care about.
[G] Three things. First, contamination by construction is a solvable problem and this paper shows the price: your authors write every item, trained by domain experts, with two rounds of cross-validation. Second, item-level accuracy on multi-question stories systematically overstates comprehension, and the fix is cheap — you already have the per-question results, you just have to also report the all-correct-per-story number. Third, and this is the uncomfortable one, the task where the models looked most human is the task that collapses hardest under the stricter rule. Templated structure buys the score.
