[S] And this is a change of scenery for this show. We spend most of our time on text benchmarks, where the grader is a string match, or a rubric, or another language model. Today the grader is a physics engine.
[O] Merrick, welcome. You know this work closely. Give us the one-line reason it is on the docket.
[G] Thank you for having me. The single most interesting thing about BEHAVIOR is what the word success means in it. Each activity is defined as a goal condition in a formal logical language, and at every simulation step the simulator checks whether those predicates are literally true of the physical world it is simulating. Is this book actually inside that shelf. Is this fish actually cooked. No judge model, no reference trajectory, no rubric. That is a completely different epistemic position from nearly everything else this feed covers.
[S] Push on that, because "predicate check" could just be a dressed-up exact match, and exact match is the weakest grader we know.
[G] The difference is where the predicate is grounded. Cooked is not a token the agent emitted. It is a temperature state the simulator tracks for that object, and changes when the object spends time near a heat source. On top of is not a string in an answer. It is a geometric and contact relation the physics engine evaluates. So the check is exact, yes, but exact about the world rather than exact about text. You cannot satisfy it by describing the outcome. You have to cause it.
[O] Back up to the problem statement, because the paper is quite structured about it. What was broken in embodied benchmarking in 2021?
[G] The authors frame it as three difficulties, one per stage of building a benchmark. Definition: which activities even belong in a household benchmark, and on what authority. Realization: can a simulator actually support them. Evaluation: what do success and efficiency mean when activities are wildly different lengths in wildly different rooms. Existing benchmarks each solved one and punted on the others.
[S] Give me the concrete failure modes. Which benchmarks, and what did they miss?
[G] Roughly, the field had split in two. Navigation and instruction-following environments — VirtualHome, ALFRED — gave you diverse scenes and language grounding, but the agent picked among predefined discrete actions with generous access to world state. Low physical complexity. Manipulation suites — RLBench, Meta-World, Robosuite, SoftGym — gave you real contact physics, but confined to a handful of hand-picked short tasks. Low diversity. And rearrangement-style benchmarks added realistic scenes with physics, but reduced success to moving objects to target poses.
[O] And the objection to that last one is the one I find most persuasive, honestly.
[G] It is the cleanest argument in the paper. Most real housework is not rearrangement. Cooking changes temperature, laundry changes wetness, cleaning changes cleanliness. If your simulator can only move things, then cooking, cleaning and laundering are structurally out of scope no matter how good your physics is. BEHAVIOR's answer is a simulator that tracks those extended states as first-class properties.
[S] Fine. So how do they choose the hundred activities? Because "we picked a hundred chores" is exactly the researcher-judgment move they are criticizing.
[G] This is my favourite design decision in the paper. They do not choose them. They draw them from the American Time Use Survey — a U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics survey of how Americans actually spend their time, which the paper says contains more than twenty two hundred activities. They filter that space for semantic diversity, for diversity in the kinds of state change required, and for simulation feasibility. No outdoor scenes, no other humans or animals, nothing requiring heavy cloth or fluid simulation.
[O] So the selection criterion is external. That is a real methodological asset — the benchmark is not a mirror of the authors' intuitions about what robots should do.
[S] It is an asset with an asterisk. The filter for simulation feasibility is applied by the authors, and it is doing enormous work. Whatever is hard to simulate quietly leaves the distribution, and what is left is no longer distributed like real housework. It is distributed like the simulatable subset of real housework.
[G] That is fair, and the paper is reasonably upfront about it — the claim is that BEHAVIOR activities come from, and are distributed similarly to, the full space of simulatable activities in the survey. Simulatable is in the claim. But your point stands: the frontier of what the simulator can do is still a researcher-controlled boundary.
[O] Merrick, take us into the language itself. This is the piece I think is most portable to other benchmarks.
[G] It is called BDDL — the BEHAVIOR Domain Definition Language. It is predicate logic, and it is explicitly inspired by PDDL, the classical planning language, but the paper is careful to say it diverges. PDDL defines actions symbolically so a planner can search over them. BDDL defines only state. The agent acts in physics; the language just describes which physical states count.
[S] So an activity is what, formally?
[G] An activity is a pair of state sets: a set of acceptable initial states and a set of acceptable goal states. Both are logical formulas over predicates grounded in the simulator. Unary predicates over a single object — cooked of fish, sliced of tomato, dusty of table, soaked of rag, toggled-on of stove. Binary predicates over two — on top of apple and plate, inside of food and fridge, under present and tree. The variables are object categories drawn from WordNet, and the paper ships a new object dataset of twelve hundred and seventeen models across three hundred and ninety one categories to fill them.
[O] And the payoff of that being declarative is what, exactly? Say it plainly, because this is the crux.
[G] Two things, and they are symmetric. On the input side, an initial condition does not name a scene — it names constraints. So the simulator can sample many different physical scenes that all satisfy it, in different homes, with different object models, in different poses. The paper calls this potentially infinite instances per definition. On the output side, a goal condition does not name a trajectory — it names an acceptable end state. So the agent gets credit for any physically valid way of getting there, not only the one route the designer happened to script.
[S] Here is where I want to be careful with vocabulary, because I have seen people garble this. A hundred is the number of what?
[G] Activity definitions. Not instances, not demonstrations, not episodes. There are a hundred activities. The paper crowdsourced two definitions for each, so two hundred definitions total, and from those it instantiated three hundred concrete activity instances in simulation. And separately there are five hundred human demonstrations. Four different numbers, four different things, and conflating any two of them gets the benchmark badly wrong.
[O] Say more about the crowdsourcing, because I did not expect the definitions to be crowdsourced.
[G] The authors deliberately did not write them. They hired annotators through Upwork, based in the United States for familiarity with the survey activities, paid fifteen dollars per annotation — roughly twenty to thirty dollars an hour by the paper's estimate. The annotators used a block-based visual interface, no formal logic background required, with a live feasibility button that sampled the draft definition against remote simulator instances before it was accepted. The stated reason is to avoid injecting researcher bias into what a chore means.
[S] I like that far more than I expected to. The subjectivity of "what counts as a clean kitchen" is real, and pushing it onto annotators with a feasibility check is a defensible way to handle it.
[O] What is the simulator side? Because none of this works if the environment cannot represent burnt or soaked.
[G] It runs in iGibson 2.0, built on the Bullet physics engine, across fifteen fully interactive reconstructions of real homes — a hundred rooms in total. The upgrade over the previous version is exactly what you said: heat sources, water sources, dust and stain particles, and object appearance that changes with extended state. Two embodiments. A bimanual humanoid avatar with twenty four degrees of freedom, which the humans in VR control. And a Fetch mobile manipulator at twelve or thirteen degrees of freedom, whose kinematics match real hardware. Both run at thirty hertz.
[S] And the action space?
[G] Two options, and the distinction matters enormously later. Either low-level continuous control — for the humanoid, an eighteen-dimensional action, three dimensions of base motion and seven per hand. Or six temporally extended action primitives: navigate to, grasp, place on top, place inside, open, and close. Each primitive has two variants. Fully simulated, which motion-plans with a bidirectional RRT-star and actually executes against physics. And partially simulated, which teleports the body to a feasible target configuration and skips physical execution entirely.
[O] Hold that thought, because I suspect that variant distinction is the whole ballgame later.
[S] Before we get there — the metric. Q. Walk me through it, because a graduated success score is exactly where benchmarks usually smuggle in generosity.
[G] The goal condition is a logical expression, so you flatten it into a set of disjunctive clauses, each one a conjunction of ground literals. For each clause you compute the fraction of its literals that are currently true, and Q is the maximum of those fractions over all the clauses. So Q equals one only when some single clause is entirely satisfied, which is the definition of having completed the activity. And below one, it is partial credit against the best-matching way of finishing.
[O] The max over disjuncts is the elegant bit. It means the agent is scored against whichever legitimate interpretation of the goal it is closest to, rather than being punished for choosing a different valid route than the grader expected.
[S] It also means Q is not a probability of anything and does not compose across activities. But I will grant it is the honest way to do partial credit on a logical goal. What is the reward?
[G] Q is the reward. The appendix states it plainly: the agent receives a reward of one for every ground goal literal it satisfies during the episode, and the episode terminates either when Q reaches one or on timeout. So the grader and the training signal are the same object.
[S] That is worth sitting with. In text evaluation we agonize about whether optimizing the metric corrupts it. Here they have collapsed them on purpose, and I think that is actually defensible when the metric is a physical fact rather than a proxy — you cannot Goodhart "the book is inside the shelf" without putting the book inside the shelf.
[O] What about efficiency? A robot that takes four hours and destroys the kitchen has technically succeeded.
[G] There are six secondary metrics. Simulated time. Kinematic disarrangement, meaning physical displacement the agent causes in the scene. Logical disarrangement, meaning change in the logical state. Distance navigated by the base. And accumulated hand displacement while in contact, separately for left and right. The critical thing to keep straight is that all six are costs. Lower is better. The paper's motivation is that a successful solution may still be ineffective if it takes too long or disrupts the scene. So these are not scores you want to maximize.
[O] And they can be normalized against humans, which brings us to the demonstration dataset.
[G] Five hundred successful demonstrations, seven hundred fifty eight and a half minutes in total, collected in VR on an HTC Vive Pro Eye, with humans controlling the same bimanual embodiment the agents use. The structure is deliberate: for each of the hundred activities, three demonstrations of the same activity instance, one of a different instance in the same scene, and one in a different scene. So it spans both within-instance human variability and the benchmark's own instance diversity.
[S] And I want to nail down the epistemic status of those demonstrations, because "human ground truth" invites overreading.
[G] They are a reference for normalization, not an absolute ceiling — and the paper gives you the reason to be careful. These are humans in VR, inheriting VR's interaction and sensing constraints. The appendix even observes that demonstrators preferred moving objects one at a time rather than carrying two, and notes explicitly that this strategy performs worse on the efficiency metrics. So the human reference is not an optimum. It is a well-instrumented sample of how people behave under a particular interface.
