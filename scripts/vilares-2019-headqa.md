[S] The best system in this paper is a bag-of-words retriever. Not a reader. Not an entailment model. A T F I D F index over Wikipedia, scoring question-plus-candidate-answer as a search query. That is the state of the art here, and I think that is the whole story.
[O] I think that is half of it. The other half is what the retriever does with its win. On the twenty sixteen exams, that best system's score sits below the official pass mark in every one of the six subject areas. Not short in one and close in another. Below in all six.
[S] So the headline is a benchmark nobody can pass, won by a method nobody would call reasoning.
[G] Both of those are accurate, and the paper would not dispute either. What it adds is a scoring rule that makes the failure legible. It reports the exam's own points scheme alongside accuracy: plus three for a right answer, minus one for a wrong one. Under that rule a confidently wrong system scores below zero, and one of the four neural systems does exactly that on medicine.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio, where an optimist, a skeptic, and a visiting scholar take one paper apart. Today it is HEAD Q A, a healthcare dataset for complex reasoning, by David Vilares and Carlos Gomez-Rodriguez, published at A C L in twenty nineteen and posted to arXiv that June.
[S] It is on the docket because it is one of the earliest benchmarks built out of real professional licensing exams, in a language that is not English, with the exam's real scoring rule attached.
[O] And our guest today is Arabella, who has read this one closely. Arabella, welcome. Start us at the gap. What was broken in twenty nineteen that this was built to fix?
[G] Thank you. Two gaps, stacked on each other. The first is saturation. By twenty nineteen, neural readers were closing on human-level scores on bAbI and SQuAD, and the paper cites the critiques of that moment directly, Kaushik and Lipton, and Clark and colleagues, arguing those sets often let surface-level knowledge suffice. The field's answer was a wave of multi-choice datasets designed to demand more. ARC on grade-school science. RACE, one hundred thousand questions from Chinese middle and high school exams. SWAG for everyday commonsense.
[S] Grade school, middle school, high school. I see the pattern.
[G] That is exactly the paper's complaint. Its own words are that this kind of dataset is scarce for complex domains like medicine. What did exist in medicine was textual entailment over medical text, or document-and-snippet-level question answering in the BioASQ mould. The authors say they know of no resource demanding general reasoning in a genuinely expert domain. The second gap is language: essentially all of that infrastructure, the datasets and the readers alike, is English-only.
[O] So where do the questions actually come from?
[G] Spain's health ministry runs annual examinations to fill specialization positions across the public healthcare system. To sit one you need a bachelor's degree of four to six years in the field, applicants typically prepare for a year or more, and the exam ranks thousands of candidates for a limited number of posts. In medicine, your rank is what decides whether you get, say, a cardiology position at a particular hospital.
[S] So the exam has consequences. That is not nothing, but it is not yet evidence about the questions.
[G] The consequences shape the questions, and they shape a design decision we will come back to. The corpus scrapes these exams, from twenty thirteen onward, across six areas: medicine, nursing, pharmacology, psychology, biology and chemistry. Radiophysics is excluded because its equation-heavy exam files would not parse cleanly. Questions the board later invalidated are dropped. The total is six thousand seven hundred sixty-five questions.
[O] And the split.
[G] Two thousand six hundred fifty-seven for training, one thousand three hundred sixty-six for development, two thousand seven hundred forty-two for test. The crucial detail is that it is split by exam year, not randomly. Twenty thirteen and twenty fourteen are the training set, twenty fifteen is development, twenty sixteen onward is the test set.
[S] Why not random? Randomized splits are the default for a reason.
[G] The paper gives two reasons. First, the questions and the per-area counts are already designed by a team of healthcare experts trying to avoid bias, so the usual motivation for shuffling is weaker here. Second, and the authors call this the more relevant one, a random split would destroy any ability to compare against official, aggregated human results, which are published per exam year.
[O] That second reason is the good one. You cannot line yourself up against a real pass mark if you have shredded the exam first.
[S] I will grant that. I will also note that nobody in this paper trains on that training split.
[G] Correct, and the paper is explicit about it. Every approach it tests is unsupervised or distantly supervised. The split exists so future supervised work has a fixed, standard partition to report against.
[O] What else is in the corpus that people miss?
[G] Two things. The twenty thirteen and twenty fourteen exams offer five answer options; every later year offers four. Hold onto that, it matters for the metrics. And roughly fourteen percent of the medicine questions, medicine specifically rather than the corpus as a whole, refer to an accompanying image, an X-ray or a scan, that carries information the text alone does not. The dataset stores a path to that image. No baseline in this paper reads it.
[S] So a slice of medicine is unanswerable by construction for every system tested.
[G] For every system in this paper, yes. The example the paper prints is image number twenty-one from the twenty seventeen medicine exam: a chest X-ray for a thirty-eight-year-old patient, a long smoking history, mild anaemia, normal spirometry, and four candidate diagnoses.
[S] Then the English version. I want this nailed down, because half the numbers in this paper live on one side of it.
[G] HEAD Q A is natively Spanish. The authors additionally release HEAD Q A E N, a full translation produced with the Google Translate A P I. To check it they sampled sixty random questions with their answers, had two fluent Spanish-English speakers score adequacy, and one native English speaker score fluency, on the Koehn and Monz five-point scales. Adequacy came back at four point three five and four point seven one out of five, which is the most-of-the-meaning band. Fluency came back at four out of five, which is good.
[S] Sixty questions, and the two adequacy annotators differ by more than a third of a point.
[G] They do, and the paper reports both rather than averaging them away. The annotators also gave texture: most disease names translated successfully, while molecular formulae in the chemistry questions were the weak point, which the authors attribute partly to the translator and partly to the difficulty of pulling formulae out of the original exam files in the first place.
[O] Now the systems. What is being run, and on which language?
[G] Keep those two straight, because the paper's tables do. Three control methods, all reported on the Spanish corpus. Random sampling. Blind-x, which always picks option number x, motivated by Poundstone's observation that exam writers do not place correct answers uniformly and tend to cluster them toward the middle options. And Length, which always picks the longest answer, on the theory that an option which has to be totally correct often needs more words.
[S] Those are good controls. Cheap, and they catch precisely the failure modes people pretend do not exist.
[G] Above the controls sits the information-retrieval baseline. It takes the question, concatenates each candidate answer to it, and sends each of those as a separate query to D R Q A's document retriever, against a downloaded Wikipedia dump with headers and tables stripped out. Scoring is T F I D F weighted bag-of-words, with word order and bigram counts folded in. The predicted answer is whichever query scored the highest document relevance.
[O] So there is no reader at all in that model. No span extraction, no entailment.
[G] None. Run against Spanish Wikipedia it is the Spanish retrieval baseline. Run against English Wikipedia, on HEAD Q A E N, it is the cross-lingual one. Then four neural systems, and this is important: all four are run only on HEAD Q A E N, never on the Spanish original. Multi-choice D R Q A, an attentive-reader-style span extractor trained on SQuAD, whose extracted span is matched against each candidate by token overlap. Multi-choice BiDAF, trained on SQuAD and then continued-trained on the ARC science questions. And two entailment systems, D G E M and a decomposable-attention model, which score question-plus-option as a hypothesis against retrieved sentences.
[S] And when a system selects more than one answer?
[G] The tie-break is to keep the longest one. Same fallback when D R Q A's extracted span shares no tokens at all with any candidate, which the paper says happened for many spans.
[O] That is a control leaking into a system.
[G] That is a fair way to put it, and the paper does not report how often the fallback fires.
[S] Metrics.
[G] Two, side by side throughout. Plain accuracy, and POINTS, the exams' real rule: plus three for a right answer, minus one for a wrong one. Higher is better on both, but POINTS can go negative, and it does. And because some exam years have five options and others four, the paper says explicitly that the two need not rank areas the same way. An area can have better accuracy than another and a worse POINTS score.
[O] Which is exactly why you should never quote one of those numbers as if it were the other.
[O] Give me the unsupervised table. Full corpus, unsplit.
[G] Averaged across all six areas. On the Spanish side, the Length control gets twenty-eight point one percent accuracy, and separately twenty-eight point two POINTS. Spanish retrieval gets thirty-two point nine percent, and seventy-one point seven POINTS. On the English side, cross-lingual retrieval gets thirty-four point six percent, and eighty-seven point six POINTS. That English retriever is the best system in the paper, on both metrics.
[S] And the four neural readers.
[G] All of them below both retrievers, all on the English corpus. Multi-choice D R Q A, twenty-eight point five percent and thirty-two point one POINTS. BiDAF, twenty-eight point nine percent and thirty-five point three POINTS. D G E M, twenty-nine point one percent and thirty-seven point eight POINTS. The decomposable-attention model, twenty-seven point five percent and twenty-three point three POINTS.
[O] Hold on. Twenty-seven point five is under the Length control's twenty-eight point one.
[G] It is, on the six-area average, and the same ordering holds on POINTS: twenty-three point three against the control's twenty-eight point two. Broken out by subject, that model beats Length only in biology and trails it in the other five. The paper's discussion says the evaluated models surpass all control methods, which is its framing of the overall picture, and the tables show this one system as the exception to it.
[S] Say the caveat on that comparison, because I think there is one.
[G] There is. The controls are reported on the Spanish corpus and that model runs on the English one, so it is a cross-language comparison. It is the same cross-language comparison the paper's own summary sentence is making, since the controls are only ever run on the Spanish side.
[S] Thank you for putting that on the record. A model that loses to always-pick-the-longest-answer is a result, not a footnote.
[G] It is also the model that goes negative. On medicine, on POINTS, on the English corpus, it scores minus thirteen.
[O] And the Spanish Length control on medicine?
[G] Minus one, on POINTS. Different corpora, same subject, so do not read that as a like-for-like gap. And the middle-option control on the Spanish side, always picking option three, averages minus two point three POINTS across the six areas, which tells you how punishing minus one for a wrong answer is when you are guessing.
[S] Now the official test split. Does the ranking survive?
[G] At the top, yes. Cross-lingual retrieval on the English corpus leads with thirty-seven point two percent accuracy and one hundred eleven point eight POINTS. Spanish retrieval follows at thirty-five point two percent and ninety-three point two POINTS. BiDAF on English is thirty point three percent and forty-eight POINTS. D G E M is thirty point six percent and fifty-one point five POINTS. And BiDAF's per-subject POINTS swing enormously, from fourteen point five on medicine up to one hundred four on biology.
[S] What does the Length control do on that same split?
[G] Thirty point five percent accuracy, and fifty point two POINTS, on the Spanish corpus. Which puts BiDAF, on English, under the control on both metrics: thirty point three against thirty point five on accuracy, forty-eight against fifty point two on POINTS. And it puts D G E M essentially level with it, thirty point six against thirty point five, fifty-one point five against fifty point two.
[O] That is a worse picture for the readers than the unsupervised table, not a better one.
[G] It is a smaller, harder partition, twenty sixteen onward, which is also the years with four options rather than five. I would not read a trend into it. [O] Now the number that made me sit up. The comparison against real exam-takers.
[G] Different slice again, so keep it separate from everything we just said. This is the twenty sixteen exam only, not the test split, and it is POINTS only. Three rows. The average of the ten best human scorers, the official pass mark, and the best model, cross-lingual retrieval on the English corpus. In biology, the ten best humans average six hundred twenty-seven point one, the pass mark is two hundred nineteen, the model gets one hundred sixty-eight. In psychology, the pass mark is two hundred ten and the model gets sixty-two.
[S] Below the pass mark in how many of the six?
[G] All six. Biology is the closest and it is still fifty-one points short. Medicine: pass mark two hundred seven, model one hundred twenty-four. The authors flag their own caveat, that the comparison is not strictly comparable, because the last ten questions of the human exams are treated as backup questions and scored differently than they are for the model.
[O] Even with that caveat, the gap is not a rounding error.

[O] Then let me make the optimist case properly. This benchmark is honest in a way a lot of twenty nineteen benchmarks were not. The questions were not written for a paper. They were written by domain experts to rank thousands of real applicants for real jobs. The scoring rule is the real one, with a penalty for guessing. The split is by exam year, so it lines up against real human results. And the source language is not English. Every one of those choices cost the authors something and bought the field something.
[S] My deflationary case is narrower than you would expect. I am not going to argue the task is easy. I am going to argue that the second-order claims outrun the evidence. But those two systems do not differ only by translation. They search two different Wikipedias.
[G] That objection is well taken, and the paper does not address it. English Wikipedia's coverage of medical topics is denser than Spanish Wikipedia's. The sixty-question adequacy and fluency check is a genuine check on the translation, but it cannot separate those two explanations, and the retrieval gap is being offered as evidence for one of them. Point to the skeptic.

[G] Hold the retrieval corpus fixed. Run both language versions against the same encyclopedia, or against a deliberately coverage-matched pair. The paper already has everything it needs to run that ablation.
[S] Claim two: the human comparison gives you the pass mark and the average of the ten best. That is a floor and a ceiling. It never tells you what a median applicant scores, and thousands sit these exams and many fail them.

[O] One more for you. Medicine is the hardest subject in almost every row. The paper attributes that to length. Does that explanation hold?
[G] It holds as far as it goes, and the ordering supports it. Biology, with an average question of eleven point one one tokens, is among the easiest. But it is not the only candidate explanation. Roughly fourteen percent of medicine questions depend on an image that no baseline reads. [O] What changes if this holds up?
[G] Two things I would carry forward. First, the design pattern. A benchmark built from a real credentialing exam arrives with a scoring rule, a pass threshold, and human results you did not have to construct. Those are validity anchors most benchmarks have to invent from nothing. Second, the negative result about readers versus retrievers is a statement about where the bottleneck sat: the paper's framing is that effective information extraction has to come first, with reasoning built on top of it.
[S] And a contamination note the paper could not have made in twenty nineteen. These exams live at a stable government address, as public files, indexed. It is a caution for anyone quoting a modern score on this set.
