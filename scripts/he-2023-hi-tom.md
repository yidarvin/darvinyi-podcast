[O] There is a sentence in this paper that ought to stop you cold. As the theory-of-mind order climbs from the zeroth to the fourth, joint accuracy goes, in the authors' own words, from near perfect to near zero.
[S] And there is a second sentence, buried in the discussion section, that ought to stop you harder. When the strongest model gets a higher-order question right, a large share of the time it is because it repeated the answer it already gave to the first-order question.
[O] Those two findings point in opposite directions, and I think only one of them is really a measurement of recursive belief reasoning.
[S] Complete agreement. I just think it is the second one, and I think it quietly undercuts the first.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio. Today we are on Hi Tom, a benchmark for evaluating higher-order theory of mind reasoning in large language models, from He, Wu, Jia, Mihalcea, Chen and Deng, published in the Findings of E M N L P in twenty twenty-three.
[S] It is on the docket because it is the paper that broke the first-and-second-order ceiling that every prior Sally-Anne benchmark sat on, and because the way it is scored is genuinely contested.
[O] Joining us is Alastair, a researcher who has spent a lot of time with this benchmark and with what came after it. Alastair, welcome.
[G] Delighted to be here. And I would start by defending both of your readings, because the paper contains both, and it does not resolve the tension between them. That is the interesting part.
[S] Then set the table. What was the actual gap before this benchmark existed?
[G] Theory of mind in language models had been evaluated almost entirely at the first and second order. First order is, where does agent one think the object is. Second order is, where does agent two think agent one thinks the object is. Nematzadeh and colleagues' Tom Q A, from twenty eighteen, and Le and colleagues' ToMi, from twenty nineteen, are both Sally-Anne-style question-answering sets generated from bAbI-style story templates, and both of them stop right there.
[O] Which is a strange place to stop, given that actual social reasoning goes deeper than that all the time.
[G] The authors make exactly that argument. Multi-party negotiation, tacit coordination, the layered they-don't-know-that-we-know-they-know inference. The paper's own opening figure borrows a scene from the television series Friends to illustrate what a fourth-order belief statement sounds like in ordinary speech. Their diagnosis of why nobody had gone deeper is technical, not conceptual, though. The automatic story-generation scripts prior work used simply do not carry enough information in a short template to support a well-posed higher-order question.
[S] So the contribution is a generator, not just a dataset.
[G] Both, and I think the generator is the more durable of the two. They extend the Sally-Anne pipeline so that a single story supports questions at the zeroth, first, second, third and fourth order, all about the same object. And they add something ToMi does not have at all, which is agent communication.
[O] Tell me about the communication piece, because that seems like the real design move.
[G] It is. After the agents leave a room, they can make statements to each other. Public statements are heard by everyone present. Private statements are heard only by the listener. And crucially, the authors deliberately make some of that communicated information false. The worked example in the paper has the milk starting on the table, Sally leaving, Anne moving the milk to a box, and then outside the room a character publicly claiming the milk is in the fridge, which it is not, while Sally privately tells Anne the milk is on the table, which was true when Sally left but is no longer true by the time she says it.
[S] That is a nice construction, because the falsehood is not a single flavour. One is a lie and one is a stale belief sincerely held.
[G] Precisely, and that distinction is what forces the higher orders to be about who told whom what rather than merely who saw what. Without communication, a nested belief question degenerates into a visibility bookkeeping problem.
[O] What is the scale of the generator?
[G] The component pool is thirty rooms, thirty-seven objects, thirty-nine containers and forty agents, and every story features five agents. A story runs one to three chapters. Each chapter is an entry, where agents come into a room and observe everything in it, then optional sequential object movements, then optional outside-the-room communication. They also sprinkle in random distractor sentences, things like Jack likes the red container, to break up the template regularity.
[S] Does that actually make the stories harder, or just longer?
[G] Both, measurably. Against the older sets, Hi Tom stories average twenty-six point four seven lines, five agents and seven point three nine containers per story. Tom Q A and its easy variant average about fifteen point zero five lines, three point two two agents and five containers. ToMi averages eight point eight six lines, two point seven five agents and two containers. And the position of the correct answer is spread fairly evenly through the story rather than clustering at the end. Roughly twenty-eight point seven percent of correct answers fall in the first quarter of the story, twenty-seven point two in the second, eighteen point eight in the third, and twenty-five point three in the final quarter.
[O] So you cannot win by reading the last line.
[G] Not reliably, no. Though hold that thought, because the error analysis has something to say about position.
[S] Let us get to the scoring, because I think that is where this paper lives or dies. There are two metrics.
[G] There are. Standard accuracy is the obvious one, per question. Joint accuracy is adapted from ToMi and it is strict and cumulative. For a given story, the joint score at order k is one if and only if every question from order zero up through order k in that same story is answered correctly. Otherwise it is zero.
[S] So one slip on an easy first-order question mechanically zeroes out the second, third and fourth order joint scores for that story.
[G] It does. The authors' justification is definitional. They treat a higher-order question as a recursive successor of the lower-order ones for the same story, so getting the successor right while botching the predecessor is not, on their view, a coherent success.
[O] I actually find that defensible. If you cannot say where Sally thinks the milk is, your claim to know where Anne thinks Sally thinks the milk is looks like luck.
[S] It is defensible as a definition. It is much less defensible as a measurement instrument, because it fuses two things that have completely different causes. One is genuinely harder recursive reasoning at depth. The other is just the compounding of independent per-question error rates. Flip five coins that each land heads with probability point eight and the chance of five heads is not a statement about the fifth coin.
[G] That is the sharpest methodological objection to the paper and I think it lands. The paper does not report a non-cumulative, order-k-only accuracy that would isolate the first effect from the second.
[O] Alright, the models. Who is in the roster and when.
[G] Four models, all evaluated zero-shot, under two prompting styles. The styles are Vanilla Prompting, where the model just picks an answer with no explanation, and Chain-of-Thought Prompting, where it must reason step by step first. The models are G P T three point five turbo, G P T four thirty-two K, Claude instant, and Guanaco sixty-five B, which is an open-source LLaMA fine-tune trained with QLoRA.
[S] Dates?
[G] And this is worth being precise about, because it is easy to over-claim. The two OpenAI runs are dated, June fourteenth and fifteenth of twenty twenty-three. The paper does not date the Claude instant or the Guanaco sixty-five B runs. So you can say the OpenAI numbers are pinned to a specific mid-twenty-twenty-three snapshot. You cannot say that about all four.
[O] Fine. Give me the headline table.
[G] Standard accuracy, averaged across both prompting styles and both deception conditions. G P T four thirty-two K leads at fifty-eight point nine nine percent. Claude instant is next at forty-six percent flat. Then Guanaco sixty-five B at thirty-two point one seven, and G P T three point five turbo at thirty-one point five.
[O] Every model under sixty percent. That is the paper's headline and it is a clean one.
[G] The grand-overall figure for every model is under sixty, yes, and the authors read that as evidence that Hi Tom is challenging even for the most sophisticated models. I would add one honest footnote, which is that individual cells do break sixty. G P T four's chain-of-thought, no-deception cell is sixty-four point zero four.
[S] Now the ordering there is odd. Guanaco sixty-five B, an open fine-tune, edges out G P T three point five turbo?
[G] On the grand overall, yes, by zero point six seven. And this is a case where you have to look at all four cells rather than the summary, because the summary hides a clean split. Guanaco beats G P T three point five on both of the Vanilla-prompt cells, thirty-three point three three against twenty-eight point six seven without deception, and thirty-three point three three against twenty-six point three three with deception.
[S] And the chain-of-thought cells?
[G] Reverse cleanly. G P T three point five beats Guanaco on both. Thirty-five point six seven against thirty-five without deception, and thirty-five point three three against twenty-six point nine nine with deception. So neither model wins everywhere. Guanaco takes the vanilla column, G P T three point five takes the chain-of-thought column, and the vanilla margins happen to be enough to give Guanaco the aggregate by that fraction of a point.
[O] That is a good example of why you should not quote a single overall number.
[G] It is the whole reason I keep insisting on labelling every figure with its order and its condition. The result grid here is order by model by prompting style by deception, and an aggregate over any of those axes can invert the story on any other.
[S] Which brings me to chain of thought, because I expected it to help a lot and apparently it did not.
[G] It barely moves the needle and sometimes it hurts. Going from vanilla to chain of thought on overall accuracy changes Claude instant by plus zero point six six, G P T four by plus one point seven seven, Guanaco by minus two point three four, and G P T three point five turbo by plus eight, which is the one substantial gain in the set.
[O] Plus eight is not nothing.
[S] Plus eight from a base of twenty-seven point five is also the classic pattern where the weakest model has the most headroom for a prompting trick, which tells you about the prompt and not about the reasoning.
[G] There is a more pointed version of the finding, though. Restrict to stories that contain deception, and chain of thought makes three of the four models worse. Guanaco by minus six point three four, Claude instant by minus one point six seven, and G P T four by minus zero point zero nine. The authors' explanation is that a deceptive detail derails an early step of the reasoning chain and then cascades through everything downstream.
[O] That is a genuinely interesting mechanism. Reasoning out loud gives the lie more surface to grab.
[S] It also should temper anyone's enthusiasm about scratchpads as a general-purpose fix for social reasoning.
[O] Alastair, walk us down the order ladder itself. What does the collapse actually look like?

[G] Two independent downward pressures, and deception is a third. G P T three point five collapses harder and earlier than G P T four. Several of its three-chapter, no-chain-of-thought cells sit at exactly zero by the second order.

[S] So the honest statement is that the trend is steeply downward and the top of the ladder is bad, not that everything is pinned at zero before the top.

[O] Now do the deception isolation, because that was the number that surprised me most.
[G] The paper plots joint accuracy against the number of deceptive sentences per story for G P T four, and the zeroth order is excluded from that plot because deception cannot affect the question about where the object really is. As deception sentences go from zero to four, the joint accuracy drops are thirty-two percent at the first order, eighteen point one percent at the second, sixteen percent at the third, and eleven percent at the fourth.
[O] The biggest hit is at the first order. The order the models otherwise handle best.
[S] Which has a deflationary reading you should say out loud. If you are already near the floor at the fourth order, there is very little left to lose. Eleven percent of nearly nothing is a small number for an uninteresting reason.
[G] That is a fair floor-effect caveat and the paper does not raise it. But I would not let it fully explain the first-order result away, because thirty-two percent is a very large absolute drop at the level of the ladder where you would most expect robustness. A single false public statement is enough to substantially break the model's model of one other agent's belief.
[O] Alright, I have been waiting for the answer-coincidence finding. This is the one that reframes everything.
[G] It is the sharpest thing in the paper. The authors check how often G P T four's own answers at the higher orders simply match the answer it gave at the first order. At the second order, seventy-two point four percent of the time. At the third, sixty-four point six percent. At the fourth, fifty-nine point eight percent.
[S] And the ground truth comparison?
[G] In Hi Tom itself, the correct higher-order answers coincide with the correct first-order answer only thirty point nine percent of the time at the second order, twenty point nine percent at the third, and twenty-two point two percent at the fourth.
[S] So the model repeats itself well over twice as often as the data warrants. That is the ball game.
[O] I want to push back, but honestly I mostly cannot. What that gap says is that a meaningful chunk of the apparent higher-order competence is a default, not a derivation.
[G] The paper's own phrasing is that G P T four's enhanced performance on certain questions may be due to the coincidence of correct answers across different orders. And notice how it interacts with the two metrics in opposite directions. A model that just parrots its first-order guess would look better than it deserves on standard accuracy at higher orders, because it gets the coincidences for free. But joint accuracy would punish it for any earlier slip regardless of the cause. So the two metrics are distorted by the same behaviour in opposite directions.
[S] That is the cleanest statement of why I distrust the headline collapse curve as a measurement of recursion specifically.
[O] What about the error taxonomy? That is a manual effort, which I always respect.
[G] They hand-evaluate three hundred step-by-step chain-of-thought responses, one hundred fifty each from G P T four and G P T three point five, and sort the failures into five types. Insufficient reasoning depth, commonsense errors, hallucinations, temporal ignorance, and spurious causal inference.
[O] Give me the flavour of the first one.
[G] The example in the paper is a third-order question, where does Jack think Hannah thinks William thinks the carrot is. The model answers green box, because that is where Jack last saw it. It has collapsed three nested beliefs into a single first-hand one. Which is, note, exactly the behavioural signature the coincidence statistic picks up quantitatively.
[S] Do the error rates rise with order?
[G] Four of the five climb fairly steadily from the zeroth to the fourth order. Spurious causal inference is the exception, and it is an interesting one. [O] Why would that one be non-monotonic?
[G] The paper does not offer a mechanism, so this is my read going beyond it. Spurious causal inference requires the model to be confidently building a causal story, and at the deeper orders it may be failing earlier, at the depth-tracking stage, before it ever gets far enough to invent a bad causal link.
[S] Failure modes crowd each other out. That is plausible and it is also a reason to be careful reading any single error curve.
[O] What about position bias? You said hold that thought.
[G] On three-chapter stories, G P T four is correct about seventy percent of the time when the answer is the last-mentioned container, against forty-six percent otherwise. And about sixty-two percent when it is the first-mentioned container, against thirty-eight percent otherwise. It misses things in the middle.
[S] A textbook serial-position effect. Which is a property of long-context reading, not of theory of mind.
[G] Agreed, and I think that is an underrated confound in the whole result. Some of what reads as an order effect is riding on a length effect, because deeper questions in this design tend to require reaching further back into a longer story.

Memorized answers are very unlikely here.
[S] I will grant that without argument. It is the strongest thing about the design.

[G] Both of those score. I would score the contamination point especially highly, because it is structural rather than a claim about a snapshot in time.

[O] That one bothers me too, because these stories are synthetic and long, and I am genuinely unsure a careful human reads them cleanly at the fourth order.
[S] Three, and this is the one I would put in the abstract if it were mine. The coincidence statistic means we do not know how much of the measured higher-order performance is recursion at all.

[G] That is exactly the right question, and the answer is no. [S] What did the field say about this design afterwards?
[G] The most pointed response is OpenToM, from twenty twenty-four, which names Hi Tom among the template-based benchmarks it criticises for producing, in its phrase, overly-structured and ambiguous narratives. That is a second paper's judgment, not something Hi Tom says about itself, and it sits directly against Hi Tom's own framing of its stories as offering greater diversity and challenge than previous datasets.
[O] Which is the standard benchmark lifecycle. You buy contamination resistance with synthetic generation and you pay for it in naturalness.
[G] That is the trade, and I do not think there is a free version of it. Hand-written narratives are natural and leakable. Generated narratives are clean and stilted.
[S] What would raise my confidence most, concretely?
[G] Four things, and they are all cheap relative to building the benchmark. A human baseline on the same stories. Repeated sampling so there are error bars. An ablation reporting order-k accuracy conditioned only on that question, without requiring the lower-order chain, which would settle your metric objection directly. And a rerun on post-twenty-twenty-three frontier models to see whether the collapse persists or whether it was a property of that generation.
[O] Where does this land for evaluation practice generally?
[G] I think the transferable lesson is not about theory of mind at all. It is that when you build a ladder of nested difficulty and score it cumulatively, you have built an instrument that will always produce a dramatic collapse curve, because compounding guarantees one. If you want the curve to mean what it looks like it means, you have to report the non-cumulative version alongside it. That generalises to multi-hop reasoning, to agentic task suites, to anything with dependent subtasks.
[S] And the coincidence check generalises even further. Any time a model can produce a plausible answer by copying its own earlier answer, you should measure how often it does that and compare it against how often the data says it should.
[O] That is a genuinely portable audit and I had not thought of it that way before this paper.
[G] My one-sentence takeaway is the paper's own. Hi Tom pushes theory-of-mind evaluation from a first-and-second-order ceiling to a full zeroth-through-fourth-order ladder with deceptive communication, and finds that accuracy falls steeply with order, with story length, and with deception, while its own discussion section shows that some of the remaining higher-order success is the model repeating its first-order guess.
[O] Mine is that the generator is the lasting contribution. Contamination-resistant, extensible, and it made a question askable that nobody could ask before.
