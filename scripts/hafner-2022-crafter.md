[G] The dominant approach was a suite of narrow environments. The Arcade Learning Environment, Atari, covers roughly fifty-four games depending on the protocol. [O] And that is before you get to the quality problem, which I think is the sharper complaint.
[G] Quite right. Atari games are nearly deterministic, so an agent can approximately memorize an action sequence rather than learn anything that generalizes. ProcGen fixes precisely that with procedurally generated levels and randomized textures, but it is still sixteen separate games at two hundred million steps each, and each game probes one narrow slice of behavior.
[S] What about the obvious answer, which is just use Minecraft?
[G] The paper takes Minecraft seriously and rejects it on four specific grounds. It is too complex to be solved by current methods. It is unclear what metric agents should be evaluated by. The environment is slow. And it is awkward to run because it needs Java and a window server. The paper is explicit that the goal is not to replace Minecraft but to progress faster towards it.
[O] Which I read as unusually well-calibrated ambition. He is not claiming to have built the hard thing. He is claiming to have built the thing you iterate on before you attempt the hard thing.
[S] And NetHack?
[G] NetHack uses symbolic text inputs, so it sidesteps the representation learning challenge entirely, and the original paper used one billion environment steps to acquire the domain knowledge the game demands. Crafter's stated position is that it generates complex worlds from simple underlying rules, so it leans on generalization rather than memorization of facts.
[O] So the pitch is one environment, many abilities, and a budget a normal lab can actually afford. Give me the machinery, Tobias. What is Crafter?
[G] It is a two-dimensional open world survival game inspired by Minecraft and simplified for research throughput. Every episode procedurally generates a fresh world on a sixty-four by sixty-four cell grid using OpenSimplex noise, which gives you grasslands, forests, lakes, mountains and caves. Because the world is new every episode, memorizing a map is not a viable strategy.
[S] What does the agent actually see?
[G] This is the detail I would underline. The agent receives a sixty-four by sixty-four by three color image and nothing else. That image is a local top-down view reaching four cells east and west and three cells north and south of the player. And critically, the inventory is rendered into the bottom of that same image. Health, food, water, rest, collected materials, crafted tools. The agent has to learn to read its own inventory out of pixels.
[O] That is a genuinely nice design decision. It means representation learning is not an optional add-on, it is load-bearing for basic competence.
[S] It also means a chunk of the difficulty is optical character recognition rather than planning, which is a slightly different skill than the one advertised.
[G] That is a fair objection and the paper does not disentangle them. The action space, by contrast, is deliberately trivial. Seventeen flat categorical actions. Move in four directions, interact with whatever is in front of you, sleep, place objects, make tools. If you lack the materials, the action simply has no effect.
[O] And the survival pressure is real, not decorative, which I gather is a deliberate swipe at prior environments.
[G] It is, and the paper says so directly. Food, water and rest all decay over time. When any one of them hits zero you start losing health. Zombies live in grasslands and spawn in larger numbers at night, when your view also becomes restricted and noisy. Skeletons live in caves and shoot arrows from range. The paper's complaint about earlier environments is that the player can often survive by doing nothing, which permits degenerate solutions to intrinsic objectives.
[S] Termination conditions?
[G] The episode ends when health reaches zero, whether from hunger, thirst, tiredness, a monster, or falling into lava, or when you hit the ten thousand step time limit. There is no separate death penalty, because the reward already includes a penalty for losing health. And the environment exposes privileged state that the agent is forbidden to observe, exact inventory counts, coordinates, a semantic map, purely so researchers can probe representations.
[O] Now the achievements, because that is the evaluation surface.
[G] Twenty-two achievements. Some are independent of each other, which tests breadth of exploration. Others form a dependency chain, which tests depth. The deep chain runs roughly, collect wood, place a table, make a wood pickaxe, collect stone, and onward through a furnace to an iron pickaxe and finally a diamond. Collecting a diamond requires the iron pickaxe, which requires a furnace and a table and coal and iron and wood.
[S] And the reward signal?
[G] Plus one the first time each achievement is unlocked in an episode, plus or minus zero point one per health point regenerated or lost. Since maximum health is nine, the health term only moves the first decimal, so taking the ceiling of the episode return recovers the number of achievements unlocked. Optimal episode reward is twenty-two.
[O] So walk us to the metric, because it took a hammer to the head at the top of this show.
[G] Two benchmarks, identical except for one thing. In the first the agent receives the reward signal. In the second it receives nothing and must run on intrinsic motivation alone. Both get a budget of one million environment steps. The paper defends that cap in a footnote, arguing that a larger budget would result in a comparison of compute resources rather than algorithm quality.
[S] Which I will grant is a principled reason, and rarer than it should be.
[G] Then the success rate for each achievement is the fraction of training episodes in which that achievement was unlocked at least once, computed across all episodes leading up to the one million step budget. And the score aggregates those twenty-two rates as a geometric mean. Formally, take the natural log of one plus each success rate, average those, exponentiate, subtract one. Success rates are on a zero to one hundred percent scale, and the number of achievements is twenty-two.
[O] Explain the plus one, because I do not think it is cosmetic.
[G] It is not. The offset floors the contribution of an unachieved task at zero rather than annihilating the score.
