[G] Delighted to be here. I will say up front that this paper is really two papers stapled together, and keeping them apart is most of the work of understanding it.
[O] Which two?
[G] There is a benchmark, MedExQA, and there is a model, Med Phi two, which the authors trained themselves and evaluated on their own benchmark. The benchmark's claims and the model's claims travel separately, and the easiest mistake a reader can make is to let one borrow the other's numbers.
[S] Noted, and we will come back to that hard. Start with the gap. Why build another medical QA benchmark at all?
[G] Because the existing ones are structurally the same object. The medical subjects inside MMLU, then MedQA, then MedMCQA, are all built around picking a letter and counting how often it is right. Classification accuracy is the metric. The authors' argument is that accuracy alone cannot tell you whether a model reasoned its way to the answer or landed on it for a bad reason, and in clinical decision support that distinction is the entire ballgame.
[O] Fluent and wrong is worse than silent, especially near a patient.
[G] Exactly the paper's framing. And where prior benchmarks do carry explanations, the authors argue they are thin. They single out MedMCQA specifically: nearly half of its evaluation set has no explanation at all, duplicate explanations show up, and accuracy is the only metric that paper actually reports. The explanations exist in the file and go unused.
[S] What about coverage? That is the other half of the pitch.
[G] The existing benchmarks are physician-facing. USMLE-style questions, Indian postgraduate medical entrance questions, the medicine subjects of MMLU. Allied health professions with their own licensure exams — speech language pathology, occupational therapy, clinical laboratory science — barely appear. So MedExQA picks five: biomedical engineering, clinical laboratory science, clinical psychology, occupational therapy, and speech language pathology.
[O] Give us the shape of it.
[G] Nine hundred and sixty-five questions total. Clinical laboratory science is the largest at three hundred and seventy-seven. Occupational therapy is one hundred and ninety-four, biomedical engineering one hundred and forty-eight, speech language pathology one hundred and thirty-five, clinical psychology one hundred and eleven. Those split into a tiny few-shot development set of five questions per specialty, twenty-five in all, and a test set of nine hundred and forty, with every specialty holding at least a hundred test items.
[S] Where did the questions come from? This is where I always start with a benchmark.
[G] Read the paper's own sentence carefully, because it matters. The raw data was manually collected from diverse freely accessible online sources, including mock tests and online exams tailored to each specialty. And then: some questions of the mock tests and online exams have explanations for the answers, which the authors used in creating the dataset. The pass mark on those source exams was sixty percent, which becomes a threshold they use later.
[S] So the explanations are harvested, not commissioned.
[G] At least in part, yes, and this is the point I would ask listeners to hold onto. The paper does not say the two explanations per question were independently written by separate authors. What it says is that both explanation sets and the questions underwent human validation, and that the validation, in the paper's words, aimed to ensure that the explanations exhibited distinct writing styles and provided comprehensible reasoning for the correct answer. That is a style check, not an independence claim.
[O] That is a narrower claim than I had in my head.
[G] It is narrower than most summaries of this paper suggest, and the paper's own diagnostic supports the narrower reading. They report something they call Ex SIM, the average cosine similarity between the two explanations for each question. Overall it is seventy-eight point seven percent. Clinical laboratory science is the most divergent pair at seventy-three point seven, speech language pathology the least at eighty point five.
[S] Nearly eighty percent similarity is not two independent experts writing from scratch. That is a paraphrase relationship.
[G] That is a fair characterization of what the number implies, and the paper offers no threshold for what value would count as different enough. It presents Ex SIM descriptively, as evidence of lexical difference, not as a validated pass or fail bar.
[O] What is the rest of the pipeline? Cleaning, filtering?
[G] Duplicate questions and duplicate explanations removed. Near-duplicate questions identified and eliminated with a BERT cosine similarity pass. Questions keyed to specific laws or regulations filtered out with a hand-curated keyword list, which is sensible for a benchmark meant to outlive a jurisdiction. And under fair use guidance the answer options were systematically shuffled.
[S] Do they show the new questions are actually new content, rather than a resampling of what already exists?
[G] They do a t-S N E projection. Nine hundred and sixty-five questions sampled from each of MedExQA, MedQA, MedMCQA and the medicine-related subjects of MMLU, embedded with an off-the-shelf sentence encoder and projected into two dimensions. MedExQA forms its own cluster toward the top of the plot rather than sitting inside the others.
[O] Suggestive rather than dispositive, but I will take it. Now the model. Why build one at all if you are shipping a benchmark?
[G] Their stated reason is a monoculture argument. Essentially every open-source medical language model available at the time was a Llama two derivative — ClinicalCamel, Asclepius, Med forty-two, AlpaCare, Meditron, Medinote, all of them. So they ask whether medical domain adaptation transfers to a different, much smaller base. They take Microsoft's Phi two, at two point seven billion parameters, and continue pretraining it on a one hundred and ten million token medical corpus.
[S] What is in that corpus?
[G] Meditron's medical guidelines at forty-eight point three million tokens, SNOMED C T descriptions at twenty-eight point three million, biomedical article abstracts at thirteen point six million, Wikipedia medical terms at thirteen point three million, and PMC patient notes at six point seven million. Then instruction tuning on roughly two hundred and thirty-nine thousand instructions drawn from the Asclepius, AlpaCare, and N H S question-answering instruction sets. Batch size sixteen, learning rate one times ten to the minus five, three epochs, thirty-six hours total on eight A six thousand GPUs.
[O] Thirty-six hours on eight cards is a genuinely modest budget for a medical model.
[G] It is, and that frugality is part of the argument. They call the result Med Phi two.
[S] Now the evaluation. This is the part that decides whether any of the numbers mean anything.
[G] Three automatic axes, all zero-shot, temperature zero, batch size one. First, accuracy from logits: they look at the next-token logit values over the answer-choice tokens and take the highest as the prediction. The G P T models are excluded from this axis because the A P I does not expose logits.
[O] Second?
[G] Accuracy from chat. They let the model generate free text, then hunt for the answer letter or the matching choice text with regular expressions plus fuzzy string matching. The argument is that this is closer to how a deployed chat system would actually be graded.
[S] And that gap between the two is going to be informative, I suspect.
[G] Enormously. Third axis is explanation generation quality: BLEU, ROUGE-L, METEOR, and BERTScore computed with SciBERT embeddings, against the reference explanations. With one important guard — if the string-matching classifier judged the model's answer wrong, the generation score for that response is set to zero. So a beautifully written wrong answer cannot inflate the score.
[O] That is a good guard. Cheap and it closes an obvious hole.
[G] It closes it if the string-matching classifier is accurate, and the paper reports no precision or recall check on that classifier. Then there is a fourth axis, human evaluation, run only on that twenty-five-question development set. Three annotators with master's degrees in health-related subjects scored each answer-and-explanation pair as zero, a half, or one.
[S] Hold on. The tables report human evaluation on a zero to five scale.
[G] They do, and that mismatch is real and unexplained. The rubric described in the methods section is zero, a half, or one per item. Table six and the radar chart report per-specialty values that go up to five. The paper never spells out the aggregation step between the two. So treat the zero to five numbers as the paper's reported scale, not as something you can rederive from the rubric.

Llama two seventy B takes occupational therapy at eighty point four. ClinicalCamel is second overall at sixty-nine point three.
