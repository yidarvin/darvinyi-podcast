[G] Thank you. And the framing your skeptic just gave is the right one. The single most important sentence in this paper is not in the results. It is in the contributions list, where the first item is, quote, a dynamic evaluation protocol. The graph algorithm that everyone remembers is contribution number two. In Related Work the authors are even blunter, saying DyVal is not a detection approach but a new protocol to mitigate the contamination issue. This paper is a paradigm claim wearing a benchmark's clothes.
[O] Let's set up the gap. What was broken about evaluation in late 2023?
[G] Two things, and the paper is careful to say these are structural, not the fault of any particular benchmark. First, data contamination. Test sets are static, public, and scraped, so a model trained on ever more of the web can memorize the answer key. The paper points at the GPT-4 and LLaMA technical reports, which acknowledge the phenomenon themselves, and at the contamination-detection work that was appearing at the time. Second, fixed complexity. A benchmark built once at one difficulty stops discriminating as models improve. The paper leans on the Faith and Fate result, which showed models handle simple compositional problems well and fall apart on harder ones, and then observes that a static set has no built-in way to generate that next harder tier.
[S] There were already benchmarks calling themselves dynamic, though.
[G] DynaBench and DynaBoard, yes, and the paper's objection to them is specific rather than dismissive. Those are dynamic by crowd-sourcing. Humans keep submitting new adversarial examples. The authors call that expensive and tedious, and the deeper problem is that once you have collected the examples, you are holding a fixed artifact again. You have bought yourself some time, not changed the shape of the thing.
[O] So the pitch is: make the generator the benchmark.
[G] Precisely. The formalism is that a dynamic evaluation algorithm for a task is F of G of C. G is a generation algorithm with randomness built in. C is a constraint set with two halves, a task constraint that keeps samples valid and a complexity constraint that sets difficulty. F is a description function that turns the generated structure into natural language. Because G resamples on every call, the paper's claim is that contamination is avoided by construction rather than detected after the fact.
[S] Walk me through the instantiation, because the abstraction is easy and the instantiation is where the work is.
[G] The instantiation is graph-informed DyVal, and every problem is a directed acyclic graph. Two families cover seven tasks in three domains. Tree-based graphs, where nodes chain from leaves up to a root, handle the two mathematics tasks, arithmetic and linear equations, and the three logic tasks, boolean, deductive, and abductive. General graphs, which need genuine non-tree dependency structure, handle the two algorithm tasks, reachability and maximum sum path. The description function then linearizes the graph into English.
[O] And difficulty is the graph's shape.
[G] Directly. For tree-based graphs it is depth and width. For general graphs it is node count and maximum links per node. The four complexity levels the paper calls D one through D four are literally four parameter settings. For general graphs, seven, ten, fifteen, and twenty nodes, with three, four, six, and eight maximum links. For tree-based graphs, the depth-and-width pairs are two and two, three and two, three and three, and then four and two.
[S] Hold on. Read me that last list again.
[G] Depth two width two, depth three width two, depth three width three, depth four width two.
[S] So D four is deeper than D three but narrower. That ladder is not nested. D three and D four are not the same problem with more of it, they are two different shapes, and calling one of them harder is an assumption the parameters do not enforce.
[G] That is a fair reading of the table, and the paper does not defend the ordering explicitly. What it does defend is the direction, via the ablation. The paper's phrase is that depth emerges as the predominant challenge in tree-based graphs, which they read as difficulty with extended inference steps.

[O] Now the contamination argument. There are theorems.
[G] Two.
