[O] Start with the gap. Why does CLIPScore fail so badly?
[G] The failure is architectural, not a matter of scale. CLIP embeds an image and a text into a shared space and takes a cosine similarity between two pooled vectors. The text encoder is known to behave like a bag of words — the paper's own framing example is that it conflates "the horse is eating the grass" with "the grass is eating the horse." Same words, inverted relation, and a pooled embedding does not see the difference.
[S] And that generalizes past subject-object inversion.
[G] It generalizes to everything compositional. Attribute binding, so a blue bat and a green ball rather than the reverse. Counting. Spatial and part relations. Comparison. Negation, as in no cars crossing. Universal quantification, as in all the chairs are occupied except one. Those are exactly the constructions real prompts are full of, and they are exactly where a similarity between pooled vectors has nothing to grip.
[O] So what had people already tried?
[G] Three families. The first is divide and conquer: use a language model to decompose the prompt into simpler question-answer pairs, run a VQA model on each, average the accuracy. TIFA does this, so does VQ-squared, so does Davidsonian Scene Graph. The second family finetunes a vision-language model directly on large-scale human preference data — PickScore, ImageReward, HPSv2. The third simply prompts GPT-4V to write out an alignment score between zero and one hundred, which is what GPT4V-Eval and VIEScore do.
[S] The divide-and-conquer family is the one I'd have bet on going in. Decomposition is the standard move when a model can't handle a hard instance directly.
[G] It is, and the paper's argument is that it breaks precisely on the prompts that need it. Their example, from page seven, is the prompt "someone talks on the phone angrily while another person sits happily." Davidsonian, the most sophisticated decomposer they test, generates the questions "is the someone talking angrily?" and "is the someone talking on the phone?"
[S] Neither of which tests the thing that makes the prompt hard.
[G] Neither of which tests the binding at all. The prompt is hard because two emotions have to attach to two different people. Both generated questions are about the same person and neither one contains the other person, so a model could answer yes to both on an image that has the emotions completely swapped. The decomposition destroys the compositional structure it was supposed to isolate.
[O] Right, so let's get to the method. Imogen, walk us through what VQAScore actually computes, because I want to be precise about the part I think people gloss over.
[G] It converts the text into a single question with a fixed template — does this figure show, quote, the text, unquote, please answer yes or no — and defines the alignment score as the generative likelihood the VQA model assigns to the answer "Yes." Formally it's an auto-regressive product over the answer's tokens, conditioned on the image and the question.
[S] And that is different from asking the model and checking whether it said yes.
[G] Materially different, and this is the part people gloss. If you generate, you sample a token and compare it to the string "Yes" — you get a binary. If you score, you read the softmax distribution the answer decoder already produces and take the probability mass on "Yes" — you get a continuous number. There's also an efficiency argument the paper makes: because the whole answer sequence is available as input, those terms compute in parallel, whereas generation has to produce each token before it can condition the next.
[O] What does the continuous version buy you beyond finer granularity?
[G] It routes around a specific pathology. The paper reports that on Winoground, with Davidsonian's generated questions, LLaVA-1.5 answers "Yes" to eighty percent of the questions that should be answered "No." A yes-biased model is nearly useless as a binary judge. But the likelihood underneath that bias still carries signal — the model can be pushed over the line into saying yes while still assigning meaningfully less probability to it.
[S] Then I want the ablation, because the paper's headline framing is "simpler than prior art," and simpler is doing two jobs there: one question instead of many, and likelihood instead of generation. Those are separable.
[G] They are separable and the paper separates them, in appendix Table fifteen, holding the VQA backbone fixed at CLIP-FlanT5. Four cells. That's not an improvement, it's a point worse.
[O] So skipping decomposition, on its own, does nothing.
[G] On that cell, nothing. Now hold the questions at Davidsonian's decomposition and change only the scoring to likelihood: forty-one. And both changes together — single template, likelihood scoring — sixty.
[S] So the load-bearing choice is the scoring, not the simplicity. Twenty-five points of the lift comes from reading a likelihood, and the single template only pays off once you're already reading likelihoods.
[G] That's the correct read of that table, and I'd say it's underplayed in the paper's own prose.
