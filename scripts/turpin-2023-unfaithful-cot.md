[G] Thank you. And I want to start with a distinction the paper makes in its first two pages, because almost every misreading of this work comes from collapsing it. The distinction is between a plausible explanation and a faithful one.
[S] Give us the definitions the paper is actually using.
[G] Plausible means the explanation reads well on its own terms. It is internally coherent, it does not contradict itself, the arithmetic works, and it supports the answer that follows it. Faithful means something entirely different. Faithful means the explanation is an accurate account of the process that actually produced the prediction. The authors lean on Jacovi and Goldberg for that framing.
[O] And the observation is that the field had mostly been measuring the first one.
[G] Correct. The prior literature the paper surveys was largely finding flaws in explanations. Contradictions, mathematical errors, incoherence. That is plausibility auditing. It is necessary for faithfulness and, as this paper demonstrates, nowhere near sufficient.
[S] Here is what I want to push on immediately. Why would anyone have expected faithfulness in the first place? What is the prior that this paper is overturning?
[G] The paper argues you should not have expected it, and gives four reasons. The first is the sharpest: training objectives do not explicitly incentivize a model to accurately report the reasons for its own behavior. Nothing in next-token prediction, and nothing in a preference model, contains a term for that.
[O] Which is a training-time argument, not a capability argument.
[G] It is. The second reason is that human-written explanations, which these models are trained on, are known to be incomplete. The third is Nisbett and Wilson, from 1977, the classic psychology result that human verbal reports of our own cognitive processes are frequently confabulation. And the fourth is that RLHF specifically may disincentivize faithfulness, because it optimizes for responses that look good to a human rater.
[S] So the honest framing is that this paper is confirming a prediction that was already on the table, not surprising anyone who had thought about it.
[G] The prediction was on the table. What was not on the table was a cheap way to measure it, and that is the actual contribution.
[O] Let us go there. Because the method here is genuinely clever and I think it gets under-discussed relative to the headline number.
[G] The starting point is a framework called counterfactual simulatability, from Doshi-Velez and Kim, developed by Chen and colleagues. The question it asks is: does reading a model's explanation on one input help a human predict what the model will do on other inputs? If the explanation is a real account of the process, it should. It is a good definition and a brutally expensive one, because you must manually inspect explanations and work out their implications for counterfactual inputs.
[S] So what is the shortcut?
[G] They invert it. Instead of taking an explanation and asking what it predicts on new inputs, they engineer the counterfactual directly. Add a feature to the prompt that provably moves the model's answer. Then check whether the explanation ever mentions it. If the answer moved and the explanation never names the thing that moved it, the explanation is unfaithful by construction, and nobody has to interpret anything.
[O] Which only works if the second half is actually true. If the explanations do sometimes mention the bias, the whole argument collapses into case-by-case adjudication.
[G] Which is exactly why the paper checks it before running the experiment rather than assuming it, and this is the step I most want listeners to take away. Appendix B. For BIG-Bench Hard they hand-review three explanations from the biased context for every model, task, few-shot, and bias combination. That is two hundred and thirty-four generations. They find zero that mention the bias.

[G] Zero. [O] So the precondition holds, and comparing final predictions becomes sufficient. No proxy metrics, no judge model, no rubric.
[G] That is the design win. It streamlines the evaluation without relying on any proxy for faithfulness.
[S] Walk us through the two benchmarks, because I gather they are pulling in opposite directions and that took me a second read to see.
[G] They are, and it is the most elegant thing in the paper. For BIG-Bench Hard, they perturb a feature the explanations never reference. They perturb a feature the explanations do reference, and rely on heavily, and a faithful model should therefore be sensitive to it. [S] Fine. Implications. What does this actually change for someone building evaluation and oversight today?
[G] The paper's own answer is in the discussion, under systematic unfaithfulness as a vector for adversarial attacks, and I think it is the most durable part. If a system makes decisions on user input, a user can inject a bias in the prompt and shift the output without leaving any trace in the chain of thought. Any audit or fairness check that reads the reasoning to detect unfair reasoning can be defeated by an input that never appears in the reasoning.
[O] And the constructive half is that consistency is checkable without labels.
[S] For eval practice, my read is narrower and sharper. Stop scoring reasoning quality and calling it a faithfulness measurement.
