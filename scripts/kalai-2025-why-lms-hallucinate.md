# ---- context and problem ----
[G] Let me back up to why this needed proving at all, because the field isn't short on hallucination content. There are taxonomies of hallucination types, dozens of hallucination-specific benchmarks, whole papers on mitigation techniques. What was missing was a statistical account of why a well-trained model would produce them in the first place — and the authors open by pointing out these things are still showing up in the newest systems, citing OpenAI's own GPT-5 system card as evidence that hallucination hasn't gone away just because models got bigger.
[S] Both of the two questions we opened with, by the way, were the same model — DeepSeek-V3, six hundred billion parameters — and both happen to be about co-author Adam Kalai himself. Which is a clean trick, actually. If you're testing whether a model will confidently invent a fact instead of admitting it doesn't know one, asking about your own birthday is about as controlled as a ground truth gets.
[O] There's a third one in the paper, and it's my favorite. They ask three different models — ChatGPT running GPT-4o, DeepSeek, and Llama — what the title of Kalai's own PhD dissertation was, all accessed in May of twenty twenty-five, none of them searching the web.
[G] All three get it wrong, and in three completely different ways. ChatGPT says "Boosting, Online Algorithms, and Other Topics in Machine Learning," CMU, two thousand two. [S] So let's take them one at a time. Half one: pretraining makes some hallucination statistically unavoidable, even under ideal conditions. Half two: post-training doesn't fix it, because of how models get graded. Astrid, start with half one.
# ---- method deep-dive ----
[G] Right. The central device is something they call Is-It-Valid, IIV for short. Take every plausible thing a model could output and split it into two buckets — valid statements, and errors, meaning false but plausible-sounding ones. IIV is just the binary question "which bucket is this in," trained on a fifty-fifty mix of real training examples, labeled positive, and random draws from the error bucket, labeled negative.
[O] And a language model gets turned into an IIV classifier for free — you don't need to train anything new for that part?
[G] Exactly, you just threshold the model's own probability estimate: call something valid if the model assigns it more than one over the size of the error set, invalid otherwise. Here's the direction that's easy to get backwards, so I want to say it precisely — generating good outputs is at least as hard as answering that yes-or-no validity question, because generating well implicitly requires getting the yes-or-no answer right about everything you could possibly say. So if that classification problem has some unavoidable error rate, generation inherits it too, at roughly double the rate, minus a couple of small correction terms.
[S] Say the "minus small correction terms" part again, because that's not nothing.
[G] It's Corollary 1, the simplest case: generative error is at least twice the classification error rate, minus the ratio of valid outputs to error outputs, minus a calibration gap they call delta. Theorem 1 generalizes it to prompted models, where that single global ratio gets replaced by a worst-case, per-prompt version — the ratio of valid to error responses within whichever specific prompt is hardest, rather than one number for the whole vocabulary. That's the version that actually describes a system answering many different questions, not just producing one unconditional stream of text. And there's one assumption doing real work underneath both results: the training data has to be noiseless, zero errors in it. The paper is upfront that this is a best case — with realistic, messier data, they expect the true error rate to be even higher, which is the right direction for a lower bound to be wrong in, if it's going to be wrong at all.
[O] Okay, so that's the abstract machinery. What does it actually predict happens, concretely?
[G] Three regimes, and the letter-counting example from the cold open is one of them. DeepSeek's reasoning model, R1, spells the word out token by token in its own chain of thought — D, that's one D, E, not a D, and so on — and gets it right. The base model, V3, can't do that and guesses. Same underlying training data, roughly, different fit. That's what the paper calls a poor-model problem, not a statistical floor — a better model in the same family just solves it.
[S] So the letter-counting example doesn't actually demonstrate the theorem. It demonstrates the opposite — that particular failure is fixable.
[G] Right, it's there to draw the contrast. The theorem bites on the third regime: arbitrary facts, where there's no pattern to learn at all. Birthdays are the running example — for a given person there's exactly one correct answer, and it's essentially chosen at random, independent of everything else you know about them. For that regime the paper defines something called the singleton rate — the fraction of training prompts that show up exactly once in the training data, ignoring any that were answered "I don't know." It's built directly on Turing and Good's old missing-mass estimator, the same idea used to estimate the probability of species you haven't yet seen in an ecological survey.
[O] And the theorem says base models hallucinate at roughly that singleton rate.
[G] That's Theorem 2, and I want to be careful here, because it actually has two separate halves with two different quantifiers, and it's easy to blur them into one clean statement.
[O] Go on — my read was, nobody can beat the singleton rate, and there's an algorithm that hits it exactly, so the bound is tight and basically solved.
[G] Half right, and the half that's wrong matters. The first half is universal — for any pretraining algorithm at all, its error is at least roughly the singleton rate, with at least ninety-nine percent probability over the random training draw. That part really does apply to everyone. The second half is not "every algorithm achieves the singleton rate." It's an existence proof — they construct one specific, efficient, calibrated algorithm and show that particular algorithm's error is at most roughly the singleton rate too. That's what makes the bound tight rather than just pessimistic, but it's an existence claim about one algorithm they built, not a property of algorithms in general.
[S] So the honest one-sentence version is, nobody can do better than the singleton rate, and at least one specific method gets close to it — not "every reasonable training method automatically lands there."
[G] Exactly that. And the paper's own worked number is illustrative, not measured — if twenty percent of some category of birthday-style facts appear exactly once in training, base models should hallucinate on at least around twenty percent of questions in that category. That's a consequence of the theorem, plugged into a hypothetical, not something measured on an actual deployed model. This is also the theorem that generalizes an earlier result of Kalai and Vempala's from twenty twenty-four — the original singleton-rate bound, extended here to handle prompts and "I don't know" responses, which the earlier version didn't cover.
[O] There's a third piece too, isn't there — the multiple-choice one.
[G] Theorem 3, for the case where there's exactly one correct answer among C options and no "I don't know" allowed. The bound there is roughly two times one minus one over C, times the best error rate achievable within whatever restricted model family you're using. Corollary 2 makes it concrete with a genuinely fun example — "she lost it and was completely out of her mind" versus "he lost it and was completely out of his mind." A model that only looks at the two preceding words, a trigram model, literally cannot tell those two prompts apart, so its best possible performance is a coin flip, and its error rate on that one pair has to be at least one half.
[S] I'd flag that's a constructed pair, not a claim about trigram models on language in general.
[G] Correctly flagged — it's a single illustrative example, not "all trigram models fail half the time at everything." And to close the loop on all of this — the paper is explicit that none of these bounds say hallucination is inevitable for language models in general. Their own words are that it's inevitable "only for base models," under these specific statistical assumptions. You can trivially build a system that never hallucinates — a fixed database of known facts plus a calculator, defaulting to "I don't know" on everything else. It just won't be a very capable system.
# ---- results ----
[O] Let's get to what they actually show, versus what they prove.
[G] Fair distinction, because there's only one piece of empirical evidence in the entire paper, and it isn't even the authors' own data. Figure two is reprinted, with permission, from OpenAI's twenty twenty-three GPT-4 technical report — not something this paper's authors measured themselves.
[S] Worth pausing on that. A proof-heavy paper leaning on a chart somebody else made, from a different report entirely.
[G] Right, and it's worth being precise about what it shows. On multiple-choice questions with four options, the pretrained GPT-4 base model, before any reinforcement learning, has its stated confidence track its actual accuracy almost exactly — expected calibration error of zero point zero zero seven, essentially perfectly calibrated. After reinforcement learning, that same model's confidence badly overshoots its accuracy — calibration error jumps to zero point zero seven four, something like a tenfold increase.
[O] And the read is that pretraining's ordinary objective happens to produce good calibration as a side effect, while reinforcement learning optimizes something else entirely, with no such guarantee built in.
[G] That's the argument, and it's consistent with what the theory predicts about delta, the calibration gap. But I want to underline what I just said — this is one reused chart, one model family, one report from a different paper. It's suggestive, not a demonstrated result of this paper's own.
[S] "Consistent with the theory" and "evidence for the theory" are two different epistemic statuses, and I'd rather we not blur them.
[G] Agreed, and the paper itself doesn't claim otherwise — it reads the figure as consistent with the argument, nothing stronger.
[O] Okay, so that's the pretraining half's one piece of outside evidence. What about the post-training argument, the grading one?
[G] That one has real legwork behind it, in the form of a meta-evaluation, not a benchmark result. They looked at ten influential leaderboard evaluations — their word is "influential," not "most-cited," worth being precise about — drawn from two curated leaderboards, HELM Capabilities and the Open LLM Leaderboard, which overlap on three of their picks — MMLU-Pro, GPQA, and IFEval show up on both — plus two standalone, independently well-known benchmarks: SWE-bench and Humanity's Last Exam.
[S] Name a few of the ten.
[G] GPQA, graduate-level science questions. MMLU-Pro. IFEval, which checks whether a model followed exact formatting instructions. Omni-MATH and the hardest split of MATH, both graded by another language model for equivalence. BBH, a grab-bag of hard reasoning tasks. MuSR, multi-step reasoning. SWE-bench, real GitHub issues a model has to actually fix. And HLE, Humanity's Last Exam, deliberately built to be extremely hard across many domains.
[O] And how do they score an "I don't know"?
[G] Nine of the ten grade purely binary, right or wrong, zero credit for abstaining. The one exception is WildBench, which uses a one-to-ten rubric with some partial credit for uncertainty — except the paper points out that even there, an honest "I don't know" typically lands in the three-to-four band, below the five-to-six band given to a confidently wrong answer that's at least fluent. So even the one benchmark with partial credit still, in practice, rewards a fluent guess over an honest abstention.
[S] That's a genuinely damning detail, if it holds up.
[G] It's their own footnote reading of WildBench's public rubric, not something they ran themselves, but it's a specific, checkable claim, not a vague impression. And it's not just an artifact of these ten in isolation — several of the same names turn up unprompted in real model cards. Google's Gemini two-point-five Pro card reports GPQA, MMLU, SWE-bench, and HLE. OpenAI's own recent releases report GPQA, MMLU, SWE-bench Verified, IFEval, MATH, and HLE. These aren't obscure academic benchmarks — they're the numbers that get put in a press release.
[O] So the fix. What do they actually propose?
[G] Not a new benchmark — a scoring change to the ones already in use. Bake an explicit confidence threshold directly into each question's instructions. Their simplest worked example: tell the model, answer only if you're more than fifty percent confident, because a wrong answer costs one point, a right answer earns one point, and "I don't know" earns zero. They also give a stricter version, answer only if you're more than ninety percent confident, where a wrong answer now costs nine points against one for being right.
[S] That's just negative marking. Standardized tests have used that forever.
[G] Which the paper says explicitly — they cite it as precedent. India's JEE, NEET, and GATE exams, the AMC math competitions, and older versions of the American SAT, AP, and GRE all penalized wrong guesses relative to leaving a question blank. The twist here is making the threshold explicit in the prompt itself, so an uncertain model has a well-defined rational choice, rather than an unstated norm it has to guess at. They call the resulting policy "behavioral calibration" — answer only when your true confidence exceeds the threshold — and show it's simultaneously the optimal strategy at every threshold value, not something you'd need to retune per question.
[O] And their pitch is to retrofit this into benchmarks that already exist — SWE-bench is their own named example — rather than adding another dedicated hallucination eval to an already crowded pile.
[G] Exactly the pitch. Which is also exactly where the evidence runs out.
# ---- the debate ----
[O] Quick question before we get into the debate proper — doesn't retrieval or search already solve a lot of this? Plenty of deployed models ground their answers in search results now.
[G] The paper actually addresses that directly, in a section literally called "search and reasoning are not panaceas." Retrieval can reduce some hallucinations, sure, but the binary-grading incentive doesn't care how you got your answer — the observation that an optimal test-taker never benefits from abstaining under binary grading holds for any model, retrieval-augmented or not. And search doesn't help with something like the letter-counting mistake anyway, since that's a computation error, not a missing fact you could look up.
[S] Fair — so it's orthogonal to the fix they're actually proposing, not competing with it.
[G] Right, complementary at best. Okay, now go ahead and make your case.
[O] Let me make the strongest version of why this matters, before we tear into it. If the diagnosis is right, this is one of the cheapest high-leverage fixes I've seen proposed in a while. Nobody has to train a new model, invent a new architecture, or collect new data. You're asking a benchmark maintainer to add one sentence to their instructions and change a scoring formula. That's an afternoon of engineering work against a problem the whole field has been throwing enormous compute at.
[S] I'll grant it's cheap to try. My problem is that nobody has tried it. Nowhere in this paper does a model actually get scored under explicit confidence thresholds and come out hallucinating less. Section four's derivation of when abstaining beats guessing is, in their own words, "a simple calculation" — algebra, not an experiment.
[G] That's accurate. The proposal is analytically motivated and empirically untested, and the paper doesn't claim otherwise.
[S] Second issue, and I think it's the bigger one — "benchmarks cause hallucination to persist" is asserted, and I don't think it's separated from the obvious alternative, that binary scoring and unreduced hallucination are both just downstream of the same cause. Binary accuracy is cheap to compute and easy to put on a leaderboard. Maybe that's why it dominates, independent of any causal effect on model behavior, and rescoring wouldn't move the underlying number at all.
[G] The paper doesn't rule that out, no. What it has is a mechanism that's true by construction — one short observation that under any binary grader, an optimal test-taker never benefits from abstaining, correct and almost trivial to prove — plus the inventory of how ten real benchmarks actually grade. That's a real mechanism and a real inventory. It isn't a causal experiment.
[O] I'll push back a little there. "A real mechanism, plus a documented inventory of the actual incentive" is still meaningfully more than a hand-wavy guess. It's just not proof.
[S] Sure, call it a strong hypothesis, not a demonstrated cause. Third thing — the paper names the trade-off and doesn't size it. Section four-point-one says weighing accuracy more heavily than comprehensiveness is what reduces hallucination, fine, but by how much comprehensiveness do you give up? If a behaviorally-calibrated model withholds a lot of otherwise-useful answers to hit that bar, that's a real cost nobody's measured.
[G] Also accurate, and also not something the paper attempts. It names the trade-off in one sentence and moves on. They do flag a related limitation themselves, though, in their own discussion section — their own phrase is "a false trichotomy." Correct, incorrect, and I-don't-know doesn't capture degree of error either; a wildly wrong guess and an almost-right one both just count as "incorrect," with no partial credit for getting close. Their defense is that a false trichotomy still beats a false dichotomy, right or wrong with no third option at all, but they're upfront it's a simplification, not a complete account of uncertainty.
[O] Fourth thing, and then I want to hear the case for the theory half too.
[S] The ten-benchmark sample. Two curated leaderboards plus two standalone giants isn't an audit of the field, it's a convenience sample of exactly ten — and the paper's own text half-concedes it. They mention, in passing, that BBQ, which sits on HELM's own safety leaderboard, already allows "I don't know" as an answer. So "nine of ten" describes these ten specific, highest-visibility evaluations, not evaluation practice broadly.
[G] Right, and to the paper's credit, it doesn't claim otherwise either — it calls these "influential," never "most" or "representative." But I agree the framing invites a broader reading than the sampling really supports.
[O] Okay, now let me hear you go after the theory half, because I think it holds up better.
[S] It mostly does, and I'll say that plainly. My one real complaint there is the noiseless-data assumption. Real pretraining corpora aren't error-free, and the fifty-fifty mixture used to define the classification problem is a modeling choice, not something measured off an actual training run. The paper's own defense, that messier data should only push the true error rate higher, is the right direction for a lower bound to be wrong in, so I don't think it's fatal. But nothing in the paper connects the singleton rate or the classification error rate to an actual number from an actual trained model. It's a real bound under stated conditions, not a measured account of any specific model's hallucination rate.
[G] I'd add one more fact, stated plainly, not as an accusation — three of the four authors, Kalai, Nachum, and Zhang, are at OpenAI. Vempala is at Georgia Tech. And the paper's central recommendation is that the field change how it scores the exact leaderboards those same authors' employer's models are measured against. Worth knowing as you weigh the recommendation, independent of whether the argument itself is sound.
[O] Which cuts both ways, honestly — they'd also be the ones best positioned to actually run this experiment first, if they wanted to.
[S] Agreed. It'll be worth watching whether they do.
# ---- implications ----
[G] Step back, and the interesting move here isn't really about hallucination specifically — it's a claim about what leaderboards reward. Any time a benchmark reduces a nuanced judgment to a single right-or-wrong score, it's implicitly telling a model that a confident wrong answer beats an honest "I don't know." That tension shows up anywhere binary grading meets genuine uncertainty, not just in factual recall.
[O] Which is why I think this is bigger than a hallucination paper, even though that's the framing. If benchmark maintainers actually picked this up, you'd change the incentive structure across a big chunk of how models get evaluated and marketed, for the cost of a sentence in a prompt template.
[S] "If" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Whoever's leaderboard number would drop first has the least incentive to switch first. That's a coordination problem, and the paper calls it one — "socio-technical mitigation" is their own phrase for it, not mine.
[G] It is, and the honest reading is that the paper has identified a plausible lever without yet showing anyone's hand on it. The obvious next experiment is sitting right there — run the SWE-bench version they already sketch, measure what happens to accuracy and abstention, and publish it.
[O] Which would take one team, one benchmark, one afternoon of prompt-writing, and an evaluation run they were probably going to do anyway.
[S] Cheap enough that "nobody's done it yet" is itself a small data point worth noticing. And one caveat before we wrap this thread — the paper itself is clear that "I don't know" is a blunt instrument. Their own discussion section lists hedging, omitting details, and asking a clarifying question as other ways a model could signal uncertainty besides a flat abstention, and says explicit confidence targets are a practical simplification, not the final word on how uncertainty should be communicated.
# ---- outro ----
[G] If you remember one thing from this paper, remember that it's two separate claims, not one. Some hallucination is a mathematical consequence of training on arbitrary facts with ordinary statistical methods — that part is proven. And it likely survives post-training because the benchmarks grading that post-training give zero credit for honesty — that part is argued, and still untested.
[O] My takeaway is optimism with a task attached — this might be the cheapest real fix anyone's proposed for a hard problem in a long time, and it's sitting there waiting for someone to actually run it.
