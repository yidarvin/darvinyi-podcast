[O] A population-based reinforcement learning agent trained for twenty billion steps of Hanabi scores twenty two point seven three out of twenty five playing with itself. That is competent play by any standard you want to name.
[S] And when you sit one of those agents down with a stranger trained by the identical algorithm on a different random seed, it scores one point three two. Not thirteen. One point three two, out of twenty five.
[O] Which I want to defend as a result rather than a failure. The self-play number was never a lie. It was measuring something other than the ability to play Hanabi, and nobody had built the instrument that could tell the difference.
[S] It was measuring how well an agent plays with a copy of itself. That turns out to be a very different quantity, and essentially the entire multi-agent benchmark literature of that era reported only the first one.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio. Today we are on The Hanabi Challenge: A New Frontier for AI Research, from Nolan Bard, Jakob Foerster, and thirteen colleagues across DeepMind, Oxford, Google Brain, and Carnegie Mellon. It went up on arXiv in 2019 and was published in the journal Artificial Intelligence.
[S] It is on the docket because challenge papers age in a brutally simple way. Either the community adopts the problem or it quietly does not. This one got adopted, and I want to understand which part of it earned that.
[O] Our guest is Rosalind, who has studied this paper and the work around it closely. Rosalind, welcome. Start us with the gap. By 2019 games had been the engine of AI progress for thirty years. Why did anyone need another one?
[G] Because every headline game victory up to that point sat in one of two boxes. Single agent problems, like the Atari suite. Or adversarial two-player zero-sum games, chess, checkers, backgammon, go, and heads-up poker. Hanabi is in neither box, and the authors are precise about why that matters technically rather than just aesthetically.
[S] Give me the technical version.
[G] In a two-player zero-sum game you can compute an equilibrium and get a meaningful worst-case guarantee, because different equilibria in those games are interchangeable. Each player can play their part of a different equilibrium profile without hurting their own utility. Hanabi is purely cooperative and plays two through five, so neither property holds. The value of your policy depends entirely on what your teammates are actually doing, and there can be multiple locally optimal equilibria that are simply much worse than the best one.
[O] And those inferior equilibria are exactly what independent learners get stuck in.
[G] That is the paper's argument, yes. It even names the degenerate case in a footnote. A pooling or babbling equilibrium, where nobody intentionally signals anything and everybody ignores what others do. There is no incentive to start communicating, because you would be ignored, and no incentive to listen, because nobody is saying anything.
[S] Before the method, walk me through the game itself. I want to know exactly what makes the information structure hard, not just that it is hard.
[G] Fifty cards. Five colours, and in each colour three ones, two each of twos, threes, and fours, and a single five. Each player holds four cards, five if you are playing with two or three players, and they face outward. So you see everybody's hand except your own. The goal is to build five stacks in ascending order, one per colour, and the score is the number of cards on the stacks, maximum twenty five.
[O] So the imperfect information is not about a hidden opponent. It is about yourself.
[G] Precisely, and that inversion is what makes it unusual. On your turn you do exactly one of three things. Give a hint, which names a rank or a colour and points out every card of that rank or colour in one partner's hand. Play a card. Or discard one. Hints cost information tokens from a shared pool that starts at eight, discarding recovers one, and a failed play costs one of three shared lives.
[S] And the token budget is the binding constraint.
[G] It is, and the paper does the arithmetic explicitly. Because of how many cards must be held rather than discarded, players can hint at most twenty nine times in an entire game, and fewer as the player count rises. There are twenty five cards you need to play. So there is provably not enough grounded information to identify them all through the literal content of hints.
[O] Which forces the interesting thing. You have to read why a partner chose that hint, not just what the hint said.
[G] That is the whole game. The paper's worked example is the finesse. You give a hint that, taken literally, causes one player to make a wrong inference and play a card they should not. The player sitting between you sees that about to happen, asks why you would give such an apparently irrational hint, and concludes the only rational explanation, which is that they themselves hold the card that rescues the play. Two cards played for one token.
[S] I want to flag how strong a claim that is. Humans do this on their first game, with no conventions agreed in advance?
[G] The paper claims something close to that. Beginners with no experience immediately start signalling playable cards, reasoning that from their teammates' perspective they cannot know it themselves. And beginners confidently play cards that are only partially identified, because they read the intent behind the partial identification. The authors are careful to call this hard to quantify. It is an argument for the domain, not a measured result.
[O] Rosalind, the other thing that distinguishes this from the emergent communication literature is that there is no side channel.
[G] Right, and that is the sharpest contrast the paper draws. Work like Foerster and colleagues on learning to communicate, Lewis and colleagues on negotiation dialogues, the emergent communication line generally, gives agents a separate cheap talk channel that is disjoint from environment actions. In Hanabi every signal has to be carried by an action that also has to accomplish something in the world. The authors say it is closer in spirit to the bidding round in bridge.
[S] Fine. So they propose the domain. What do they actually ship?
[G] Three things. The Hanabi Learning Environment, an open source Python and C plus plus framework with an interface like OpenAI Gym. Then two explicitly separate challenges with recommended protocols. Self-play, and ad-hoc team play.
[O] And the environment is cheap, which I think is underrated.
[G] About a tenth of a millisecond per turn on a CPU. That is deliberate. It makes Hanabi usable as a testbed for sample-hungry methods without a datacenter. The default observation also includes remembered card knowledge from previous hints, both positive and negative, which strips out the memory task. There is a minimal observation option if you want the memory challenge back.
[S] Two self-play regimes, correct?
[G] Correct. Sample limited caps you at one hundred million environment steps, modelled directly on the Machado and colleagues evaluation protocol for Atari. Unlimited has no cap and is about asymptotic performance, though they ask you to report compute. And for each player count they want training curves for every seed, a score histogram, the percentage of perfect games, and means over at least one thousand trials.
[O] And the ad-hoc protocol.
[G] The agent gets ten self-play games of its future teammates to observe, then plays a single game in one of their seats, then is reset so nothing carries across trials. At least one thousand trials, and at least one hundred different sets of those ten observation games. They also recommend publishing a crosstable of the held-out pool playing with each other, so a reader can judge whether the pool is actually diverse.
[S] Let us get to agents. Who is in the comparison?
[G] Two families. On the hand-coded side, four bots that were all built independently of this paper by the community. SmartBot tracks what is publicly known about every player's cards, so it can reason about what a partner already knows and avoid making them spend a hint on it. FireFlower implements human-style conventions plus a two-ply search over an evaluation function that accounts for belief state as well as board state.
[O] And then the two that get described as hat puzzles.
[G] HatBot and WTFWThat, and it is worth keeping these straight because they are not the same idea. HatBot uses the recommendation strategy from Cox and colleagues. It works out a recommended action for every other player, sums the indices, and encodes that joint recommendation with modular arithmetic into a single hint. Since you can see everyone else's cards, you can reconstruct what was recommended to the others and subtract out your own.
[S] So HatBot's hint says do this, not you hold that.
[G] Exactly. WTFWThat is a variant of the other strategy in that same work, the information strategy, which encodes each player's own card information directly rather than a recommended action. Cox and colleagues note that the information strategy requires extra bookkeeping that makes it impractical for humans, and the original versions were tailored for five players. WTFWThat is the variant that plays two through five.
[O] And the learning side.
[G] ACHA, the Actor-Critic-Hanabi-Agent, which is an importance-weighted actor-learner in the IMPALA lineage with V-trace, plus population-based training. Population of thirty to fifty, one hundred parallel actors, observations through a two hundred fifty six unit hidden layer into a two-layer LSTM with two hundred fifty six units per layer. Trained to twenty billion steps, which they estimate at roughly one hundred CPU years.
[S] And that is the unlimited regime entry.
[G] Yes. Rainbow is the sample-limited entry, a feedforward distributional DQN from the Dopamine framework, two layers of five hundred twelve units, fifty one atoms, one hundred million steps in about seven days on a single V100. And then BAD, the Bayesian Action Decoder, which is two-player only. Instead of leaning on an RNN for an implicit belief, BAD maintains a public belief and conditions the Bayesian update directly on the acting agent's current policy, and explores in the space of deterministic policies.
[O] Numbers. Give me the self-play table, and I want the reader-beware version.
[G] The reader-beware is important, so let me state it first. Table one has four separate columns, two through five players, and they are four different games. Hand sizes differ, token pressure differs, the coordination problem differs. Comparing a number in one column to a number in another is not meaningful.
[S] Noted, and I will hold you to it.
[G] In the two-player column, BAD is the best entry in the paper, at twenty three point nine two with fifty eight point six percent perfect games. Above SmartBot at twenty two point nine nine, FireFlower at twenty two point five six, and ACHA at twenty two point seven three. WTFWThat is only nineteen point four five at two players, with under a third of a percent perfect.
[O] So the hat scheme is actually bad at two players.
[G] It is, and it is spectacular everywhere else. Twenty four point two zero at three, twenty four point eight three at four, twenty four point eight nine at five, with eighty seven and ninety one percent perfect games at four and five respectively. That is essentially solving the game in those seats.
[S] And the learning agents against that.
[G] Neither Rainbow nor ACHA reaches SmartBot in two players, and neither reaches any hand-coded bot once there are more than two players. ACHA gets twenty point two four at three, twenty one point five seven at four, and sixteen point eight at five with zero percent perfect games. Rainbow is behind ACHA at every count, twenty point six four, eighteen point seven one, eighteen, and fifteen point two six.
[O] So the honest headline of the self-play half is that a hundred CPU years of population-based training does not beat community bots people wrote as a hobby.
[S] Which I would call a genuinely humbling result and also a slightly soft one. ACHA had twenty billion steps and Rainbow had one hundred million. That comparison is not clean.
[G] The paper agrees with you. It says the ACHA advantage may simply be more experience, and may also be architectural, since Rainbow is feedforward with no history of past actions beyond the last one, which plausibly makes multi-step conventions harder to learn.
[O] What about the variance across seeds? That is the part I always find more diagnostic than a mean.
[G] It is the most revealing figure in the self-play section. When they disable population evolution and run fifty independent two-player trials, the runs land in a wide array of different local minima, and roughly a third of them plateau below fifteen points and stop improving. And runs with similar final scores learn genuinely different conventions. One agent's colour hints mean your fourth card is probably discardable. Another agent's colour hints mean which card to play.
[S] And Rainbow?
[G] The opposite. Low variance across seeds, converging on nearly identical strategies. Rainbow agents hint for colour three to four times less often than ACHA, with no evidence of colour-specific conventions at all. They hint for rank, essentially always meaning your most recent card is playable. The authors speculate this is the one-step memory shrinking the strategy space, plus value-based learning starting everyone from effectively the same near-zero Q-values.
[O] Now the ad-hoc crosstable, which is the reason we are talking about this paper at all.
[G] The pool is the ten best independently trained ACHA agents plus the best Rainbow agent. Every diagonal entry in the two-player crosstable is above twenty, from twenty point five two up to twenty two point eight four. Cell i j is agent i's mean over one thousand games playing with agent j, and standard error is under nine hundredths of a point.
[S] And off the diagonal.
[G] Collapse. Agent three scores twenty one point six seven with itself and one hundredth of a point with agent one. Agent eight scores twenty point seven with itself, and against every one of its ten possible partners it scores a tenth of a point or less.
[O] Give me the arithmetic on agent one specifically, because this is where the table can mislead you.
[G] Agent one is the best self-play agent in the pool at twenty two point eight four. The printed average column for its row reads three point two seven, but that average includes the self-play diagonal cell. If you average only its ten actual stranger pairings, it is one point three two. The printed number understates the collapse by blending the good case back in.
[S] That is a real trap, and I want to be fair to the authors. They are not hiding it. The diagonal is labelled.
[G] They are not hiding it, no. And the four-player table tells the same story with slightly less extreme numbers. Diagonals run from about seventeen point eight to twenty point six, most off-diagonal cells sit between a tenth of a point and five, and the single best cross-agent cell in the whole table is seven point two eight.
[O] Here is my optimist case, and I will make it narrow. The contribution is not that agents are bad at Hanabi. It is that a metric everyone trusted had almost no construct validity, and this paper built the cheap instrument that shows it. Self-play score and stranger score are nearly uncorrelated in this pool. That generalizes far past a card game.
[S] I will grant most of that and then take something back. There is a confound in the ad-hoc result that changes its interpretation. Rosalind, do ACHA and Rainbow actually use the ten observation games the protocol offers?
[G] They do not. The paper says so directly. Neither agent makes use of sample play of its ad-hoc teammates, so the ad-hoc score depends purely on whether the two protocols happen to be compatible.
[S] Then the crosstable is not measuring failure to adapt. It is measuring protocol collision between agents that cannot adapt at all, because nothing in their architecture reads the partner. That is a weaker claim than agents cannot learn to play with strangers.
[G] That is a fair reading, and the authors themselves scope it that way. They say the purpose of the section is primarily to illustrate the difficulty and to suggest a way of building a diverse pool for future evaluation.
[O] I will concede the mechanism and hold the conclusion. Even as protocol collision, it demolishes the idea that self-play score alone tells you anything about compatibility. That was the operative assumption in the field.
[S] Agreed, and here is my second complaint, which is about the pool. Eleven agents drawn from exactly two algorithms, ten of which are seeds of the same one. That is not a diverse held-out pool by the paper's own recommendation.
[G] The paper concedes the point and gives its reason, which is more embarrassing than principled. The hand-coded bots were excluded because none of them interface with the learning environment or are even written in the same language. They tried pairing ACHA with SmartBot and the games always ended with SmartBot crashing, because ACHA makes moves SmartBot assumes are impossible. Simple fixes only produced a score of zero.
[S] So the interesting cross-family comparison does not exist, and the closest thing to a data point is a crash.
[G] Correct. And they then assume, in their own words without further evidence, that other hand-coded combinations would be equally bad.
[O] What about comparing to the prior Hanabi literature? There was a competition and a body of work before this.
[G] There was, and there is a scoring incompatibility you have to know about. This paper follows the published rules, so losing all three lives scores zero. Most prior Hanabi research scored a failed game as the number of cards played before failure. That makes the numbers non-comparable, and the authors say the difference has considerable impact specifically in the ad-hoc setting.
[S] Which numbers does that put out of reach?
[G] Bouzy reported twenty four point nine two and perfect games ninety two percent of the time at five players, though that was while ignoring the rule that you cannot hint an empty set of cards. And the 2018 competition at the Computational Intelligence in Games conference had a winner at twenty point five seven and a second place at seventeen point five two, averaged across two through five players. None of those sit on the same scale as table one.
[O] Rosalind, one more on independence. DeepMind authors designed the benchmark, the protocol, and also built ACHA and BAD, the two strongest learning agents in the paper. Does that worry you?
[G] Structurally it should, and in this case the mitigation is unusual. The paper's narrative is built around how badly its own agents underperform bots the authors did not write. BAD is the one self-serving-looking result, and even there they explicitly refuse the strong claim, saying BAD was only tested on two-player Hanabi and one small synthetic game so they cannot argue it is a general algorithmic improvement rather than something that is good at Hanabi.
[S] That hedge is more than most papers offer, and I will say so.
[O] Implications, then. What changes if you take this seriously?
[G] The concrete methodological ask is that cooperative multi-agent work report cross-play, not only self-play. And the deeper point is about what a high self-play score even certifies. It certifies that a protocol exists and that the agent can execute its half of it. It certifies nothing about whether that protocol is one any other agent, or any human, could join.
[S] Which is the evaluation lesson I would take out of the room. A metric can be perfectly reliable, one thousand trials, standard error under a tenth of a point, and still be measuring the wrong construct. Reliability is not validity.
[O] And note how cheap the fix was. Ten already-trained agents, a crosstable, a tenth of a millisecond per turn. The instrument that overturned the field's read of its own results cost almost nothing to build.
[S] My takeaway is that the self-play half of this paper is the ordinary part and the crosstable is the paper. Learning agents losing to hobbyist bots is a story we had heard. Learning agents scoring one point with a sibling is not.
[G] Mine is the authors' own framing. They argue Hanabi puts reasoning about the beliefs and intentions of other agents in the foreground, and their evidence is that methods with no such reasoning can reach twenty two or twenty three points in self-play and still fail to collaborate at all with an unfamiliar partner.
[O] And mine is that this is the rare benchmark that is more valuable for what it falsifies than for what it scores. The full writeup, with the table, both crosstables, and the figures, is on the litsearch site.
