[S] It is on the docket because it is the closest thing cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning ever got to an Arcade Learning Environment, and because it is a very clean case study in what happens when the people who build the benchmark also wrote the method that wins it.
[O] Our guest is Alistair, who has studied this work and the line of research around it closely. Alistair, welcome.
[G] Thank you. One piece of bookkeeping first, because it confuses people. The atlas files this under AAMAS 2019, and the arXiv version I read, version five from December 2019, carries an acceptance line for the deep reinforcement learning workshop at NeurIPS 2019. Same work, two venues, and the arXiv version is the one carrying the full results table.
[S] Noted. Now give me the gap. Why did the field need a new environment at all in 2019?
[G] Because single-agent RL had shared yardsticks and cooperative multi-agent RL did not. The Arcade Learning Environment for discrete control, MuJoCo for continuous control. In multi-agent land the pattern was that each new algorithm arrived with its own bespoke environment, and the paper is blunt about the failure mode. Those one-off environments can be overly simple, or tuned to the very algorithm being proposed.
[O] So you cannot tell whether the new method is better or just better matched to the sandbox its authors built for it.
[G] Exactly that. And the authors name the specific cluster of work they mean. Foerster and colleagues on COMA, Rashid and colleagues on QMIX, Sunehag and colleagues on value decomposition networks, Lowe and colleagues on MADDPG. Four important papers, four different test beds.
[S] There were existing multi-agent testbeds though. Why were none of them good enough?
[G] Each one was missing a piece. Keepaway Soccer and its Half Field Offense extension run on the RoboCup simulator with simplified two-dimensional physics, and the paper's objection is that neither scales in difficulty with the number of agents, because most agents have very little coordination to do. The MADDPG environments are simple grid worlds aimed at low-level continuous control. The social dilemma environments from Leibo and colleagues were never released as code at all.
[O] And the many-agent work? There were frameworks scaling to hundreds or millions of agents by then.
[G] There were, from Yang and colleagues and Zheng and colleagues, but they deliberately keep dynamics and the control space simple so that many-agent training stays tractable. They are targeting emergent collective behaviour, not partial observability. Pommerman, from Resnick and colleagues, is the closest in spirit and is still a two-dimensional grid world.
[S] So the claimed gap is one specific conjunction. Partial observability, plus genuinely hard dynamics, plus high-dimensional observations, all in the same environment.
[G] That is the claim, yes, and I think it is a fair one for 2019.
[O] And the answer is to borrow a game that already has all three. But I want to be precise here, because people get this wrong constantly. This is not AlphaStar. This is not playing StarCraft.
[G] It is emphatically not. The paper says so directly, twice. Full StarCraft is macromanagement plus micromanagement, driven by a single centralised controller, which is the SC2LE and AlphaStar line of work. SMAC throws away macromanagement entirely and isolates micromanagement, the fine-grained control of individual units in a fight.
[S] And then does the thing that makes it a multi-agent problem.
[G] Right. Every allied unit becomes its own independent learning agent that sees only a local neighbourhood of the battlefield. The authors are explicit that their agents are, in their words, excessively restricted compared to normal gameplay, and that SMAC is not intended to train agents for the full game. They are using the StarCraft engine as a physics simulator for decentralised coordination problems.
[O] Take me through the formalism, because the observability details are where the design lives.
[G] Each scenario is a decentralised, partially observable Markov decision process. There is a true global state, a joint action space, a transition function, one shared team reward for everybody, an observation function, an agent count and a discount. Every agent conditions its policy only on its own action-observation history. Training is centralised, execution is decentralised, so the learning algorithm may look at the global state and all agents' histories, but the deployed policy sees only its own local view.
[S] That split is the whole point of COMA and QMIX, so it is the right frame. What does an agent actually see?
[G] Within a sight range, which is set to nine for every agent on every map, it observes distance, relative x, relative y, health, shield and unit type for each ally and each enemy in view. Plus the last actions of allies in view, and eight terrain samples around it giving height and walkability.
[O] And the shooting range is deliberately shorter than the sight range.
[G] Six versus nine. That is one of the more elegant design choices in the paper. An agent can see targets it cannot yet hit, so it has to issue movement commands to close the gap before it can fire, rather than sniping from wherever it happens to be standing. It also blocks the built-in attack-move macro from doing the work.
[S] Alistair, I want to push on the observation vector, because I read the appendix. The features you just listed are not the features they actually ran.
[G] That is correct, and well spotted. Appendix C says the experiments use all the features from the main text except three. The last actions of allied units, terrain height, and walkability. So the observation space described in the body of the paper is richer than the one the reported baselines were trained on.
[S] Which matters, because two of the fourteen maps are explicitly about terrain.
[G] The corridor scenario, yes, six Zealots against twenty-four Zerglings, where the intended solution is to collectively wall off a choke point. The baselines are attempting that without the terrain height and walkability channels. The paper does not flag the tension, and I think it should have.
[O] Let me get the action space and reward on the record before we go further.
[G] Four movement directions, an attack action per enemy unit id, a stop, and a no-op that dead agents are restricted to. Medivac healer units get a heal action per ally instead of attack. The number of legal actions ranges from seven up to seventy depending on how many enemies are on the map.
[O] Seventy actions for two Colossi against sixty-four Zerglings.
[G] That is exactly the map that pushes it, and it is the reason that scenario is hard for a completely different reason from the others. The reward by default is shaped, not sparse. Positive reward equal to hit-point damage dealt each step, a bonus of ten for each enemy killed, two hundred for winning the battle, then everything rescaled so the maximum achievable return on any scenario is around twenty. There is a sparse plus one minus one alternative offered, but every reported experiment uses the shaped version.
[S] Who is the opponent?
[G] The game's own built-in scripted AI, set to difficulty level seven, described in the game as very difficult. One fixed opponent policy at one fixed difficulty for the entire benchmark.
[S] One opponent. No diversity, no adversarial curriculum, no difficulty sweep.
[G] None reported. And there is a sentence in Appendix A.2 about this that reads like it has lost a negation. It says their experiments suggest the difficulty setting does significantly impact the micromanagement of the built-in heuristics, in a paragraph whose whole point is that the setting is not doing much work. Either way, no ablation across difficulty levels appears in the paper.
[O] There is another asymmetry in there I liked, which is honest and slightly awkward. They disable the game's automatic retaliation, but only on their own side.
[G] Right. In normal StarCraft an idle unit under attack fires back automatically without an order. The authors clone the allied units with two attributes changed, acquire level set to passive and response set to no response, so that allied behaviour comes entirely from the learned policy. Enemy units keep their default auto-response. That is defensible, since the point is to stop the game AI from doing the allies' job for them, but it does mean the two armies are not running identical unit definitions even on the maps labelled symmetric.
[O] Say a word about the fourteen scenarios, because they are not fourteen variations on one theme.
[G] Three design families. Symmetric battles, identical armies on both sides, like two Stalkers and three Zealots against the same, which get interesting when unit types counter each other and agents have to learn to protect the vulnerable teammate. Asymmetric battles, where the enemy simply has more units, five Marines against six, which forces agents to track enemy health so they focus fire without overkill. And micro-trick scenarios, each of which has one specific tactic as its intended solution.
[S] Kiting being the canonical one.
[G] Three Stalkers against five Zealots. Zealots counter Stalkers in melee, so the only winning policy is to kite the enemy around the map for at least a hundred timesteps and kill them one at a time. That produces a genuinely delayed reward problem, which is a different failure mode again from the action-space blowup or the terrain puzzle.
[O] And separately from all fourteen maps, they released a codebase.
[G] Yes, and please keep these separate, because they get conflated constantly. SMAC is the environment, in one repository. PyMARL is a modular PyTorch framework for deep multi-agent RL, in a different repository. PyMARL is where QMIX, QTRAN and COMA live as the state-of-the-art implementations, with value decomposition networks and independent Q-learning as baselines. You can use one without the other.
[S] Good. Results. What is the evaluation protocol, precisely?
[G] Training pauses every ten thousand timesteps and runs thirty-two test episodes with exploration off and action selection greedy and decentralised. The fraction of those episodes where every enemy unit dies inside the time limit is the test win rate. Five independent runs per configuration, and they report the median with a twenty-fifth to seventy-fifth percentile band, deliberately using median rather than mean so outlier seeds do not dominate.
[S] Five seeds, percentile bands, no significance test.
[G] No significance test. And there is a subtlety in the results table caption that people skate past. The reported final number is not the value at the end of training. It is the maximum median across the testing intervals within the last two hundred and fifty thousand training steps.
[S] So it is a best-of over a window. That is mildly optimistic on every single number in that table.
[G] It is, uniformly, so the ranking is probably unaffected, but the absolute values are a ceiling rather than an endpoint. Worth knowing.

[O] So the Easy tier is essentially saturated for the two best methods on day one.
[G] Effectively, and the authors say the honest thing about it, which is that the Easy maps serve as sanity checks when you are implementing a new algorithm rather than as a research frontier.
[S] Hard tier.
[G] Four maps, four different reasons to be hard. Two Colossi against sixty-four Zerglings is the action-space monster, and QMIX gets fifty-eight there against twenty-one for value decomposition. The Baneling mirror match inverts the whole ranking, independent Q-learning gets ninety-nine while everything else struggles with large variance, including QMIX at eighty-five. Five Marines against six needs precise control and both leaders plateau at seventy. And the kiting map, three Stalkers against five Zealots, where value decomposition gets ninety-one and QMIX eighty-seven.
[O] I like that the Baneling map breaks the ordering. A benchmark where the favourite loses to the simplest baseline on one map is doing something right.
[S] Now Super Hard, which is where I think the actual result of this paper lives.
[G] Five maps. Only QMIX makes meaningful progress, and only on two of them, forty-nine percent on twenty-seven Marines against thirty, and sixty-nine percent on the Medivac map, M M M two. Six Hydralisks against eight Zealots, three percent. Corridor, one percent.
[S] So one extra enemy Zealot takes you from ninety-seven percent to two percent.
[G] That is the comparison, and it is startling. The authors hypothesise that exploration is the bottleneck on those maps and hand them to the community as open problems.
[O] There are two smaller experiments worth a line each.

Separately there is a recurrence ablation. [O] That ablation is the cleanest evidence in the paper that the maps test different things.

[O] Alistair, what is the fairest way to judge this paper?
[G] Judge it as what it says it is, which is narrow and well specified. It is not StarCraft. It is decentralised micromanagement under partial observability, with one scripted opponent, fixed starts and a shaped reward. Against that scope the paper delivers a diverse, honestly reported suite plus a separate framework, and it is candid about what nobody could solve. The overreach is not in the environment, it is in the difficulty taxonomy being treated as more objective than it is.
