[O] So take us inside the box. What is QuestEval actually made of?
[G] Two fine-tuned T5 models, shared across both directions. A question generator, which takes a text and an answer span and produces a question. And a question answerer, which takes a text and a question and extracts an answer. The answer spans it conditions on are simply all the named entities and nouns in the text, following Wang and colleagues. The one architectural detail that matters more than it looks is that the QA model has an explicit unanswerable token among its outputs.
[S] Why does that matter more than it looks?
[G] Because the entire recall direction depends on it. You are asking questions derived from a long source document of a short summary. Most of those questions the summary simply will not address. If your QA model is forced to always emit a span, it will confabulate one, and your recall signal is noise. The unanswerable token is what lets not answering be a first-class prediction rather than a failure.
[O] And there is a filtering step before any of the scoring happens.
[G] There is, and it is a nice piece of hygiene. For each candidate answer they generate a question by beam search, and then they throw away every question for which their own QA model, given the same text the question came from, fails to recover the original answer. So the notation in the paper, QG of T, means only the question-answer pairs that survive that self-consistency check. If the generator produced a question so vague or so garbled that the answerer cannot get back to the answer from the very passage it came from, that question never enters the metric.
[S] Give me the precision score exactly.
[G] For every question generated from the summary, you take the answer the QA model produces when it is conditioned on the source document, and you compute F one overlap between that answer and the original answer from the summary. Then you average over all the summary's questions. The logic is that a summary which hallucinates will generate questions whose answers the source contradicts or lacks. That formulation is, in the paper's own words, the framework concurrently proposed by Wang and colleagues and Durmus and colleagues. They are not claiming precision as novel.
[O] And recall inverts the direction.
[G] Questions generated from the source, answered against the summary. But here is where it gets interesting, because recall does not use F one. It uses one minus the model's predicted probability that the question is unanswerable given the summary. And the reason is a genuinely good argument that I think generalises well beyond this paper.
[S] Which is?
[G] That an answer can be correct and phrased differently. The paper's example is A C L versus Association for Computational Linguistics. Token overlap between those two strings is zero, so F one gives you zero, and you have just penalised a summary for being right. Answerability sidesteps the surface form entirely. But, and the paper is explicit about this trade, answerability cannot detect a summary that answers the question confidently and wrongly. So they use answerability for recall, where the failure you care about is omission, and F one for precision, where the failure you care about is contradiction and you need true negatives. Different failure modes, different scoring functions. That is a more careful piece of metric design than the paper gets credit for.
[O] Then the weighter, which is the actual novel component.
[G] The actual novel component. In the prior recall metric every source question carries weight one.
