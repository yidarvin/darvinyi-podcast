# ---- context and problem ----
[G] The setup is pretty stark. Multi-agent systems — several LLM-driven agents coordinating through role assignments, message passing, and shared state — have quietly become the default architecture for coding assistants, research agents, anything that needs more than one step. The paper's opening finding is that despite all the enthusiasm, performance gains over a single well-prompted model are often minimal.
[O] And when they do fail, there's historically been no shared way to talk about why. You get one pass or fail number, and if you want to know what actually went wrong, you're reading a raw execution trace by hand — and these traces can run past fifteen thousand lines.
[S] Which is a real bottleneck. Two different systems can fail for the exact same underlying reason, and nobody has a name for that reason, so nobody notices the pattern. Julian, what's the actual gap here — is this the first attempt at fixing that?
[G] It's the first one built the way they build it, which matters. Instead of sitting down and inventing categories from intuition, they do grounded theory — the qualitative-research method where you read real data first and let categories emerge from the data, rather than starting from a theory and hunting for confirming examples.
[O] So no armchair taxonomy. That's a meaningfully different claim to defend than "here's a framework we think is comprehensive."
[S] Defensible in principle. Whether it holds up is a different question, and we'll get there. But let's establish the baseline number first, because it's the thing that motivates the whole paper.
[G] Figure five. Six popular open-source frameworks, each run on its own natural benchmark — MetaGPT and ChatDev on a coding benchmark called ProgramDev, HyperAgent on SWE-Bench Lite, AppWorld on a benchmark called Test-C, AG2 on a math benchmark called OlympiadBench, and Magentic-One on GAIA. Success rates range from thirteen point three percent for AppWorld up to fifty-nine percent for AG2.
[S] Flip that around and the failure range is forty-one percent at the low end, eighty-six point seven percent at the high end. And the paper is careful — genuinely careful — to caption that these aren't head-to-head comparable, because every framework is running a different benchmark. It's not a leaderboard.
[O] Even so. When your best-case framework still fails four times out of ten, the minimal-gains problem stops being a footnote and starts being the headline.
# ---- method deep-dive ----
[S] Okay, walk us through how you actually build a taxonomy like this. Where do the fourteen modes come from?
[G] Three stages. Stage one, six expert annotators sit down with one hundred fifty real execution traces, drawn from five of the eventual seven frameworks, covering coding and math tasks. This is proper qualitative coding — open coding, constant comparison, memoing — and it takes over twenty hours per annotator. They keep going until they hit what grounded theory calls theoretical saturation: the point where a new trace stops teaching you anything new.
[O] Twenty hours per person, reading agent transcripts. That's not a weekend hackathon paper.
[G] It isn't, and stage two is where the taxonomy actually gets locked down. Three annotators independently label random five-trace batches with whatever the current draft taxonomy is, then sit down and argue about the disagreements — merging modes, splitting them, renaming them — for roughly ten hours of discussion, across three rounds. That process converges on a Cohen's kappa of zero point eight eight, measured across fifteen traces total, five per round.
[S] I want to flag exactly what that kappa is measuring, because this paper has three different kappa values in it and they get confused constantly online. Zero point eight eight is human annotators agreeing with each other, on a label set those same humans were actively still revising. That's a different, and honestly easier, claim than agreeing on a fixed instrument nobody's allowed to touch.
[G] Fair, and I'd add — it's still a real signal. High agreement while the categories are actively being negotiated at least tells you the disagreements were resolvable, not that everyone was just independently guessing. What comes out the other end is MAST: fourteen failure modes in three categories — System Design Issues, Inter-Agent Misalignment, and Task Verification — each one tagged to the stage of the conversation, pre-execution, execution, or post-execution, where it typically originates.
[O] Give us a feel for one, concretely.
[G] Figure three has a nice worked example. An AppWorld task: a Phone Agent and a Supervisor Agent, where the Phone Agent knows a login API needs the username formatted a specific way, and never tells the Supervisor — who never asks either. Repeated failed logins, task dies — that's failure mode two point four, Information Withholding, and it happens to be the rarest mode in the whole taxonomy, which we'll come back to.
[S] Okay, so that's one hundred fifty hand-labeled traces. The dataset the paper actually releases is over ten times that size. How do you get from one hundred fifty to sixteen hundred plus without redoing twenty-hour annotation sessions six more times?
[G] Stage three: you calibrate a judge. They take OpenAI's o1 model, give it a trace, the MAST definitions, and a handful of human-labeled examples as few-shot context. Without those examples, it gets eighty-nine percent accuracy and a kappa of zero point five eight against held-out human labels. With them, that jumps to ninety-four percent accuracy and a kappa of zero point seven seven.
[O] That's kappa number two.
[G] Right — the LLM annotator's agreement with human experts, a completely different quantity from the zero point eight eight taxonomy-building number. And there's a third one. They take the finalized taxonomy and run a fresh human agreement round on two frameworks the taxonomy had never seen — OpenManus and Magentic-One, on MMLU and GAIA — and get a kappa of zero point seven nine.
[S] Which is testing something different again — not whether the LLM judge agrees with humans, but whether the category definitions themselves generalize to systems nobody used to build them.
[G] Exactly right. Three kappas, three different questions: zero point eight eight is humans agreeing while building the taxonomy, zero point seven seven is the LLM judge matching humans, zero point seven nine is humans re-checking the finished taxonomy on frameworks it never saw. Conflate any two of those and you're making a claim the paper doesn't support.
[O] So with that calibrated judge doing the labeling, they scale up to the full release.
[G] MAST-Data: one thousand six hundred forty-two annotated traces, across all seven frameworks — the original five plus OpenManus and Magentic-One — and six models spanning four families: the GPT-4 series, Claude three point seven Sonnet, Qwen two point five, and CodeLlama.
# ---- results ----
[O] Let's get to the actual numbers. Across all sixteen forty-two traces, how does the failure breakdown look?
[G] Three categories. System Design Issues is the biggest, at forty-four point two percent. Inter-Agent Misalignment is thirty-two point three percent. Task Verification is twenty-three point five percent. Those three numbers are printed directly on the figure and sum cleanly to one hundred.
[S] And within System Design Issues, what's actually driving that forty-four percent?
[G] The single most common failure mode in the entire taxonomy lives there: Step Repetition, an agent looping on a sub-task it already finished, at fifteen point seven percent of all traces. Second most common overall is Reasoning-Action Mismatch, at thirteen point two percent — that one's in Inter-Agent Misalignment. Third is Unaware of Termination Conditions, twelve point four percent, back in System Design.
[O] And at the other extreme?
[G] Information Withholding — the mode from our AppWorld example earlier — is the rarest by a wide margin. Well under one percent of traces.
[S] "Well under one percent" is doing some careful work in that sentence. What's the actual number?
[G] This is one of the two places the paper trips over itself. Figure one's own diagram prints zero point eight zero percent for that mode. The body text, describing that same figure, prints zero point eight five percent. Small gap, but it's real, and the paper never resolves which one is correct.
[O] To be fair, that's a fifth of a percentage point on the single rarest event in the dataset. It's not going to change anyone's read of the taxonomy.
[S] Agreed it's small. But this is a paper whose entire contribution is precise failure-counting, and if the number can drift between the figure and the sentence describing the figure, that's worth one skeptical eyebrow, at minimum.
[G] Noted — and there's a second one, bigger in relative terms, but let's finish the descriptive picture first. Breaking the same taxonomy down by individual framework is a different, smaller slice: two hundred ten traces, thirty per framework, with its own category split of roughly forty-two, thirty-seven, twenty-one percent — close to the full-dataset numbers but not identical, because it's a different denominator. On that slice, the profiles diverge a lot framework by framework. AppWorld skews toward premature termination, because its star topology has no predefined workflow, so nothing tells an agent when it's done. OpenManus leans on step repetition. HyperAgent's dominant modes are step repetition and incorrect verification.
[O] What isolates whether that's about the base model or the framework itself?
[G] Two clean ablations. First, hold the framework fixed — MetaGPT — and swap the underlying model. Claude three point seven Sonnet versus GPT-4o. System Design Issues drop from sixty-three failures to thirty-eight, roughly a thirty-nine percent reduction, just from changing the model. But Task Verification barely moves at all — eighty-three versus seventy-seven.
[S] Which tells you verification weakness is architectural, not a base-model problem. A better model doesn't teach the system to check its own work if nothing in the framework asks it to.
[G] That's the paper's own reading, and the second ablation supports it from the other direction. Hold the model fixed at GPT-4o, and swap the framework — ChatDev versus MetaGPT, same benchmark. MetaGPT has sixty to sixty-eight percent fewer System Design and Inter-Agent Misalignment failures than ChatDev. But on Task Verification, it flips hard — MetaGPT actually has more verification failures than ChatDev, seventy-seven versus thirty.
[O] Roughly triple.
[S] That's actually the second inconsistency, and it's a good one. The paper's own prose, describing this exact comparison, states MetaGPT has one point five six times more Task Verification failure than ChatDev. But seventy-seven divided by thirty isn't one point five six. It's two point five seven times.
[G] It is. I checked the figure's printed bar labels directly — seventy-seven and thirty are unambiguous, they're data labels, not something you could misread. The direction is right in both places, MetaGPT verifies less than ChatDev either way, but the multiplier printed in the text doesn't match the multiplier in the paper's own chart.
[O] Which is a great illustration of why you check the underlying figure before you cite the summary sentence about it.
[S] Especially in a paper whose entire premise is that precise counting matters.
[O] Okay, but does naming the problem translate into fixing it? That's the part I actually care about.
[G] It does, and this is where I think the paper earns its keep. They run intervention studies on two frameworks. On ChatDev, on a thirty-two-task coding benchmark called ProgramDev-v0, the baseline success rate is twenty-five percent. Tightening the role-specification prompts alone — nothing else — takes that to thirty-four point four percent, a nine point four percentage point gain. Separately, starting from that same twenty-five percent baseline, restructuring the topology to add a verification step where a lead agent confirms all reviews are actually satisfied before the system terminates takes it to forty point six percent — fifteen point six percentage points over baseline. That fifteen point six is the paper's own headline "max improvement" number.
[S] I want to underline "separately" and "same baseline," because it would be very easy to hear that as one fix stacked on the other — prompt first, then topology on top, ending at forty point six.
[G] Right, and that is not what happened. These are two parallel arms, prompt-only and topology-only, each measured against the identical twenty-five percent starting point. The paper never tests them combined.
[O] Same pattern on the math side?
[G] Same design, different result. On AG2's MathChat setup, two hundred GSM-Plus problems, six repetitions per configuration, with GPT-4 the improved prompt alone moved eighty-four point seven five percent to eighty-nine point seven five percent, and the paper calls that significant. The topology arm, tested separately from that same eighty-four point seven five baseline, only reached eighty-five point five percent, and that smaller gain did not clear significance — Wilcoxon p equals zero point four.
[S] So on GSM-Plus with GPT-4, the prompt fix works and the topology fix basically doesn't.
[G] With GPT-4, yes. Swap in GPT-4o instead, same two arms, same benchmark, and now both interventions clear significance — Wilcoxon p equals zero point zero three — with the prompt reaching eighty-nine percent and the topology reaching eighty-eight point eight three percent.
[O] So the exact same kind of fix can be significant on one model and not on another.
[G] Which is itself a finding — "does this intervention work" isn't a single yes or no, it depends what you're intervening on. And there's a nice ceiling-effect footnote too: on ChatDev with HumanEval, where the baseline is already eighty-nine point six percent, the same two fixes only nudge it to ninety point three and ninety-one point five. When there's not much room left to improve, MAST-guided fixes don't have much to grab onto.
# ---- the debate ----
[S] Alright, I want to make the deflationary case as sharply as I can, and Julian, I want you to tell me where I'm wrong.
[O] Before you tear it down, let me put the optimist case on the table first, because I think it's genuinely strong. This is the first time anyone has built a shared vocabulary for multi-agent failure that's grounded in real data rather than invented from a whiteboard. Fourteen modes, each one specific enough that an engineer reading "your system has a Step Repetition problem" knows roughly what to go fix. And it's not just descriptive — the intervention numbers are real. Nine point four and fifteen point six percentage points, from targeted fixes that came directly out of the taxonomy. That's a taxonomy paying rent.
[S] I'll grant all of that is real. Here's my case against over-trusting it. Start with sample size. The entire per-framework story — AppWorld fails on premature termination, HyperAgent fails on verification — rests on thirty traces per framework. One model, one benchmark, one configuration, thirty traces, full stop. The paper itself calls that descriptive rather than comparative, which I respect, but it doesn't stop people from citing "AppWorld has a termination problem" as if it's a property of AppWorld rather than a property of thirty runs of AppWorld under one specific setup.
[G] That's a fair characterization of what the paper actually licenses. It explicitly caveats that framework-level figure as not a performance comparison, for exactly that reason. Where I'd push back slightly is that the seven frameworks were partly chosen through what they call theoretical sampling — deliberately picking systems likely to surface different failure types — so it's not a random thirty traces, it's thirty traces chosen to be informative. That doesn't fix the small-n problem, but it's not naive random sampling either.
[S] Fine, noted. Second point: all seven frameworks are open-source and mostly research-oriented — ChatDev, MetaGPT, AG2, Magentic-One, OpenManus. None of them are the production agent stacks companies are actually shipping, the ones with heavier guardrails bolted on. Does a forty-four, thirty-two, twenty-three split hold up on a real commercial deployment? The paper doesn't say, because it can't say.
[O] That one I'll actually take the other side on. You have to start somewhere, and starting with systems people can actually instrument and read traces from, rather than a black-box commercial product, is a completely reasonable choice for a first taxonomy. Nobody's claiming this is the final word.
[S] Sure, but then don't let the forty-four point two percent get quoted as if it's a property of multi-agent systems in general, when it's a property of seven open-source research frameworks in twenty twenty-five. Third point, and this is the one I actually think matters most: the category independence claim. The paper reports category-level correlations between zero point one seven and zero point three two, and argues that low number shows the three categories capture genuinely separate failure dynamics with only limited overlap. Julian, is that a fair read once you look at the fourteen-by-fourteen mode-level matrix too?
[G] No, and to the paper's credit, it says so itself if you read the appendix carefully. Spell out the three category pairs and it's System Design to Inter-Agent Misalignment at zero point three two, System Design to Task Verification at zero point one seven, Inter-Agent to Task Verification at zero point two eight — genuinely low, all three. But the finer-grained mode-level correlation matrix, fourteen modes against fourteen modes, has a maximum off-diagonal value of zero point six three, between Loss of Conversation History and Conversation Reset. The authors' own gloss on that number is candid: two modes that look similar on the surface can trick an automated judge into blaming the wrong root cause.
[O] I'll push back on you a little there, Julian — isn't that true of basically every taxonomy anyone's ever built? Categories always leak somewhat. Why hold this one to a higher standard?
[G] Because this one puts a specific number in front of readers — zero point one seven to zero point three two at the category level — and uses that number as the evidence for independence. If they'd just said "these feel like distinct failure types," fine, that's a qualitative claim and it gets qualitative scrutiny. But once you publish a correlation number as your evidence, a reader is entitled to check it against the correlation number one level down, and that's the zero point six three.
[O] Okay, that's fair. Quantified claims get quantified scrutiny.
[S] And there's a related gap underneath all of this. The paper's own worked examples, in the appendix, label single traces with several failure modes at once, spanning different categories. So co-occurrence is clearly happening. But nowhere does the paper report a labels-per-trace distribution or a co-occurrence rate. We know it happens because we can see individual examples. We don't know how often.
[G] That's the sharpest gap in the paper, in my view. It's the exact statistic that would settle whether "three distinct categories" means causally distinct, different root causes, or just definitionally distinct, meaning the annotators wrote three different bucket labels. Right now the paper asserts the stronger claim and the evidence in hand only supports the weaker one.
[O] Okay, that one lands. I'll say the taxonomy is still useful as a vocabulary even if the categories overlap more than the summary statistics suggest — a slightly leaky taxonomy you can act on beats no taxonomy at all — but I take the point that the low-overlap framing oversells what zero point six three actually shows.
[S] Fourth point, and it's a silence rather than a number: contamination. GSM-Plus, OlympiadBench, SWE-Bench Lite, GAIA, MMLU — every one of those benchmarks is reused off the shelf, and none of them get a contamination discussion anywhere in the paper.
[G] True, though I'd separate out the one benchmark they built themselves. ProgramDev is the authors' own instrument, and they say directly they designed it to be relatively straightforward rather than exceptionally difficult, specifically so failure dynamics would be easy to isolate. Defensible for taxonomy-building. It does mean their coding-task failure profile may not transfer cleanly to a harder, more contamination-resistant coding benchmark.
[S] Which is a reasonable trade-off for the one benchmark they control. It's the five they don't control that worry me more.
[O] So what would actually fix this, if you were reviewing the next revision?
[G] Three concrete things. First, a per-mode precision and recall breakdown for the LLM annotator, not just the aggregate accuracy and F1 of zero point eight zero — right now you can't tell if it's equally good at catching all fourteen modes or systematically worse at the rare ones. Second, an actual co-occurrence rate, not just worked examples that happen to show it. Third, just fix the two arithmetic mismatches we've already walked through. None of these are hard. All three would make the next revision meaningfully more trustworthy.
[S] Which is a pretty short punch list for a paper this careful everywhere else. One more, quick, while we're counting things: count the frameworks in Figure five. The prose right next to it describes this as seven state-of-the-art open-source systems. Figure five's own caption says six. OpenManus is just missing from that chart — present in literally every other table and figure in the paper, absent from that one.
[G] Also true, and also minor on its own — but it's the same genus of error as the zero point eight zero versus zero point eight five, and the one point five six versus two point five seven. None of these individually threatens the taxonomy. Collectively, they're a pattern worth noting in a paper that's entirely about counting things precisely.
[S] And that's really my whole case in one line. The methodology is sound. The execution has small cracks. Neither of those facts should be allowed to erase the other.
# ---- implications ----
[O] Step back for a second — why should someone who isn't building multi-agent systems care about this paper?
[G] Because the core move generalizes past multi-agent systems specifically. Read failures instead of just scoring outcomes, and you get a vocabulary you can act on instead of a single number you can only watch go up or down. Right now, most of evaluation practice lives on the wrong side of that gap.
[S] It also puts real pressure on LLM-as-a-judge as a method, in a good way. Their annotator is validated against human experts at ninety-four percent accuracy, with the calibration numbers shown, not just asserted. That's a template worth copying elsewhere.
[G] Though the open question nobody's tested is whether that judge has its own blind spots — whether it's systematically better at catching a loud failure like step repetition than a quiet one like information withholding. If it is, the whole sixteen-forty-two-trace distribution inherits that skew invisibly, and you'd never see it from the aggregate accuracy number alone.
[O] Which is exactly the kind of second-order question a good taxonomy sets up for the next paper to answer.
