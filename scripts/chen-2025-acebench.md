[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio, where an optimist, a skeptic, and a visiting scholar take one paper apart. Today it is ACEBench, subtitled Who Wins the Match Point in Tool Usage.
[S] Chen Chen, Xinlong Hao, Weiwen Liu, Xu Huang, Xingshan Zeng, and colleagues, across the University of Science and Technology of China, Huawei Noah's Ark Lab, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University. On arXiv in January twenty twenty-five, revised through November twenty twenty-five.
[O] Our guest is Rafael, who has read this benchmark and the tool-use evaluation work around it closely. Rafael, why does this one earn a slot?
[G] Because it makes a structural argument rather than a scoreboard argument. The claim is that tool use is not one capability with one number attached. It is at least three, they fail independently, and the field had been collapsing them. The paper then does the harder thing, which is grade all three without putting a large model in the loop as a judge.
[S] Set the gap up properly. There were already several of these. What was actually missing?
[G] Their table one is the cleanest statement of it. Five axes. Is the grading LLM-free, meaning deterministic rather than model-as-judge. Does it test robustness to imperfect instructions. Is it interactive, meaning the model acts inside a stateful environment instead of answering one-shot. Does it probe atomic-level capability, isolating one parameter type at a time. And does it include personalization, requests conditioned on a user profile.
[O] And the scoring across the prior work?
[G] By the authors' own accounting, no prior benchmark in that table checks more than two of the five. MetaTool and API-Bank are LLM-free, but neither robust nor interactive. BFCL adds robustness but not interactiveness. Tau-bench adds interactiveness but not robustness. HammerBench adds robustness but leans on LLM-based grading. And not one of the six comparison rows touches atomic-level or personalization at all.
[S] That is a table the authors wrote about their own competitors, so I will hold it loosely. But grant it. What is the second complaint?
[G] It is specifically about multi-turn. They characterize the multi-turn dialogues in BFCL and HammerBench as assembled from predefined, fixed content combinations. Concatenations of pre-written pieces rather than genuinely generated interaction. And then the cost complaint, which is that StableToolBench and ToolLLM route correctness through a large model, which is expensive and, they argue, unstable across runs.
[O] So walk us through what they actually built.
[G] The substrate is four thousand five hundred and thirty-eight synthetic APIs, spanning eight top-level domains and sixty-eight sub-domains. Technology, finance, entertainment, society, health, culture, environment, and an others bucket. From that pool they build two thousand annotated test entries, split into two linguistically parallel versions, Chinese and English.
[S] Synthetic APIs. Why is that not a weakness dressed as a feature?
[G] It is a deliberate trade and they say so. Real scraped APIs rot. The service changes shape, the benchmark silently breaks, and last year's scores are no longer comparable to this year's. So they generate the pool instead, using real API documentation as a reference. The generation itself uses what they call a self-evolving hierarchical API context tree. A model expands the tree node by node, and each new API informs the next, so the pool grows in breadth across domains and in depth within them rather than producing four thousand near-clones.
[O] And then the three categories.
[G] Normal, Special, and Agent. Normal and Special are fully machine-generated. Three to six candidate APIs are sampled per instance, randomly in most cases, with graph-based sampling when the instance needs deliberately similar APIs or a multi-turn arc. Then a dialogue is produced either by a single template-driven generator for simple fixed-structure cases, or by a three-agent pipeline where separate user, assistant, and tool agents interact.
[S] Break Normal down, because summary scores hide things.
[G] Five subcategories. Single-turn, which splits again into single-function and parallel-function calls. Multi-turn, which is further split into conversations that switch topic and conversations that refine the original request. Similar-API, where the candidates are deliberately near-duplicates on one topic. Preference, where the assistant also gets a user profile and has to mine it for argument values. And Atom, where the candidate functions are restricted to a single parameter type at a time — number, enum, list, bool, or object.
[O] What is Atom for? That feels like a diagnostic rather than a task.
[G] That is exactly what it is. The question is whether the type of a parameter, on its own, changes how well a model fills it in. You cannot answer that on a mixed benchmark because everything is confounded. Isolating it is what they mean by atomic-level.
[S] And Special is the broken-instruction category.
[G] Three defect types. Incomplete, where a required parameter is simply absent from the request. Error, where a supplied parameter violates the function's format or its allowed values — the paper's example is a team name containing a dollar sign against a pattern that only permits letters and spaces. And Irrelevant, where none of the candidate functions can satisfy the request at all. That last one is built by taking a normal instance and deleting the correct API from the candidate list.
[O] Which is a nicely cheap construction.
[G] Cheap and clean. And critically, the scoring for Special is not, did you eventually do the task. It is, did you correctly identify which of those three things went wrong. One if identified, zero otherwise.
[S] Now Agent, which I assume is where the expense went.
[G] It is, and it is the one part that is not generated. Domain experts abstract four real-world scenario families into runnable Python classes with explicit state. Mobile applications, so alarms, reminders, messaging. A food delivery platform. Financial services, meaning deposits, withdrawals, transfers, balance queries, loans. And a travel booking platform, with flight and train inquiry, booking, payment, rescheduling, cancellation.
[O] State meaning what, concretely?
[G] Order status, account balance, login state. Plus explicit business rules layered on top. The paper's own example is, before ordering takeout you must obtain the user's account and log in via the login function. And if the balance is insufficient you must tell the user insufficient balance and ask whether they want to change the order. So the model can produce a syntactically perfect call and still fail, because it skipped a required step.
[S] And who plays the user in these dialogues?
[G] GPT-4o. That is the load-bearing detail and we should come back to it.
[O] Note it and keep going. How does grading work in each of the three?
[G] Normal is AST parsing. Parse the model's function call, compare function name, argument names, and argument values against ground truth, one or zero. Where multiple answers are legitimately valid they use a candidate answer pool and matching any member counts. Special is the defect-identification check we described. Agent has two metrics. End-to-End Accuracy compares every tracked attribute of the sandbox object against the target state — all attributes match exactly, you get a one, otherwise a zero. And Process Accuracy is n over m, where m is the length of the ideal function-call sequence and n is how much of the model's actual sequence matches it.
[S] So the headline number is a blend of three things measured in three different units.
[G] It is a weighted sum, and the weighting is worth stating because it is not obvious. The weights are the square roots of the sample sizes, normalized. Not the raw sample sizes.
[O] Why square roots?
[G] The practical effect is that the smallest category does not get swamped. Agent is hand-built and therefore scarce. Under raw-size weighting the two machine-generated categories would dominate the headline and the expensive category would barely register. Square-root weighting compresses that. My read, going a step beyond the paper, is that this is a defensible choice they do not really defend — it is stated in an appendix formula and never justified in prose.
[S] Before results. What stops the generated data from being garbage?
[G] A three-stage pipeline. Rule-based inspection first, on four dimensions — clarity of API definitions, executability of the calls, accuracy of dialogues, consistency across samples. Then model-based semantic screening with a voting mechanism across three models. Then human review, where flagged items go to two independent experts and a third consolidates and revises. Three rounds of that.
[O] Right. Results. Give me the top of the board.
[G] GPT-4o leads overall at eighty-five point four percent, with GPT-4-Turbo just behind at eighty-four point five. Then it gets interesting. The best open-source model is not the biggest one. Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct scores seventy-nine point six, ahead of Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct at seventy-four point seven and DeepSeek-V3 at seventy-four point eight.
[O] And against the closed models?
[G] Seventy-nine point six beats five of the seven closed-source systems tested. Qwen-Max at seventy-eight point four, GPT-4o-Mini at seventy-two point five, Gemini-1.5-Pro at seventy point seven, Claude-3-5-Sonnet at sixty-eight point nine, and Doubao-Pro-32k at fifty-nine point four.
[S] Claude at sixty-eight point nine is lower than I would have predicted for a function-calling benchmark. That is second-to-last among the closed models.
[G] It is, and it is worth flagging rather than explaining away. I would note one structural reason to be careful with it, which is the language split, and we will get there.
[O] Where do models lose the most?
[G] Agent is the weakest category for most models but not all. Seventeen of the twenty-one score lowest on Agent. The other four score lower on Special than on Agent, and the clearest case is Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct: seventy-five point five on Normal, forty-two point three on Agent, but only thirty-eight point three on Special.
[S] So the ordering of difficulty is model-dependent, which slightly undercuts the tidy story of Agent-is-hardest.
[G] Yes, and the paper does not dwell on that as much as I would like. There is a second wrinkle inside Agent too. The table with the full breakdown covers only six models, and it separates multi-turn from multi-step. Multi-step means the user speaks once and the model executes a chain. Multi-turn means the user keeps participating. For GPT-4-Turbo, multi-step end-to-end accuracy is eighty-five point zero percent, with process accuracy of eighty-nine point five. Its multi-turn end-to-end is fifty point zero.
[O] So the chain of calls is not the problem. The conversation is.
[G] That is the correct reading. Executing a long sequence against a stateful environment is comparatively easy for a strong model. Tracking state across a genuine back-and-forth with a simulated user, where the requirements arrive in pieces, is much harder. And that pattern is not universal — DeepSeek-V3 is thirty-one point five multi-turn and thirty-seven point five multi-step, a much narrower gap.
[S] Now the fine-tuned models, because that is the paper's actual finding.
[G] Four models fine-tuned specifically for function calling. Hammer2.1 at three B and seven B, xLAM-7B-r, and Watt-Tool-8B. On the Special category: Watt-Tool-8B scores six point zero, Hammer2.1-7B fourteen point seven, xLAM-7B-r two point seven, and Hammer2.1-3B one point zero.
[O] Against what for a general model of comparable size?
[G] Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct gets forty-seven point zero on Special. So the tool-tuned models are not slightly behind, they are an order of magnitude behind, at similar or larger parameter counts.
[S] And on the clean split? Because if they are also bad there, the story is just that they are bad models.
[G] It is genuinely mixed and this is where I want to be precise, because it gets stated backwards. On the Normal summary, Watt-Tool-8B is at seventy-five point six, which is respectable — above Claude-3-5-Sonnet's seventy-two point nine. Hammer2.1-7B is at sixty-two point eight. But the other two are weak on Normal as well. Hammer2.1-3B posts eighteen point seven, which is the lowest Normal score of all twenty-one models. And xLAM-7B-r posts thirty-three point seven, which is the third-lowest.
[O] Third-lowest. What is in between?
[G] Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, a general-purpose model, at twenty-nine point six. So the ascending order at the bottom is Hammer2.1-3B, then Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, then xLAM-7B-r. The tool-tuned three B model is worse on clean tool-calling than a general three B model.
[S] Then the honest summary is narrower than the headline. For two of the four, the collapse on Special is a specific generalization failure on top of a decent model. For the other two, the model is weak everywhere.
[G] That is the correct and more careful version, yes. Score that one to you.
[O] So how do we know the Special collapse is a detection failure rather than a phrasing failure? That was your cold-open claim.
[G] Table five, and it is the best table in the paper. They split every Special failure into two buckets. Error detection means the model completely failed to notice a problem, or failed to flag it in the required format. Error correction means the model did notice, but gave unclear feedback — it says something is wrong without specifying which parameter or what is missing. The counts are lopsided. xLAM-7B-r: one hundred and ninety-five detection failures against one correction failure. Hammer2.1-3B: one hundred and ninety-seven against zero. Watt-Tool-8B: one hundred and eighty-eight against four.
[S] That is decisive, and it answers my opening objection. They are not being marked down for phrasing. They never see the problem.
[G] And a compare-and-contrast that makes it sharper. Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, a general model, sits at one hundred and thirty detection failures against thirty-six correction failures. A much larger share of its failures are of the I-noticed-but-explained-it-poorly kind. That is a qualitatively different error profile.
[O] Is there a per-defect-type view?
[G] There is. xLAM-7B-r scores one point zero on Incomplete, three point zero on Error, and four point zero on Irrelevant. Hammer2.1-3B is zero, three point zero, and zero. Whereas Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct gets twenty-six on Incomplete, thirty-six on Error, and seventy-nine on Irrelevant. Irrelevant is the one general models find easiest, and the tool-tuned models get no benefit from that at all.
[S] There is one more sub-score I noticed and want on the record. The personalization column.
[G] xLAM-7B-r scores zero point zero on Preference. In both languages. Not low — zero. Every instance where the model had to pull an argument value out of a user profile, it failed. That is the single starkest cell in the paper and it gets no discussion.
[O] Let us do the scaling result, because the paper's conclusion draws a broad claim from it.

[G] Go ahead, because I think it is the strongest objection available.
[S] The paper's flagship claim is LLM-free evaluation. Their own comparison table gives ACEBench a clean check in that column. But GPT-4o generates every user turn in the Agent category. So a model's Agent score is a function of how GPT-4o behaves as an interlocutor. Swap the simulator and the ranking could move.
[G] I think that is correct with one refinement. The scoring is genuinely model-free — you compare sandbox state against target state, no judge. What is not model-free is the stimulus. The model under test is responding to text a specific model produced. So the correct statement is that grading is deterministic and the test item is not fixed. The paper never runs the obvious ablation of swapping the simulator to see how much the Agent ranking moves.
[O] I will concede that one and take a different piece of ground. Special does not depend on the simulator at all. Neither does Normal. [S] That is fair, and it is the right way to salvage it. The headline number is softer than the finding.
[G] Agreed on both sides. And there is a related discipline problem worth naming. Every number in this paper is single-run. No variance, no repeated sampling, no stated decoding temperature anywhere. Function-calling output is sensitive to both, and the multi-turn Agent scores especially, where one wrong call early cascades through the rest of the dialogue.
[O] Does the paper misreport anything, as opposed to underreport?
[G] One thing, and it is small but real. The error analysis says that output format error is the second most common error type on Normal data, citing the figure that breaks errors out by model family. That is true for the GPT series — twenty point four percent output format against ten point three for parameter count. It is not true for the other two families it names. For the Qwen series, parameter-count errors are second at nineteen point eight against fifteen point three for output format. For the Qwen-Coder series the gap is wider — twenty-five point nine against seven point seven. So a sentence written as a general pattern holds for one of the three families in its own figure.
[S] Parameter value errors dominate everywhere, though.
[G] They do. Roughly sixty percent in all three families — fifty-nine point six, sixty-five point seven, and sixty point five. The models are broadly good at picking the right function and the right types, and bad at producing the right values.

[G] Not discussed anywhere in the paper. The situation is genuinely split. Normal and Special are machine-generated from a synthetic API pool, which lowers reuse risk considerably. A future model could absorb those four sandboxes during training and nothing in this evaluation would detect it.

[O] What changes downstream if the Special finding holds?
[G] It reframes what a tool-use fine-tune is for. If narrow function-calling SFT reliably buys the happy path and sells refusal and clarification, then a deployed agent built on one of these models will confidently call a function with a fabricated parameter rather than ask a question. That is a worse production failure than a lower accuracy score, because it is silent.
[S] And for evaluation practice specifically?
[G] Two things. First, negative cases need their own graded category, not a footnote — the models that look best on the positive cases are not the models that look best here. Second, it is a working demonstration that you can grade a stateful, multi-turn agent task deterministically by diffing environment state, which is a strictly better primitive than an LLM judge where the environment permits it.
