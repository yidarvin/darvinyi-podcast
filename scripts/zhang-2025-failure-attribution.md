# ---- context and problem ----
[S] So walk us through the actual problem, Desmond, because "failure attribution" sounds abstract until you've been the person doing it by hand.
[G] It's exactly that person's job, historically. You build a multi-agent system, agents with different roles talking to each other, you run it against a benchmark like GAIA or AssistantBench, and some fraction of the time it just fails to get the right answer. The next question is always the same: which agent, doing what, at which point in that conversation, is actually responsible? And until this paper, nobody had a rigorous, automatable answer to that question.
[O] Which is a little wild, because we've automated almost everything else in this pipeline. LLMs writing the agents, LLMs judging the agents' final outputs, LLMs grading the benchmarks in the first place.
[G] Right, and that's the gap the authors are pointing at directly. Even a benchmark like DevAI, which was built specifically to be more fine-grained than something like SWE-Bench, still just hands you more metrics as reference points. Somebody still has to sit down, read the whole conversation log, reconstruct what each agent was thinking, and, in the paper's own example, actually go re-visit a website a browsing agent cited, to check whether the agent misread the page or the page itself was the problem.
[S] And that's not a quick job.
[G] Not remotely. Three annotators did exactly this, by hand, across a hundred twenty-seven multi-agent systems, and it cost them eighty-four hours of expert time combined, thirty point nine, thirty point two, and twenty-three point two hours each, to produce a hundred eighty-four labeled failure instances.
[O] Which is really the motivating number for the whole paper, isn't it? Not "here's a cool new method," but "this specific bottleneck costs this many human hours, and it doesn't scale."
[G] Exactly, and the instinct they borrow is from a different automation success story, LLM-as-a-judge, replacing a human evaluator grading how good an answer is with an LLM doing the grading. This paper just points that same instinct at a different question. Not "is this output good," but "who broke this trajectory, and where."
# ---- method deep dive ----
[O] So how do you even formally define "the agent responsible for a failure"? That sounds like it could get philosophical fast.
[G] They make it concrete with a counterfactual test, and I think it's the paper's most underrated contribution. Picture the full trajectory of the run, agent by agent, step by step, ending in failure. Now imagine you could reach into that history, take one agent's action at one specific step, swap in the correct action instead, and let everything downstream replay accordingly. If that one swap flips the outcome from failure to success, that action is what they call a decisive error. The agent who took it is the failure-responsible agent, and the step it happened at is the decisive error step.
[S] That's clean in theory. What happens when a trajectory has more than one action like that, more than one agent whose fix would have saved the run?
[G] It happens constantly, and the authors say so themselves, plenty of instances have several agents making mistakes, not just one. Their tie-break rule is to take whichever decisive error happened earliest in time and call that the principal cause. It's a specific, disclosed modeling choice. Worth remembering, because every accuracy number in this paper is ultimately measured against ground truth built on that one convention.
[O] Hold that thought, we'll come back to it. Give us a concrete picture of what one of these failure logs actually looks like.
[G] The paper walks through one of its own worked examples. In an algorithm-generated system, an agent called DataAnalysis Expert gets flagged at step seven for fabricating synthetic numbers instead of retrieving the real ones the task needed. In the Magentic-One example, the Assistant agent gets flagged at step eighty-two for steering the whole team toward directly emailing offices instead of actually solving the task. Same definition, two very different failure shapes.
[S] Where do the logs themselves come from?
[G] Two sources, and they're not remotely equal in size. A hundred twenty-six of the hundred twenty-seven systems are algorithm-generated: an algorithm called CaptainAgent, from the AG2 library, takes a query from GAIA or AssistantBench and automatically assembles a bespoke team of agents for it, names, prompts, tools, all built on one GPT-4o snapshot from August twenty twenty-four. They only kept the configurations that ended up failing the query, which gives ninety-eight GAIA-derived systems and twenty-eight AssistantBench-derived systems.
[S] And the other one system?
[G] The other one is Magentic-One, an independently engineered five-agent framework with a web-browsing agent, a file-navigation agent, and so on, run on AssistantBench's validation set plus a thirty-instance sample from GAIA. That's fifty-eight more failure logs. Add it up and you get a hundred eighty-four total annotated failure instances, a hundred twenty-six algorithm-generated, fifty-eight hand-crafted.
[O] And every one of those got the full three-round human treatment you mentioned earlier.
[G] Three anonymized experts, three rounds. Round one, each expert annotates independently, which agent, which step, a plain-language reason, and flags anything they're not fully confident about. Round two, the group works through every flagged item together until everyone actually agrees, not just a majority vote. Round three, they cross-check each other's confident annotations for consistency. And the paper reports the friction in that process rather than hiding it: individual annotators flagged as many as twenty-six point three percent of their hand-crafted-system annotations as uncertain, and on the items flagged uncertain, one pair of annotators disagreed with each other as much as fifty percent of the time before that round-two discussion happened.
[S] Fifty percent, before discussion. That's a genuinely large number to bury in a supplementary figure.
[G] It is, and we should come back to what happens after that discussion, because that's where I have real questions. But first, the actual methods, because there are three, and they differ only in how much of the failure log the judge model sees at once. All-at-Once hands the entire conversation to the model in one prompt and asks it to name the agent and the step. Step-by-Step reveals the log one step at a time and asks, at each point, is this the error, stopping the moment it says yes. Binary Search sits between the two: it asks whether the error is in the upper or lower half of the remaining log, keeps halving, and converges on a single step in about log-base-two-of-n rounds.
[O] Which sounds like it should be the best of both worlds.
[G] It's the best of both worlds on cost, at least on paper. All-at-Once is cheapest by the authors' own token accounting, Step-by-Step is the most expensive because it re-sends a growing log at every single step, and Binary Search lands in between. Whether it's the best of both worlds on accuracy is a different question, and the answer is more interesting than you'd guess.
[S] And each of those three gets run twice more, with and without ground truth.
[G] Right. With ground truth means the judge is also told the correct final answer to the original query, mimicking debugging against a known answer during development. Without ground truth means the judge only sees the failure log itself, closer to a deployed system doing something like self-reflection after the fact, with no answer key at all.

[O] Let's get to the headline number, because "fifty-three point five percent" is what everyone quotes from this paper.

Average those four and you land almost exactly on fifty-three point five, which is the number the abstract quotes.
[S] And the fourteen point two follows the same pattern, just for a different method.
[G] Exactly. That one's Step-by-Step's step-level accuracy, averaged across its own four conditions: twenty-five point five one, seven point oh two, fifteen point three one, eight point seven seven. Average those and you get fourteen point one five, which rounds to the fourteen point two the abstract reports. So the two headline numbers everyone quotes aren't even the same method. All-at-Once is best at naming the agent. Step-by-Step is best at naming the step. No single method does both.

True for eleven of twelve agent-level cells, not all twelve.

Plus one cell each for Step-by-Step and Binary Search, both on algorithm-generated systems without ground truth. All-at-Once's own step-level average across all four conditions, eight point seven percent, sits below the average random floor of eleven point six one. That's the one the paper actually calls out by name in its own text, not a blanket statement about all three methods, just that one.
[O] So there's a real, structural tradeoff here, not just noise.
[G] A very consistent one. All-at-Once sees the whole conversation at once, so it has the context to correctly guess who's responsible. It wins agent-level accuracy in every one of the four conditions in Table one, by nineteen to twenty-five points over Step-by-Step. But that same wide context is exactly what hurts it at pinning down one specific step, it's a needle-in-a-haystack problem, and it's the worst of the three at step-level in three of the four conditions. Step-by-Step is the mirror image, worse at naming the agent, but it wins step-level accuracy in three of the four conditions, because it only has to make one small yes-or-no call at a time. Binary Search splits the difference on agent-level in all four conditions, and on step-level in three of the four. In the fourth, algorithm-generated without ground truth, it's actually the best of the three at sixteen point five nine percent, just ahead of Step-by-Step.
[S] What happens as the conversations get longer? A five-step failure log and a hundred-thirty-step failure log feel like very different debugging problems.
[G] They fall apart, is the short answer, and this is on hand-crafted systems only, since those are the only ones with enough length variation to test it. All three methods decline as the log gets longer, and step-level accuracy declines faster than agent-level. By the longest bucket, ninety-three to a hundred thirty steps, all three methods are converging toward roughly zero percent step-level accuracy. Worth sitting with: the annotators spent the most hours on exactly these long hand-crafted logs, and that's precisely where the automated methods are least useful.
[O] Is there anything that actually helps close that gap?
[G] Two things, again on hand-crafted systems specifically. First, tolerance. If instead of demanding the exact step you'll accept a window around it, the ranking flips: Step-by-Step leads at plus-or-minus-one step, fourteen point six six percent versus twelve point oh seven for All-at-Once, but by plus-or-minus-five steps, All-at-Once takes the lead at forty-three point one percent. Second, a hybrid: run All-at-Once to name the agent, then run Step-by-Step just within that agent's own actions to localize the step. That reaches fifty-seven point oh two percent agent-level and twelve point two eight percent step-level, in a separate head-to-head comparison, not a subset of Table one's numbers. The agent-level score ties All-at-Once's own score in that same comparison exactly, fifty-seven point oh two both times, which makes sense once you know the hybrid is literally reusing All-at-Once's agent guess rather than re-deriving it.
[S] At what cost?
[G] Roughly a hundred forty-nine thousand input tokens for the hybrid, against roughly seventeen thousand for All-at-Once alone, about eight and a half times the cost for a real but not enormous step-level gain, from single digits up to twelve point two eight percent. And even that doesn't clear every bar. A separate table shows Step-by-Step paired with OpenAI's o1 reasoning reaching thirteen point seven nine percent step-level on its own, so the hybrid's twelve point two eight isn't even the best step-level number anywhere in the paper, just the best among the non-reasoning methods on hand-crafted systems.
[O] Speaking of o1, this is where reasoning models come in, right? I'd have bet on them helping a lot here.
[G] That's the interesting negative result. On hand-crafted systems, comparing GPT-4o against OpenAI's o1 and DeepSeek's R1, Binary Search is excluded here because its prompt doesn't have a reasoning step to swap in, DeepSeek's R1 actually underperforms plain GPT-4o in three of the four method-and-metric combinations tested. OpenAI's o1 does better, winning three of four, but it loses the fourth, All-at-Once's agent-level accuracy, forty-one point three eight percent against GPT-4o's fifty-four point three one. So neither reasoning model consistently beats a non-reasoning model at this task.
[S] That's genuinely surprising, given how much reasoning models have moved the needle on math and code benchmarks.
[G] It is, and the authors flag it as surprising too. What does move the needle, a little, is just asking the base model to explicitly reason through its answer in the prompt, rather than swapping in a different underlying model. Strip that reasoning instruction out of the prompt entirely, and agent-level accuracy on algorithm-generated systems drops by seven point four percentage points for All-at-Once, and step-level accuracy drops by four point four points for Step-by-Step. Prompting for reasoning helps more here than upgrading to an actual reasoning model.
[O] Give me one genuinely optimistic number before we move to the debate, if there is one.
[G] There's a real one, and it's a different kind of result than everything else we've covered. Instance by instance, these methods are unreliable. But pool the predictions across all the hand-crafted-system failure logs and ask a coarser question, which single agent, overall, causes the most decisive errors, and all three methods agree with the ground truth on the answer. They also get the top-two ranking of offending agents right in two of the three cases. So even when a method can't reliably tell you "agent X broke this one specific run," it can tell you "agent X is your problem child, structurally, across your whole system," which might honestly be the more actionable question for someone maintaining a production system anyway.

[O] So where does that leave the core definition, the counterfactual decisive error, the earliest-error tie-break you flagged earlier?
[G] That's actually the deepest issue, underneath the measurement question. The paper's own text admits plenty of failures involve several agents making mistakes, not one. Their answer is to always pick the earliest fixable one and call it the cause. That's a coherent modeling choice, but it's a choice, not a discovered fact. In a long cascading failure, the earliest technically fixable error and the error an engineer would actually point to as the real problem aren't guaranteed to be the same step. The whole task, as formalized here, assumes a single decisive step always exists and is well-defined. Plenty of real failures are genuinely distributed, several agents each contributing partial blame, and this framing doesn't have a way to express that.
[S] Which would mean some fraction of that fourteen percent step-level ceiling isn't even about the methods being weak, it's about the target itself being underspecified for part of the dataset.

# ---- implications ----
[O] Zooming out, why should this matter to someone who isn't personally building a multi-agent framework?
[G] Because the same construct-validity question applies anywhere an LLM judge sits between a system and a claim about quality. Any pipeline that trains on or reports against LLM-graded labels inherits this exact question, how reliable is the judge, and how would we know? This paper is a clean, contained example of that problem, precisely because it's honest about not having answered it.
[S] It's also a useful data point against a certain kind of hype cycle, swap in a bigger reasoning model and the problem doesn't just dissolve. Worth remembering the next time someone claims a harder benchmark will fall to the next model generation alone.
[O] This actually connects to another paper on the feed, the one on why multi-agent systems fail more broadly, MAST.
[G] Right, and it's a nice complementary pair, because they're asking different questions about the same mess. MAST reads through something like sixteen hundred multi-agent traces and builds a taxonomy, fourteen named failure modes across three categories, essentially a vocabulary for what can go wrong. This paper isn't cataloging modes, it's localizing one specific instance, given a trace that already failed, name the one agent and the one step. Diagnosis of a category, versus pinpointing a single case.
[S] And MAST actually cites this paper.
[G] It does, and this paper doesn't cite MAST back, they're contemporaneous work, not one building on the other. But you can imagine the natural next step being to run this paper's attribution methods and then label the result with MAST's failure-mode vocabulary, not just naming the agent and the step, but classifying which of MAST's fourteen modes that specific failure actually is. Nobody's done that pairing yet, as far as either of these papers show.
