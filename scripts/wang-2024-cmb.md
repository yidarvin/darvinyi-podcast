[O] That is a much better hook than mine. Let us start with why the paper exists at all. There were already Chinese medical question sets by 2023.
[G] Several, and the authors name them. MLEC-QA compiled 21,700 manually annotated questions from the Chinese National Licensed Pharmacist Examination. CMExam gathered 68,119 tagged questions from the same source. ExplainCPE is a free-text explanation benchmark, also scoped to pharmacists. The authors' first complaint is occupational. They put it plainly: the assistance an LLM can give in medical professions is not confined to just pharmacy. If your question bank is pharmacist-only, it tells you nothing about nurses or medical technicians.
[S] That is a coverage complaint, and coverage complaints are cheap. Anyone can say a dataset should be bigger.
[G] Agreed, and the second complaint is the one that actually carries the paper. It is localization. The abstract argues that medical environments in different regions have their local characteristics — the example being the ubiquity and significance of traditional Chinese medicine within China — and therefore that merely translating English-based medical evaluation may result in contextual incongruities to a local region.
[O] Give me the concrete version. Why can you not just translate MedQA into Chinese and be done?
[G] Because some of the structure has no counterpart to translate into. In traditional Chinese medicine a disease has two aspects, bing, the disease entity, and zheng, the pattern. The same common cold presents as a wind-cold pattern in one patient and wind-heat in another, and the treatment differs. Diagnostic methods include tongue and pulse examination — redness on the tip of the tongue suggesting heat in the Heart, redness on the sides suggesting heat in the Liver. There is no Western-medicine sentence that becomes that sentence when you translate it. The paper also notes roughly thirteen thousand compounds used in China and over one hundred thousand recorded traditional recipes.
[S] I will take that argument seriously if they measured it rather than asserted it.
[G] They did measure it, in a small appendix table that I think is underrated. They ran two-gram segmentation over the traditional-medicine subcatalog of CMB and over MedQA from the USMLE, and compared the top five medically meaningful keywords. In CMB's traditional-medicine slice: Qi and blood, tongue coating, deficiency syndrome, course of disease, pale tongue. In MedQA: blood pressure, respiratory rate, physical examination, heart rate, abdominal pain.
[S] That is a fair bit more persuasive than the prose. Two vocabularies with essentially no overlap. Fine — the localization case lands. What did they build?
[G] Two things that are genuinely different instruments. CMB-Exam is the multiple-choice half. The questions are sourced mostly from a tutoring site called Medtiku, from which the authors say they obtained explicit permission to share the data. The taxonomy has six categories — Physician, Nurse, Technician, Pharmacist, Undergraduate Disciplines, and Graduate Entrance Exam — which cover four clinical professions, and those six split into 28 subcategories.
[O] Six categories, four professions, 28 subcategories. Those are three different counts and I want to keep them straight.
[G] You should, and there is a fourth count that does not resolve cleanly. Underneath the subcategories there is a finer subject-level division. Table 1's subject column totals 176, and the appendix directory enumerates 176 as well. But the body text says the subject division results in a total of 174 categories. The paper never explains the gap between 174 and 176, and I am not going to invent a reconciliation for it. Both numbers are the authors' own.
[S] Noted, and unremarkable in isolation, but I will remember it when we get to how carefully the rest was checked.
[G] After de-duplication and manual correction of character errors from OCR, the pool is 280,839 questions. From that they carve 400 questions per subcategory as the test set — that is 11,200 questions — and ten per subcategory as a dev set, 280 questions, each with an expert-written chain-of-thought explanation for few-shot prompting. Everything left over, 269,359 questions, is released as CMB-Exam-Train. Each question has four to six options with one or more correct answers.
[O] And scoring?
[G] Exact string match. They pull the answer out of the model's free text with what they call an empirically designed regular expression, and the response counts as correct if and only if the extracted answer exactly matches the key.
[S] Hold that thought. I have a question about it later.
[O] Then the second half. This is the part I actually find novel.
[G] CMB-Clin. Seventy-four expert-curated, complex, real clinical cases drawn from medical textbooks, turned into 208 questions. The screening is deliberate: they removed cases that require image information such as CT or MRI to resolve, kept only cases containing enough diagnostic information to answer the questions, and dropped cases with similar diseases to preserve diversity.
[O] What does one case look like?
[G] Three parts. A description — history, physical examination, ancillary tests. Then a sequence of questions. Then expert reference answers. And the evaluation simulates a multi-turn consultation: at round k the model gets the case description, plus every prior question and its reference answer, plus the current question. So it has to carry the consult forward, not answer each question cold.
[S] Every prior question and its reference answer. So the model is handed the gold answer to turn one before it attempts turn two.
[G] Correct, and going slightly beyond what the paper says about it: that design choice deliberately removes error propagation. A model that blows the diagnosis in turn one is not made to live with it in turn two. That is defensible for isolating per-question quality, but it is not the failure mode of a real consult, where a wrong early read poisons everything downstream. The paper describes the mechanism clearly and simply does not discuss that consequence.
[O] What is in the 74 cases, distribution-wise?
[G] Internal Medicine is the largest department at 15.57 percent, Surgery next at 14.87 percent. And by where the question sits in the consultation, the largest share is the treatment-principles stage at 30.09 percent, with diagnosis at 18.44 percent. So the emphasis is on what to do about it, more than on naming it.
[S] Now the evaluation protocol, because that is where these papers usually get sloppy.
[G] This one is not sloppy about the controlled part. On CMB-Exam every model is run in four conditions: answer-only versus chain-of-thought, crossed with zero-shot versus three-shot. Greedy decoding for every model to reduce generation variance, minimum new tokens set to one and maximum to 512 so answers are neither empty nor rambling. GPT-4 is the gpt-4 API version, ChatGPT is gpt-3.5-turbo-16k-0613, and all the experiments were conducted in August 2023.
[O] How many systems?
[G] By the appendix's own tally, thirty, in three groups: twelve Chinese medical LLMs, five proprietary systems, and thirteen publicly available general-domain models. That includes Qwen at three sizes, Yi at two, Baichuan2 at two, ChatGLM3, Internlm at two, Deepseek, Mixtral and Mistral, plus HuatuoGPT-II at three sizes and the smaller medical models.
[S] And CMB-Clin cannot be exact-matched, so.
[G] So GPT-4 is the judge. It gets the case description, the dialogue history, the current question, the model's response, and the expert reference answer, and it scores on four dimensions — Fluency, Relevance, Completeness, and Medical Proficiency — one to five, using the same rubric the human raters got. And to their credit, they did not stop there. Three annotators with professional medical knowledge independently scored a randomly sampled subset of 320 responses spanning eleven models. Between the expert rankings and the automatic rankings, the Spearman correlation is 0.93. Per aspect, the Pearson correlations are 0.71 for Fluency, 0.81 for Relevance, 0.78 for Completeness and 0.75 for Proficiency.
[O] That is more judge validation than most papers in this line do at all.
[G] It is, and there are two more layers. They re-ran the GPT-4 judge three independent times over both model sets in late January 2024 and report mean and standard deviation. And they checked ranking stability across five decoding temperatures — zero, 0.2, 0.6, 1.0 and 1.5 — with pairwise Spearman correlations between settings running from 0.87 up to a perfect 1.00 between the two hottest settings.
