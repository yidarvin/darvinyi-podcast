[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio. Today it is GTA, a benchmark for general tool agents, from Jize Wang and colleagues at Shanghai Jiao Tong University and Shanghai AI Laboratory, published at NeurIPS twenty twenty-four in the Datasets and Benchmarks track.
[S] It is on the docket because the tools genuinely execute rather than being simulated, and because that headline result gets quoted constantly without the caveat Crispin just raised.
[O] Crispin, welcome. Start us at the gap. What was actually broken about tool-use evaluation when this landed?
[G] Nothing was broken exactly, but the field had accumulated benchmarks that each hit some of the properties you want and none that hit all of them. The paper opens with a comparison table across five properties: real-world user queries, real deployed tools, multimodal context inputs, human-annotated tool chains, and execution-result evaluation. They line up APIBench, ToolBench, API-Bank, GAIA, and m and m's against those five columns, and GTA is the only row with a check in every one.
[S] Every benchmark paper has a table where it wins every column. What is the substantive complaint underneath it?
[G] The substantive complaint is about AI-generated queries, and it is sharper than the usual. Their argument is that when a language model writes the evaluation query, the tool-invocation steps end up explicitly and rigidly included in the query itself. The paper shows this side by side. A ToolBench query names its tools outright, spelling out figlet, list figlet styles, and matheq. An m and m's query hands over ImageGeneration, ObjectDetection, and Tagging up front.
[O] So the model is being graded on reading comprehension rather than on figuring out what to do.
[G] Precisely. Their own comparable query reads: convert the table into a statistical chart with the type of image shown in the example. It names no tool at all. The model has to work out on its own that this needs ImageDescription, then OCR, then Plot. The authors call this tool-implicit and step-implicit, and it is the whole design thesis.
[S] What about GAIA? That one is human-written and multimodal.
[G] GAIA is the closest in spirit, and the paper says so. Real-world queries, multimodal inputs, execution-result evaluation. But GAIA targets general AI-assistant evaluation rather than tool agents specifically, and it does not ship a deployed executable tool platform or annotated ground-truth tool chains. And m and m's has real tools, multimodal inputs, annotated chains, and execution evaluation, but its queries are still machine-generated. GTA's claim is the intersection, not any single column.
[O] Take us into the construction. How does a sample actually look?
[G] Each sample is a five-part tuple. There is one or two real image files, a human-written query grounded in those files, the subset of tools actually required, a reference tool chain where every step records the tool name, its arguments, and its return value, and finally the ground-truth answer. The platform holds fourteen real deployed tools in four categories. Perception is OCR, RegionAttributeDescription, DetectGivenObject, and ImageDescription. Operation is DrawBox, AddText, and GoogleSearch. Logic is Calculator, Plot, MathOCR, CountGivenObject, and Solver. Creativity is TextToImage and ImageStylization.
[S] Fourteen tools is small. ToolBench advertises thousands of APIs.
[G] It is small and that is deliberate. The trade is coverage for executability. Every one of those fourteen is deployed and callable, so the ground truth comes from someone actually running the chain rather than from a plausible-looking annotation. You cannot do that at API-scale with a research budget.
[O] How did they get human queries that are genuinely tool-implicit? That seems like the hard part.
[G] They call it exemplar expansion. Experts hand-design an initial set of exemplars covering diverse scenarios and tool combinations. Annotators are then instructed to hold each exemplar's tool set roughly fixed while inventing a new real-world scenario around it. And there is an explicit disqualification rule that I find telling: a query like please describe the image for me is thrown out, because it obviously names the ImageDescription tool.
[S] Who are these annotators, and what is the quality control?
[G] Three annotators majoring in computer science manually execute each surviving query against the real deployed tool set to build the chain and the final answer. Per the paper's datasheet, the queries and images came from researchers and student annotators paid roughly forty dollars a day, over twenty twenty-three and twenty twenty-four. The quality control I like most is the discard rule: if a tool fails to return an accurate result during annotation, say OCR misreads something, the query is discarded outright rather than kept with a flawed ground truth.
[O] That is unusually disciplined. What is the final dataset size?
[G] Two hundred twenty-nine queries. Broken down, that is one hundred fifty-six objective, sixteen subjective, and fifty-seven image-generation, which leaves one hundred seventy-two with plain-text answers. Built from two hundred fifty-two images, with five hundred fifty-seven total tool calls across the fourteen tools. Most tasks need two or three tools and two to four solution steps.
[S] Two hundred twenty-nine items. That is a small evaluation set for the confidence people place in the resulting rankings.
[G] It is, and hold that thought, because it compounds with something else we will get to.
[O] Now the part you flagged at the top. Two regimes. Walk us through it slowly, because I think this is where people misread the paper.
[G] Step-by-step mode is a diagnostic. The model is handed the correct first n steps of the reference chain and asked only to predict step n plus one. Its own mistakes never compound, because it never has to live with them. There is no real tool execution driving the sequence. That regime produces four metrics: InstAcc, ToolAcc, ArgAcc, and SummAcc.
[S] Define those precisely, because the names are doing a lot of work.
[G] The paper defines InstAcc as instruction-following accuracy, which it describes as the percentage of steps executed without errors. ToolAcc is tool-selection accuracy. ArgAcc is the accuracy of argument-name prediction. SummAcc is how accurately the model summarizes a final answer given all previous ground-truth steps.
[O] And end-to-end mode?
[G] End-to-end mode lets the model actually call the tools and drive the whole chain itself. Each step depends on the model's own prior output. That regime produces F one scores for tool selection in each of the four categories, plus AnsAcc, the accuracy of the model's own final execution result. AnsAcc is what the paper says reflects the overall performance.
[S] So when the summary of this paper says the best model gets forty-six percent, that is AnsAcc, end-to-end.
[G] Correct. And I want to be careful here, because this is the trap. It is tempting to say the model goes from eighty-five point one nine down to forty-six point five nine, and call the difference error compounding. But InstAcc and AnsAcc measure different things. One is the fraction of steps executed without error, the other is whether the final answer is right. The benchmark reports no single metric under both regimes, so you cannot cleanly isolate the compounding effect from that pair.
[O] That is a real limitation of the instrument, not just a caveat.
[G] I think so, yes. The gap is suggestive and it is worth quoting, but as a causal claim about compounding it is underdetermined by what the paper reports.

[S] Where does the failure actually happen? Because if models can name the right tool and still fail, that is a different story than if they cannot plan.
[G] This is the paper's best finding, and it is the argument, not the plan. ArgAcc is low almost everywhere. [G] They call it a buckets effect, as in the weakest stave sets the level. And they give two clean demonstrations. [S] Right. Let me make the deflationary case properly, because I think there is one and it is not about the finding, it is about the instrument.

[S] Every number in that main results table is a single run. One run per model, over two hundred twenty-nine tasks, against tools that include live web search and a live image-generation backend, neither of which is fully deterministic. I have no idea how much a second run moves any of these numbers, and neither does anyone reading it.
[G] And that is not an outside criticism, it is the paper's own disclosure. In its NeurIPS reproducibility checklist, asked whether it reports error bars with respect to the random seed after running experiments multiple times, the authors answer not applicable. So the paper tells you itself that repeated-run variance is not reported.
[O] I actually respect the candour there. But it does mean the ordering of the mid-table models should be held very loosely.
[S] Especially the mid-table. Eight point three two versus nine point five one versus ten point five six, on two hundred twenty-nine items, single run. I would not build a research agenda on that ordering.
[G] I think that is right, and I would extend it to a second axis. The model sweep is genuinely broad by family — five API-based models and eleven open-source ones, spanning six open-source families: Llama three, Qwen one point five, Mistral, Mixtral, Yi, and Deepseek. But it is narrow by scaffold. Every model runs through the same Lagent framework with the same ReAct-style prompting schema.
[O] And the Agent Flan result is the argument that scaffold fit matters.
[G] Exactly, which is why it cuts both ways. If tuning for a scaffold moves InstAcc by forty points without touching underlying reasoning, then some unknown portion of the spread in that main table reflects each base model's fit to this one specific harness rather than a scaffold-independent ceiling on tool use. The paper does not separate those, because there is only one scaffold.
[S] What about contamination? Two hundred twenty-nine hand-written items with a public release is a familiar shape.
[G] Two-part answer. For the models this paper actually tested, contamination risk is genuinely low. The queries are human-written, and the answers come from executing real tools rather than sitting in a static corpus a model could have memorized before GTA existed. That is a real design strength.
[O] And the second part?
[G] The dataset, the tool set, and the ground-truth answers are all publicly released on GitHub. So for any model trained after that release, it is now precisely the static, public corpus that the first half of my answer said it was not. That is a live leakage vector going forward, and the paper does not discuss contamination at all. To be clear about attribution: the low-risk claim for the tested models is my read of the design, and the absence of contamination discussion is simply an observation about what is not in the text.
[S] What else is not in the text?
[G] Two things I would want. Fifty-seven of the two hundred twenty-nine queries are image-generation, and the actual generated image is never judged. And the sixteen subjective queries are scored by cosine similarity against three annotator-written references, taking the best of the three. That is an automatic proxy for semantic correctness, and the paper does not validate it against human judgment.
[O] So roughly a quarter of the dataset is scored on a proxy, and a smaller slice on an unvalidated similarity metric.

Those are plainly two different accountings, and there is nothing self-contradictory about a paper having two of them. But the paper does not spell out the relationship between the two figures, so a reader trying to reconcile them cannot.
[S] Fine. Let me ask the question that matters. Does this benchmark change what I should believe about agents?
[O] I will go first. Yes, and specifically in one direction. Before this, the story about agent failure was planning — models cannot decompose a task. This says planning is comparatively fine. Tool selection F one scores are respectable across the top models. What breaks is filling in the arguments. That is a narrower, more tractable target than teach the model to plan.
[S] My version is more deflationary. Read the numbers as a lower bound on the capability under this harness, not as a ceiling on the capability itself.
[G] I would score that exchange fairly evenly, and I would adjudicate it this way. The optimist's claim that argument prediction is the bottleneck is well supported — it survives the correlation analysis, it survives the two buckets-effect examples at opposite ends of the table, and it survives the Agent Flan ablation, which is the case where you would most expect it to break. The skeptic's claim is not about whether the finding is real, it is about the precision of the ranking, and there he is right, because the paper's own checklist concedes the variance is not reported.
[O] What is the implication for benchmark design, beyond this paper?
[G] The two-regime separation is the transferable idea. Most agent benchmarks report one number and it is a step-level score, because step-level scoring is cheap and does not require deploying anything. This paper builds both, keeps them clearly labelled, and the difference between them is where the interesting behaviour lives. If you are designing an agent evaluation, building only the diagnostic regime will flatter your models.
[S] And the corollary for readers: check which regime a quoted number came from before you put it in a slide.
