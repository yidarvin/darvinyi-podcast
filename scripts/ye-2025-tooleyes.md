[O] Here is the sentence in the abstract. Expanding the model size exacerbates the hindrance to tool learning. Bigger models get worse at using tools. That is a strange thing to claim in early twenty twenty-four, and this paper puts a table behind it.
[S] It puts a table behind something more specific than that. Llama-two-chat scores thirteen point five nine overall at seven billion parameters, then rises to seventeen point nine eight at thirteen billion, then falls off a cliff to five point two nine at seventy billion. That is not a scaling trend. That is one checkpoint breaking.
[O] Two families break in the same direction though. Vicuna one point five goes from thirty-eight point seven six at seven B down to twenty-four point two seven at thirteen B.
[G] They do, and in both cases the authors diagnose the mechanism themselves, in their own error analysis, and the mechanism is a parser. That is the most interesting thing in this paper, and it is not what the abstract is selling.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio, where an optimist, a skeptic, and a visiting scholar take one paper apart. Today it is ToolEyes — fine-grained evaluation for tool learning capabilities of large language models in real-world scenarios.
[S] Junjie Ye, Guanyu Li, Songyang Gao and colleagues, all out of Fudan University. It went up on arXiv at the very end of twenty twenty-three, and it was published in the main conference at COLING twenty twenty-five.
[O] Our guest today is Hugo, who has been through this one carefully, appendices included. Hugo, why does this one earn a slot?
[G] Because its real contribution is an argument about what a tool-use benchmark is permitted to measure, and that argument has aged considerably better than any of the numbers in it. The numbers are a twenty twenty-three model roster. The argument is still live.
[S] Set up the prior work then. What were people doing that these authors object to?
[G] Three families. The first is manual expert review — a human walks the whole trajectory and judges it. ToolAlpaca is the named example. It works, and it does not scale, because you are paying an expert per trajectory.
[O] And the second?
[G] The second measures tool use indirectly. Run the model on a downstream task with tools and without tools, and call the delta the tool-use ability. Toolformer and ToolQA sit here. It never inspects how the tool was used, only whether the final score moved.
[S] Which means a model that picks a terrible tool and recovers looks identical to one that picked well.
[G] Exactly. And the third family, which is by far the most common, is rule-based automatic grading against a pre-set expected call. Gorilla, API-Bank, MetaTool. You write down the correct tool and the correct parameters in advance, and you check for an exact match.
[O] That last one sounds perfectly reasonable to me. What's wrong with an answer key?
[G] Two structural things, and the authors are careful to say these are structural rather than merely expensive. First, tools functionally overlap. If a query needs the weather in two cities, querying A first and B first are both correct, and the answer key only has one of them. Two different search APIs can both correctly serve the same request.
[S] And second?
[G] Second, the information a real tool returns changes over time. The correct answer to a weather lookup today is not the correct answer tomorrow. So a benchmark that insists on one frozen gold trace has to either pretend that variability away or confine itself to the narrow set of scenarios where it does not arise.
[O] So the pitch is: build the environment out of tools that actually run, and grade the process rather than the endpoint.
[G] That is the pitch, and the design follows from it directly. Seven scenarios: text generation, data understanding, real-time search, application manipulation, personal life, information retrieval, and financial transactions. Behind them one shared tool library — forty-one categories, ninety-five subcategories, five hundred and sixty-eight tools, drawn from LangChain's tool integrations and from SerpAPI, documented in the same schema OpenAI's function calling uses.
[S] Genuinely invocable, or invocable in the sense that a mock returns a canned string?
[G] Genuinely invocable. The model's chosen call is executed and a real result comes back. That is the whole premise, and we should come back to what it costs them later.
[O] Small thing, Hugo. The abstract says approximately six hundred tools. The body says five hundred and sixty-eight. Which is it?
[G] Five hundred and sixty-eight is the real number, stated twice in the introduction and printed as the total in Table 1. Six hundred is rounding in the abstract and the conclusion. Worth flagging a related trap: if you sum the per-scenario tool counts in Table 1 you get seven hundred and two, not five hundred and sixty-eight, because a handful of subcategories — papers, trends, weather — are listed under two scenarios each. The per-scenario column overlaps, it does not partition.
[S] Good. Where do the queries come from? That is usually where these benchmarks quietly cheat.
[G] They do not cheat here, and it is the most expensive part of the build. They went to professionals associated with each scenario, had them read the tool documentation, and had them write out actual requirements, working one tool subcategory at a time so the coverage would be broad rather than clustered. Three hundred and eighty-two queries after manual validation. Every query and every tool document is self-constructed, so it is out of domain for all ten models tested.
[O] Now the five dimensions. This is the part I actually like.
[G] Format alignment is purely mechanical. If the model invokes a tool over N rounds and N-valid of those rounds carry the right keywords with no redundant text, the score is N-valid over N. No model in the loop.
[S] So that one is a rule.
[G] It is. Intent comprehension is GPT-4 graded, on a zero-to-one scale, on how relevant the model's stated reasoning is to the user's need and how well it adapts when new information arrives. Behavioural planning is where the structure gets interesting: it is a validity score times an integrity score. Validity covers whether the summary of the current state is reasonable, whether the next actions are timely, and whether the plan shows diversity. Integrity covers grammatical soundness, logical consistency, and the ability to correct its own thinking.
[O] Multiplied, not averaged.
[G] Multiplied. Same for the other two. Tool selection, per valid round, is a reality score times a match score — reality is rule-based and asks whether the chosen tool and its parameters actually exist in the documentation, match is GPT-4 graded and asks whether the tool the model picked is the tool its own stated reasoning called for. Those are multiplied per round and averaged over valid rounds. Answer organisation is a pass indicator, did it finish inside the turn budget, times a GPT-4-graded quality score.
[S] Stop there, because that multiplication is doing real work and nobody ever mentions it. Three of the five dimensions are products of two sub-unit quantities. A product of two numbers below one is smaller than either of them. So when we get to the headline that GPT-4 only manages thirty-five point seven percent on behavioural planning, some fraction of that shortfall is arithmetic, not incapacity.
[G] That is a fair objection and the paper does not address it. What I would say in the design's defence is that both sub-scores have to be high simultaneously for the composite to be high, which is the right semantics — a plan that is well-reasoned but internally inconsistent should not score well. But you are right that thirty-five point seven is not comparable to a single zero-to-one judgment of planning quality, and the paper never says so.
[O] Fine. Overall score is the mean of the five dimensions, unweighted?
[G] Simple mean of the five. And the experimental setup: ReAct format for everyone, maximum nine interaction turns, temperature zero point three, top-p zero point five. Ten models across three sources — Llama-two-chat at seven, thirteen and seventy billion, Vicuna one point five at seven and thirteen, then the two tool-tuned ToolLlama-two-seven-B checkpoints, then Text-davinci-oh-oh-three, GPT-three point five turbo, and GPT-4.
[S] And the prompting is identical across all ten?
[G] It is not, and this matters. The five plain open-source models get five in-context demonstrations, because they were never trained on tool-learning data. Everyone else runs zero-shot. Flag that and hold it, because it detonates one of the headline comparisons.
[O] Give me the results.
[G] GPT-4 is the top scorer in all seven scenarios and overall. Seventy point three one on the ALL column. GPT-three point five turbo is second at fifty-nine point six one. The best tool-tuned model, ToolLlama-two-seven-B version two, is third at fifty-six point three zero, which the paper correctly reports as about eighty percent of GPT-4 — I checked, it is eighty point one.
[S] What is the ALL column, precisely? Because if it is a flat mean of seven scenarios I want to know that.
[G] It is not, and this is a genuine trap. ALL is the score over all three hundred and eighty-two queries, which makes it a query-count weighted average of the seven scenarios, and the scenarios have different query counts — seventy for personal life, forty-five for application manipulation. I recomputed every one of the ten rows with those weights and every one lands on the printed value. The flat mean does not. For GPT-4 the flat mean is seventy point two one, and the printed figure is seventy point three one.
[O] So do not call it the average across scenarios.
[G] Do not call it that. Call it the overall score across the full query set.
[S] Now the comparison you said would detonate.
[G] Vicuna one point five seven B is the strongest plain open-source model at thirty-eight point seven six. Text-davinci-oh-oh-three, zero-shot, is thirty-nine point seven one. Those are within a point of each other — zero point nine five, to be exact — and Vicuna actually beats davinci in three of the seven scenarios: text generation, real-time search, and personal life. The paper's own wording is that Vicuna performs comparably to Text-davinci without demonstrations.
[O] That is a remarkable result for a seven-billion-parameter open model in twenty twenty-three.
[S] Except Vicuna had five demonstrations and davinci had none. That comparison is not clean.
[G] Correct, and to the authors' credit they say so themselves, in the same paragraph, with a figure. Give Text-davinci-oh-oh-three the same five-shot treatment and it pulls ahead of Vicuna by roughly fifteen points. So the honest statement is that Vicuna matches a zero-shot davinci and loses badly to a five-shot one.
[O] I will take the concession. What about the capability breakdown?
[G] Behavioural planning is the bottleneck, essentially universally. It is the lowest of the five dimensions for nine of the ten models. The single exception is Llama-two-chat seventy B, and only because its answer organisation has collapsed to under one point, below its own planning score of roughly three. Even GPT-4 sits at thirty-five point seven percent on planning, well under its scores on the other four axes.
[S] Here is my problem with the five-dimension framing. They also report Pearson correlations between the dimensions, and intent comprehension against behavioural planning is zero point nine seven. Every correlation involving tool selection is above zero point seven. If your five axes are that collinear, you have one number wearing five hats, and the decomposition is decorative.
[G] That is the sharpest objection available and I want to answer it precisely, because the correlation matrix contains its own rebuttal. The lowest cell in that matrix is format alignment against behavioural planning, at zero point five nine. Format alignment against intent comprehension is zero point seven zero. So format alignment is the axis that moves independently of the cognitive ones.
[O] And format alignment is the axis that explains the scaling collapse.
[G] It is exactly that axis. Which means the decomposition earned its keep in precisely the place the paper's most cited finding lives. If they had reported one aggregate number, the seventy-B result would have looked like a capability regression and nobody could have said otherwise.
[S] All right, that is a real point to the paper. Take me through the scaling analysis properly, because I think the abstract is overselling it.
[G] It is overselling it, and section three point four is more careful than the abstract. For Llama-two-chat the authors count, across all scenarios, the number of turns containing missing keywords and the number containing redundant sentences. Redundant sentences climb sharply with parameter count. The mechanism is that a heavily conversational chat-tuned model appends extra friendly sentences after emitting its tool call, to sound more like everyday dialogue, and those trailing sentences break the output parser.
[O] How badly?
[G] Interactions with Llama-two-chat seventy B fail completely in ninety-one percent of the test data. Ninety-one. That is the entire explanation for five point two nine.
[S] So the seventy-B model is not bad at tool use. We do not know whether it is bad at tool use, because we never got a parseable call out of it nine times in ten. Those are extremely different claims.
[G] Agreed, and the Vicuna case is even more pointed. Vicuna one point five thirteen B automatically inserts escape characters, which corrupts the tool and parameter names it emits, so they no longer match anything in the library. The rule-based reality sub-score drops from sixty-three point four nine at seven B to fifty-one point eight six at thirteen B. But the match sub-score — does the tool it picked agree with the reasoning it gave — goes up, from eighty-nine point three two to ninety-three point one four.
[O] So the larger Vicuna reasons about tool choice better and types it worse.
[G] That is precisely what those two numbers say. The paper even states the general version at the end of that section: as parameters increase, the model's core abilities are enhanced, while its behavioural characteristics that deviate from the task requirements are amplified at the same time. That is a much more defensible claim than expanding model size exacerbates the hindrance to tool learning.
[S] Then let me make the deflationary case cleanly. Two model families, both chat-tuned, both failing an output contract for diagnosed and different mechanical reasons. No non-chat-tuned scaling ladder anywhere in the paper to isolate format compliance from reasoning. And the Llama trend is not even monotone — it rises from seven B to thirteen B before it collapses. The headline is one broken checkpoint and one escaping bug, generalised into a scaling law.
[O] I will grant most of that, and here is what survives. The finding that a strict output contract is a first-class failure mode, on par with reasoning, is real and it was not obvious in twenty twenty-three. Every agent framework that came after has had to solve exactly this problem, and constrained decoding and structured outputs are now standard partly because failures like the ninety-one percent one kept happening.
[G] I would score that to the optimist, with one qualification. The finding is real. The framing that it is about scale is not — it is about conversational tuning, and scale merely amplifies it. Those are different research programmes.
[S] Now the other structural problem. GPT-4 grades four of the five dimensions, and GPT-4 is one of the ten models being graded.
[G] They anticipated it and did more work than most papers in this space, so let me lay out exactly what they did and what it does and does not establish. First, three human annotators scored two hundred pairs of trajectories from different models on the same query. Agreement between GPT-4's preferences and the human majority exceeds eighty-three point five percent on every one of the five GPT-4-graded sub-metrics.
[O] Five sub-metrics, not five dimensions.
[G] Right, and that distinction matters. Format alignment is rule-based, it is never graded by GPT-4 at all. The five graded sub-metrics are intent comprehension, planning validity, planning integrity, tool-selection match, and answer quality.
[S] And the inter-annotator agreement?
[G] Kappa from zero point seven eight on tool-selection match up to zero point nine six on planning validity. But be careful what that measures — it measures the three humans agreeing with each other, which tells you the rubric is well specified. It is a different claim from GPT-4 agreeing with humans. Do not merge those two numbers.
[O] And the self-favouritism check?
[G] They report, per metric, the proportion of items on which each other model ties or beats GPT-4. It ranges from four point one nine percent — Llama-two-chat seventy B on answer quality — up to forty-eight point four three percent, GPT-three point five turbo on answer quality. Their reading is that GPT-4 is not uniformly rated top by its own rubric, so there is no gross self-preference.
[S] That check does not do what they want. The low end of that range is the model that fails ninety-one percent of its interactions. Of course it rarely ties GPT-4. The statistic conflates GPT-4 genuinely being better with GPT-4 flattering itself, and it cannot separate them by construction.
[G] I agree, and I would add that they ran a second and better check that gets less attention — eighty trajectory pairs specifically pitting GPT-4 against other models, re-scored by humans, where they note that humans actually preferred GPT-4's results slightly less than GPT-4 did on the tool-selection match metric. That is a directional signal against self-inflation, and it is the right experiment. It is just small.
[O] What would settle it?
[G] A blinded human re-grade of GPT-4's own trajectories, on the four GPT-4-judged dimensions, at scale. Nothing else closes it. The authors say plainly in the limitations that GPT-4 scoring cost is what capped the roster, so the budget went to breadth rather than to that.
[S] Which brings me to the thing nobody says about live tool environments. Five hundred and sixty-eight real third-party endpoints. Weather APIs, stock feeds, search engines, all on free or community keys, in early twenty twenty-four.
[O] Ah. The authenticity has a shelf life.
[S] The authenticity has a shelf life. Every number in Table 2 is conditional on those specific services still existing and still returning comparable data. That is not contamination in the training-data sense — the queries were freshly written by professionals, that part is clean — but it is environment drift, and the paper never mentions it as a limitation.
[G] It does not, and I think that is the single largest omission. It is also the sharpest tension in the design: the same choice that makes the benchmark realistic makes it non-reproducible on a multi-year horizon. You cannot have both, and the paper only argues one side.
[O] Let me make the forward-looking case anyway. This benchmark did not stay a benchmark. The queries and the tool inventory here became the clean-level environment that RoTBench was built on, by the same first author — the whole noise-perturbation study needs a realistic uncorrupted baseline, and this is where it came from.
[G] That is accurate and it is the strongest argument for the build cost. Constructing a real invocable tool environment with professionally written queries is expensive enough that almost nobody does it, and once it exists other work can vary one thing against it. That is infrastructure, and it outlived the specific results.
[S] It did. And the specific results are a museum piece. Llama two, Vicuna one point five, ToolLlama two, Text-davinci-oh-oh-three. Not one model on that list is one you would deploy.
[O] Which is not really a criticism of the paper, it is a criticism of time.
[G] Both, mildly. The roster was defensible for a January twenty twenty-four submission and the limitations section is honest about why it stopped where it did. But it does mean you should read this for its method and its error analysis, and not quote its leaderboard.
[S] My takeaway: the decomposition into five graded capabilities is genuinely good methodology, and the most quotable sentence in the abstract is, on the authors' own evidence, an output-parsing artifact in two chat-tuned families rather than a fact about scale.
[O] Mine: grading the process rather than the endpoint against tools that actually execute was the right call, and the finding that format compliance fails independently of reasoning — the one place their five axes genuinely decorrelate — was worth the entire build.
[G] And the paper's own, which is more careful than its abstract: as models grow, their core abilities improve while their behavioural habits drift further from what the task format demands, and both effects land in the same score.
