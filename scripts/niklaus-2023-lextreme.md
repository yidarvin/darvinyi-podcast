[G] It is a versioning problem, and the direction it runs in is what makes it worth two minutes of air time. The stale number is the flattering one. The camera-ready is twenty points harder on its own baselines than the abstract most people will ever read.
[S] Welcome to Litsearch Audio. Today it is LEXTREME, a multilingual and multi-task benchmark for the legal domain, from Joel Niklaus, Veton Matoshi and colleagues, published in Findings of E-M-N-L-P twenty twenty-three. Lowell, thanks for joining us.
[G] Happy to be here. I would describe this as a paper with a genuinely good idea about how to score a heterogeneous benchmark, wrapped in a framing claim that is doing more work than the data underneath it can support.
[O] It is on the docket because legal N-L-P is one of the few domains where the multilingual problem is not optional. And because the aggregation scheme here is a small, portable idea that more benchmarks should steal. Lowell, set the stage. What existed before this?
[G] The direct predecessor is Lex-GLUE, from Ilias Chalkidis and colleagues in twenty twenty-two, which this paper calls the first benchmark for the legal domain. It bundled a set of English legal tasks — European Court of Human Rights cases, S-COTUS, E-U-R-Lex, LEDGAR, unfair terms of service, and CaseHOLD — into one leaderboard. And here is the detail I like: Chalkidis is the last author on LEXTREME. This is an explicit, self-authored sequel, not a coincidental resemblance.
[S] So the authors of the English benchmark are the ones saying the English benchmark was not enough.
[G] They said it in the original paper. LEXTREME quotes Lex-GLUE's own limitations section — there is an increasing need for developing models for other languages. Meanwhile general-purpose N-L-P had already gone multilingual. XTREME, from Hu and colleagues, covers six cross-lingual predictive tasks over forty languages. There were Chinese equivalents for general and biomedical text. Legal N-L-P had nothing comparable.
[O] Were there partial attempts?
[G] Two, and the paper names both. FairLex, also from Chalkidis and colleagues, a multilingual legal benchmark aimed specifically at fairness evaluation. And L-BOX, from Hwang and colleagues, which covers two classification tasks, two judgment-prediction tasks, and one summarization task — all in Korean. So neither one spans European language diversity.
[S] Before we get to the construction, I want to know why law specifically. The lazy version of this project is: take GLUE, machine-translate it, done.
[G] Because legal text is jurisdiction-bound in a way news text is not. The lexicon, the citation conventions, the document structure — those differ across legal systems, not just across languages. A translated English benchmark would test whether a model can read translated English law, which is not the same question. That is the paper's motivating argument, and I think it is a sound one.
[O] So how did they actually assemble it? Because eleven datasets from a literature that large involves a lot of judgment calls.
[G] Deliberately, and this is one of the parts I would hold up as good practice. They ran a keyword search across the major venues — A-C-L, E-A-C-L, N-A-A-C-L, E-M-N-L-P, L-R-E-C, I-C-A-I-L, and the natural legal language processing workshop — covering twenty ten through twenty twenty-two. That surfaced one hundred eight candidate papers. Then four inclusion criteria and four exclusion criteria.
[S] Give me the exclusions, because those are where the real filtering happens.
[G] Not publicly available. No public redistribution license. Labels generated by a machine learning system rather than a human. And not peer-reviewed. The inclusion side requires legal text, a legal or legal-understanding task, a European language for now, and human-derived labels. What survives is eleven datasets, covering twenty-four languages from eight language groups.
[O] And those eleven become eighteen tasks.
[G] Eighteen task configurations, which is the term the paper uses, and the distinction matters. One dataset can carry several configurations. The Greek Legal Code has three, at volume, chapter and subject granularity. Multi-Eur-Lex has three, at three levels of label granularity in the EUROVOC taxonomy. Brazilian Court Decisions has two — approval versus dismissal, and whether the judges were unanimous. Online Terms of Service has two, one single-label and one multi-label.
[S] What are the task shapes?
[G] Three of them. Single-label text classification, multi-label text classification, and named entity recognition. The rest of the roster is Swiss Judgment Prediction, roughly eighty-five thousand cases from the Swiss Federal Supreme Court. German argument mining. A COVID-nineteen emergency-measures set spanning seven European jurisdictions. And then Greek, Romanian and Brazilian legal N-E-R sets, plus MAPA, which is multilingual legal N-E-R at coarse and fine granularity.
[O] Legal documents are long. Five-twelve tokens is not going to cut it for a Swiss Supreme Court ruling.
[G] It does not, and they handle it with a hierarchical variant rather than truncation. Split the document into chunks of one hundred twenty-eight tokens. Encode every chunk in parallel with the model under test and take the C-L-S embedding, which gives you a context-unaware chunk representation. Feed those chunk representations through a two-layer transformer encoder so each becomes aware of its neighbours. Max-pool over the result to get one document vector, then classify. They note the reason they cannot just use a long-context model: Longformer and S-LED were not available multilingually.
[S] Now the part I actually care about. How is this scored, and why is that interesting?
[G] Every configuration is scored with macro F one, and the justification is specific: it is the one metric computable uniformly across both classification and N-E-R. Accuracy is not, Matthews correlation is not. That uniformity is what makes aggregation coherent at all.
[O] And then instead of averaging everything into one number, they compute two.
[G] A dataset aggregate and a language aggregate, each built as a cascade of harmonic means. For the dataset aggregate: harmonic mean over the languages inside a configuration, then over configurations inside a dataset, then over datasets. The language aggregate does the same walk in the other order — configurations inside datasets, datasets available for each language, then over languages. The final LEXTREME score is the harmonic mean of those two.
[S] Why harmonic mean rather than arithmetic?
[G] Their argument is that F one scores are already rates, and F one is itself a harmonic mean of precision and recall, so compounding with harmonic means is consistent. Practically, harmonic means punish a single catastrophic score much harder than an average does. You will see that bite in a moment.
[O] And the point of separating the two aggregates before combining them is fairness.
[G] Fairness in a specific, mechanical sense. Multi-Eur-Lex and MAPA are the two datasets with data in all twenty-four languages. If you flat-averaged every language-by-configuration cell, those two would swamp the score, because they contribute the overwhelming majority of cells. Weighing datasets and languages separately, then combining, prevents that. Whatever else I would push back on in this paper, that instinct is right.
[S] What about jurisdiction? Because you said legal text is jurisdiction-bound, and jurisdiction is not language.
[G] The authors say it outright and I will quote them, because it becomes load-bearing later: we do not address the dimension of the jurisdiction, which we consider beyond the scope of this work.
[O] Fine. Baselines. Who is on the board?
[G] Five multilingual encoders, all pretrained on at least one hundred languages. Mini-L-M at one hundred eighteen million parameters. Distil-BERT at one hundred thirty-five million. Em-Deberta version three at two hundred seventy-eight million. X-L-M-R Base, also two hundred seventy-eight million. And X-L-M-R Large at five hundred sixty million. Everything is capped at one billion parameters, and the paper's stated reason is resource constraints.
[S] So we are firmly in the fine-tuning era of this benchmark's design.
[G] We are, but there are two more model families. Monolingual models fine-tuned per language, wherever a language appears in at least three datasets — split into Native-BERT, meaning a generic monolingual model pretrained on ordinary text, and Native-Legal-BERT, meaning one pretrained primarily on legal text. And separately, one zero-shot large language model.
[O] Which one, and on what?
[G] G-P-T three point five turbo, run on the fifteenth of June twenty twenty-three, on eight of the eighteen configurations — the classification tasks with fewer than fifty labels. Multi-Eur-Lex is excluded for two stated reasons: its labels are numeric taxonomy codes rather than textual ones, and twenty-four-language inputs at that length make a cost-reasonable comparison impossible. They cap it at one thousand samples per task for cost.
[S] Test split?
[G] No, and this is a choice I want to give them credit for. Validation split, explicitly so they do not leak held-out test data into a closed commercial A-P-I and poison the benchmark for everyone downstream. In twenty twenty-three that was still an unusual thing to think about in advance.
[O] All right, results. Give me the headline table.
[G] Five models, three columns each: dataset aggregate, language aggregate, and the combined LEXTREME score. Mini-L-M: nineteen point nine and eighteen point one, combining to nineteen point zero. X-L-M-R Base: thirty-six point eight and nineteen point seven, combining to twenty-five point seven. Distil-BERT: forty-three point two and thirty-one point nine, giving thirty-six point seven. Em-Deberta version three: forty-four point one and thirty-four point eight, giving thirty-eight point nine. And X-L-M-R Large: forty-eight point five and thirty-six point zero, giving forty-one point three.
[S] Hold on. X-L-M-R Base is a bigger model than Distil-BERT and it loses by eleven points on the combined score.
[G] It does, and the paper explains it. X-L-M-R Base collapses on Multi-Eur-Lex, particularly on the finest label granularity. Because Multi-Eur-Lex touches all twenty-four languages, one bad configuration drags the language aggregate down everywhere at once, and the harmonic mean refuses to forgive it. That is the harmonic mean biting, exactly as advertised.
[O] Let us go back to the number we opened on, because I want this pinned down properly.
[G] So. Forty-one point three is the harmonic mean of forty-eight point five and thirty-six point zero. Two times forty-eight point five times thirty-six point zero, divided by their sum, is forty-one point three. Figure one of the paper prints that number under X-L-M-R Large. And the same formula reproduces every other row in that table — nineteen point zero, twenty-five point seven, thirty-six point seven, thirty-eight point nine. The arithmetic is internally consistent and checkable.
[S] And sixty-one point three comes from where?
[G] Version one of the arXiv preprint, in January twenty twenty-three, where the abstract reported both the dataset aggregate and the language aggregate as sixty-one point three. If both inputs are sixty-one point three, their harmonic mean is also sixty-one point three, which is why the single number looked so clean. There are two later versions of the preprint, and the abstract metadata on the landing page was never updated. So the abstract you read on arXiv today still says sixty-one point three, while the P-D-F it links to has an abstract with no number in it at all, and tables that give forty-eight point five and thirty-six point zero.
[O] And Semantic Scholar mirrors the arXiv abstract, so the wrong number propagates.
[G] It does. If you cite this paper's headline score from anywhere other than the camera-ready tables, you will cite sixty-one point three.
[S] Good. Now tell me what the aggregate hides, because a single number over eighteen configurations always hides something.
[G] A great deal of churn. X-L-M-R Large tops the dataset-aggregate column, but it wins outright on only five of the eleven datasets — the Greek Legal Code, Online Terms of Service, Multi-Eur-Lex, and the Romanian and Brazilian N-E-R sets. X-L-M-R Base wins three. Em-Deberta wins two. And Mini-L-M, the smallest model on the board at one hundred eighteen million parameters, wins one — it edges out X-L-M-R Large on German argument mining, seventy-three point three to seventy-three point one.
[O] That is a nice result for the small model.
[S] It is also a warning about the spread. What is the range across models?
[G] Enormous on some datasets, negligible on others. On Online Terms of Service the gap between best and worst model is fifty-two points. On German argument mining, Swiss judgment prediction and MAPA it is under six points. So on a third of this benchmark, model choice barely matters at all.
[S] And I have to flag something, because the paper does not get this right. Lowell, does the results text match that table?

I have swept the bold markers — five for X-L-M-R Large, three for X-L-M-R Base, two for Em-Deberta, one for Mini-L-M. [O] Does it change any conclusion?
[G] No. But it is the kind of claim a reader takes on faith, and it is wrong. There is a second one like it. Table three's caption says, quote, the twenty-four E-U languages are, and then lists twenty-one codes. Croatian, Polish and Slovenian are missing from the list — even though all three have real, populated columns in the language-aggregate table. [S] Two arithmetic slips in the tables of a benchmark paper. That is not fatal, but it is a tell about proofreading.
[O] Give me the language side, because I suspect it is cleaner.
[G] It is. X-L-M-R Large is the best of the five multilingual models on twenty-one of twenty-four languages. It loses only three, all to Em-Deberta version three — Irish, Hungarian and Dutch.
[O] So the story is: larger multilingual model wins, modestly, with a lot of local noise.
[G] That is the story until you add the monolingual row, and then it changes character entirely. The single best entry in the language-aggregate table is not any multilingual model. It is Native-BERT, at thirty-nine point one, against X-L-M-R Large's thirty-six point zero.
[S] Wait. A generic monolingual BERT beats the five-hundred-sixty-million-parameter multilingual model?
[G] On the language aggregate, yes. On thirteen of the nineteen languages where such a model exists, a generic monolingual BERT is the top performer in the entire table — beating every multilingual model and every legal-pretrained monolingual model.
[O] I want to be careful here, because you said the single best entry, and there is a catch.
[G] There is, and it is important. Native-BERT and Native-Legal-BERT are only evaluated on the language-aggregate table. You cannot put thirty-nine point one next to forty-one point three and say the monolingual model wins the benchmark. Those are different quantities.
[S] So the correct statement is: X-L-M-R Large is the strongest multilingual baseline, and the monolingual result is a separate, partial comparison.
[G] Precisely that, and I would hold anyone to that phrasing.
[O] Still — even scoped correctly, that is the most interesting thing in the paper. And there is a second half to it.

Head to head against the generic monolingual model on those same four languages, the generic model wins three. [S] So the domain-pretraining premise — that law is special enough to need its own pretraining corpus — does not survive its own benchmark.
[G] On these tasks, it does not clearly survive. I would put it that way rather than more strongly, because four languages is a thin comparison and the specific checkpoints differ in size and pretraining budget. [G] X-L-M-R Large versus zero-shot G-P-T three point five turbo on the eight configurations tested. The fine-tuned encoder wins all eight. [O] So a five-hundred-sixty-million-parameter fine-tuned encoder beats a chat model across the board. In twenty twenty-three that was a genuinely useful corrective to the everything-is-solved mood.

Eight of eighteen configurations, chosen by label count and cost. One thousand samples per task. Zero-shot, no few-shot, no prompt engineering — and the paper says why, prohibitively long inputs. One model, one day, June twenty twenty-three.

What it supports is: in mid twenty twenty-three, one non-legal-specialized, non-fine-tuned chat model underperformed a fine-tuned encoder on eight legal classification tasks. What it does not support is a durable claim about frontier language models and legal understanding.
[O] Then let me make the optimist case, because I think it is stronger than this conversation has let on. This is the first benchmark that lets you ask a multilingual legal question at all. The aggregation scheme is a real methodological contribution — two aggregates instead of one is why we can even see that no single model dominates, and why the Native-BERT result is visible rather than averaged into nothing. And forty-one point three is a gift. A benchmark that launches near saturation is dead on arrival.
[S] My deflationary case is one sentence: the twenty-four-language framing is doing rhetorical work that nine of the eleven datasets cannot support.
[O] Unpack that.
[S] Only Multi-Eur-Lex and MAPA have data in all twenty-four languages. The other nine are jurisdiction-limited — Portuguese-only, German-only, Greek-only, Romanian-only, three languages for the Swiss set, seven for the COVID set, four for terms of service. In configuration terms, five of the eighteen carry the entire twenty-four-language claim. Lowell, is that right?
[G] It is right, and it has a consequence worth stating plainly. For most of the twenty-four languages, the language-aggregate score is really a EUROVOC-classification-and-N-E-R score. Judgment prediction, argument mining and the three monolingual N-E-R sets — the task variety in the headline — never touch most of those languages at all.
[O] But the authors did say jurisdiction was out of scope.
[G] They did, and I think that disclaimer undersells how load-bearing it is. Saying you are not modelling jurisdiction reads like a scoping note. What it actually means is that the benchmark's language breadth and its task breadth are almost disjoint.
[S] Point to me. Now the construct-validity question, which is the one I always ask of an outcome-prediction task. What does a high macro F one on Swiss judgment prediction certify?
[G] The authors raise this themselves, in the limitations section, which I want to credit. They note that datasets like the Brazilian and Swiss outcome-prediction sets predict a case's result from the court's own decision text — not from the parties' original filings. They cite Semo and colleagues as a rarer, more realistic alternative that uses actual complaints as input.
[O] But they push back on their own objection.
[G] They do. They argue the task may still require legal reasoning, because the model has to locate the decisive information inside that text. And I think that is a fair complication rather than a concession. A high score is probably some mix of recovering signal the judge already wrote into the ruling and genuinely extracting the facts that mattered. The paper offers no way to separate those two.
[S] Which is exactly the kind of thing that should be separable before anyone builds on the metric. And contamination — anything?
[G] Handled in the one place they address it, which is the chat model, via the validation split. Not addressed at all for the five fine-tuned encoders. Nobody checks whether X-L-M-R's Common Crawl and Wikipedia pretraining data overlaps the largely public, web-sourced legal text in Multi-Eur-Lex, the Greek Legal Code, or the Swiss set. That is a real gap, and it is the single thing I would most want added.
[O] So where does this leave a benchmark designer in twenty twenty-six?
[G] With one idea worth taking and one warning worth heeding. The idea is the two-aggregate design. If your benchmark is heterogeneous along two independent axes — tasks and languages here, but it generalizes — score both axes separately and combine at the end, rather than flat-averaging cells. It costs you nothing and it is the reason the interesting structure in this paper is visible.
[S] And the warning is about breadth claims. Twenty-four languages, eleven datasets, eighteen configurations — those three numbers invite you to multiply them in your head, and the actual coverage matrix is sparse and lopsided. Any benchmark reporting a language aggregate should say, per language, which datasets contributed.
[O] I would add a third, which is a publishing warning rather than a benchmarking one. The abstract on the landing page and the tables in the P-D-F disagreed by twenty points for over three years, and nobody caught it. If your headline number changes between preprint and camera-ready, update the metadata, not just the file.
[G] My takeaway is the paper's own: LEXTREME is the first serious attempt at multilingual legal N-L-U evaluation, its two-aggregate scoring is the durable contribution, and its most instructive single finding is that generic monolingual pretraining matched or beat legal-specific pretraining wherever both existed to compare.
[S] Mine is that I trust the scoring machinery here more than I trust the coverage claim it is applied to, and that a paper which mis-states its own win count twice earns a second look at everything else it asserts.
[O] And mine is that a benchmark whose best model reaches forty-one point three, where the winner changes across half the datasets and a small model takes one of them, is a benchmark that still has something to teach. Full writeup, with the tables and the figure, is on the litsearch site.
