[O] Here is a benchmark paper whose headline result is that it cannot give you a recommendation. Six components swept, four application scenarios, and the best setting flips depending on which scenario you are scoring. That is an unusually honest thing for a benchmark to conclude.
[S] Here is the same paper described differently. Every number in it comes from one fifth of its own dataset, run once, with no variance reported anywhere in 31 pages. And in two of its six ablation tables, the bold marker for best in column is on the wrong cell.
[O] Both of those are true and they are not the same complaint.
[S] They are not, and I will concede the framing is the contribution. But I want to talk about the obvious objection to a benchmark where GPT-4 wrote most of the answer keys and GPT-4 is also one of the systems being scored.
[G] You should, and it is the most interesting thing in the paper, because the paper's own numbers cut against that objection rather than for it. That took me two passes to believe.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio. Today it is CRUD-RAG, a comprehensive Chinese benchmark for retrieval-augmented generation of large language models, by Yuanjie Lyu, Zhiyu Li, Simin Niu, Feiyu Xiong, Bo Tang, Wenjin Wang, Hao Wu, Huanyong Liu, Tong Xu and Enhong Chen. It came out of the University of Science and Technology of China and the Institute for Advanced Algorithms Research in Shanghai, posted to arXiv in January 2024, revised that July, and published in ACM Transactions on Information Systems in 2025.
[S] I am the skeptic, and this one is on the docket because it is the rare RAG evaluation paper that ablates the retrieval stack instead of holding it fixed and grading only the generator.
[G] Callum here, glad to be on. The framing I would offer up front is that this paper has two contributions and they are worth grading separately. There is a methodology, which is evaluate every stage of the pipeline across genuinely different application shapes, and that holds up well. And there is a set of numbers, which is a snapshot of one model generation from early to mid 2024 and should be read as time-boxed.
[O] Start with the gap, Callum. What was wrong with how people were evaluating RAG in early 2024?
[G] Two narrownesses that compound. The first is that essentially every reference-required benchmark was a question answering benchmark. RGB, Natural Questions, that family. You hand the system a query, you check the answer against a reference, and you call it RAG evaluation. But question answering is one of several things people build these systems to do. Feeding a model retrieved context to draft new text, to correct a factual error in an existing draft, to condense many sources into one summary, those are all common, and an optimization that helps question answering has no guarantee of helping any of them.
[S] And the second?
[G] Even inside question answering, the field had split into two evaluation styles that each discard half the pipeline. Reference-free frameworks like RAGAS and ARES check whether the generated text is internally consistent with whatever the retriever happened to return. That criterion says nothing when the retrieved content is itself wrong. Reference-required frameworks do check against ground truth, but they typically grade only the generator, or only the retriever on a knowledge-intensive task, never both together with the surrounding machinery that decides what the model actually sees.
[O] The chunk size and the embedding model.
[G] Chunk size, chunk overlap, embedding model, retriever, top-k. All of that determines the context, and almost nobody was varying it.
[S] So the CRUD acronym. Create, read, update, delete. Is that a real taxonomy or a mnemonic somebody liked?
[G] Slightly more than a mnemonic, and it is the right question to press. It is borrowed from database interfaces, the four canonical operations on a data store, and mapped onto four ways a model touches an external knowledge base. Create is generating original content from retrieved context. Read is knowledge-intensive question answering. Update is correcting inaccuracies in existing text using retrieved evidence. Delete is condensing many documents into one summary.
[O] And each gets its own dataset.
[G] Each gets its own dataset built by a genuinely different pipeline, which is what makes it more than a relabeling. Create is text continuation, 10,728 instances. Read is question answering split by how many documents the answer needs, single-document 3,199, two-document 3,192, three-document 3,189. Update is hallucination modification, 5,130. Delete is open-domain multi-document summarization, 10,728. That is 36,166 task instances over a shared retrieval corpus of 86,834 documents.
[S] Where does the corpus come from, and does it dodge contamination?
[G] Chinese news published after July 2023, deliberately, to minimize the chance any evaluated model saw it in pretraining. They crawled roughly 300,000 articles, removed duplicates, filtered out ones too long or too short, and ended with more than 80,000. Then the construction pipeline modifies that pool. It takes 10,000 seed articles out, and adds back the articles it retrieved from Baidu while building the datasets. What is left is the 86,834-document retrieval database.
[O] Say more about that reverse construction. It is the part I found clever.
[G] It is adapted from prior multi-document summarization work. The naive way to build a summarization set is to gather related articles and then write a summary, which needs expensive human annotation. The reverse way starts from one seed article, has GPT-4 extract its key events and a summary, and then searches Baidu for roughly ten more articles connected to those events, excluding anything too similar to the seed. Those retrieved articles become the multi-document context, and the seed's summary becomes the target. Text continuation follows the same shape.
[S] And the multi-document questions? Because the failure mode there is obvious. You ask a model to write a two-document question and it staples two single-document questions together.
[G] The paper names that failure mode explicitly and builds a five-step chain-of-thought pipeline against it. Retrieve two or three connected articles covering the same event from different angles. Have GPT-4 identify what they share, the contact point. Have it identify how they differ. Generate a question per focus point. Then reconstruct those into one question whose parts are interdependent. Their own illustration of the bad case is asking someone to describe the history of the Second World War and explain the basic principles of quantum physics in one breath, versus asking how the wartime environment fostered the development of quantum physics.
[O] That is a real distinction.
[S] It is, and it is also GPT-4 grading its own homework structurally. Was there human review?
[G] There was, and its scope matters. Three native-Chinese annotators with at least a bachelor's degree reviewed generated queries, marking each reasonable or needs refinement and rewriting the latter. Five point eight percent needed refinement. But the coverage is one fifth of the dataset, not all of it, and the paper says why. Substantial cost of manual annotation, large dataset, so they polished one fifth. That five point eight percent is over the reviewed fifth.
[S] Good. I want that stated, because a five point eight percent defect rate over everything and over twenty percent of everything are different claims.
[O] Let us do the metric. RAGQuestEval.
[G] So the motivation is that BLEU and ROUGE-L are word-overlap measures and BERTScore is embedding similarity, and none of them isolate whether the specific factual content of the reference survived into the generation. The paper takes QuestEval, which was built as a reference-free summarization metric checking consistency between a summary and its source document, and repoints it. In a RAG setting the retrieved source may itself be wrong, so consistency with the source is not a valid target. So they check consistency with the ground-truth reference instead, and call that RAGQuestEval.
[O] Mechanically?
[G] A question generator pulls entities and noun phrases out of the ground-truth reference and turns them into questions. A question answerer then tries to answer each one from the model's generated text, returning an unanswerable token when it cannot. Recall is the fraction of questions that are answerable from the generation. Precision is the average token-level F1 between the answer from the generation and the reference answer.
[S] There is a wrinkle in that definition, I am told.
[G] There is, and it is small but real. The prose says precision is the average answer similarity of all questions, excluding the unanswerable ones. The worked example in Figure 6 does exactly that, three questions, two answerable, averaging over the two. But the printed equation divides by the count of all generated questions, not the answerable subset. The formula does not match either the prose or the figure. My read is the prose and figure are what was intended, but a reader reimplementing this from the equation gets a different metric.
[O] Let us get to results, because the pattern is what makes the paper.
[G] The setup first. There is a fixed baseline configuration, chunk size 128, chunk overlap zero, bge-base embedding, dense retriever, top-k 8, GPT-3.5-turbo as the generator. Then they change one component at a time. Chunk size 64, 128, 256, 512, with top-k moved to 16, 8, 4, 2 so the token budget stays roughly constant. Chunk overlap zero through seventy percent. Four embedding models. Four retrievers. Top-k 2 through 10. And finally eight language models.
[S] And the finding is that nothing wins everywhere.
[G] Nothing wins everywhere, and it is not vague. Take chunk size. Larger chunks help text continuation and multi-document question answering, because preserving article structure helps both creative generation and cross-document reasoning. Larger chunks hurt single-document question answering, where a smaller targeted chunk makes one specific fact easier to extract. And for summarization, larger chunks raise RAGQuestEval recall while lowering its precision. More key information retrieved, harder to select only the relevant part. That is a trade-off, not an improvement.
[O] The retriever comparison is the one I want the exact numbers on.
[G] This is Table 5, and it is the cleanest illustration in the paper. Four retrievers, BM25, dense, hybrid, and hybrid plus reranking, across six task blocks and five scored metrics. Thirty cells. Hybrid plus rerank takes 16 of the 30.
[S] Which sounds like a winner until you ask where.
[G] Exactly where it wins is the point. Fourteen of those sixteen are question answering. It sweeps single-document and three-document outright, and takes four of five on two-document. Its other two are ROUGE-L and recall on summarization. It wins nothing at all on text continuation and nothing on hallucination modification.
[O] And who takes those?
[G] BM25 takes seven cells total, and three of them are a clean sweep of the overall-similarity metrics on hallucination modification, BLEU 33.09, ROUGE-L 54.21, BERTScore 80.86. Dense takes four, mostly on text continuation. Plain hybrid takes three. Sixteen plus seven plus four plus three is thirty.
[S] I want the one cell hybrid plus rerank misses in question answering, because I gather that has been misreported.
[G] Two-document question answering, BLEU. BM25 posts 24.61, hybrid plus rerank posts 24.53. BM25 wins it by eight hundredths of a point, and the paper's own bold marker correctly sits on BM25 there. So the honest sentence is fourteen of fifteen question answering cells, not fifteen of fifteen.
[S] Eight hundredths of a point with no variance reported is not a result, it is a coin flip.
[G] I agree, and I would go further. That margin is exactly why the sample size matters, which we should get to.
[O] Before that, is there any independent check that the retriever ranking is real?
[G] There is one, and it is worth crediting. They ran a separate retrieval-only evaluation using mean reciprocal rank, reported in Figure 7, on question answering and hallucination modification only. Continuation and summarization were excluded as too subjective for a clean ground truth, and plain hybrid was excluded because it reorders retrieved content. Hybrid plus rerank leads on all three question answering settings, 52.5, 44.2 and 41.2. On hallucination modification it sits at 49.3, slightly behind BM25's 50.4, with dense also at 49.3.
[O] Which is the same shape as the end-to-end result.
[G] Same shape, and the paper reads that as confirming the reliability of its end-to-end evaluation. It is a real cross-check. It is also a check between two automated metrics, not between a metric and a human.
[S] Hold that thought. Embedding models?
[G] Same story, no uniform winner. The one exception is m3e-base on hallucination modification, where it tops all five scored columns. That is the only task block any embedding model sweeps outright in that table, and it is notable because m3e-base ranks lowest of the four on the general retrieval leaderboard. The paper's own conclusion is that you cannot read your RAG embedding choice off MTEB.
[O] Now the flagship. Eight language models.
[G] Table 8. ChatGLM2-6B, Qwen-7B, Baichuan2-13B, Qwen-14B, GPT-3.5-turbo and GPT-4-0613 in the original, with Qwen2-7B and GPT-4o added in the July 2024 revision. Across the six task blocks and five scored metrics, GPT-4-0613 and GPT-4o between them take 22 of 30 cells.
[S] So GPT-4 wins.
[G] Mostly, and the eight it does not win are the interesting ones. And there is one task block where GPT-4 takes only one of five.
[O] Which block?
[G] Single-document question answering. GPT-3.5-turbo posts the highest BLEU, ROUGE-L and BERTScore there, 39.76, 57.24 and 83.81. Baichuan2-13B posts the highest recall at 76.92. GPT-4o takes precision. And GPT-4-0613's own BLEU is 33.87, trailing GPT-3.5-turbo by 5.9 points.
[S] Now this is where I want to make my strongest case, so let me make it. Four of the five datasets have GPT-4 in their construction. Summarization, all three question answering sets, hallucination modification. GPT-4 wrote or guided the references. Then GPT-4 is scored against them. That is a self-preference loop and it should invalidate the model comparison.
[O] And I want to steelman the paper, so let me note the metric is token-level F1, not an LLM judge. There is no GPT-4 grading GPT-4 step inside the metric itself.
[S] That defuses the crudest version. It does not defuse stylistic alignment. If the reference phrasing is GPT-4 phrasing, GPT-4's output overlaps it more by construction, and overlap is what BLEU and ROUGE measure.
[G] That is the right objection and the paper never tests for it. But here is what makes this paper unusual. Its own numbers let you test the hypothesis, and they refute it. Ask where self-alignment should bite hardest. It is single-document question answering, because that is the one dataset where GPT-4 authored both the question and the reference answer.
[S] And that is the block where GPT-4 does worst.
[G] That is the one block of six where GPT-4 variants take one of five instead of four or five of five. It is the wrong direction for the self-preference story. If stylistic alignment were driving the table, single-document question answering should be GPT-4's strongest block, not its weakest.
[S] That is a genuinely good rebuttal and I will take the point. I do not think it fully closes the question, because the effect could exist and be swamped by something else, but as evidence goes it is the right test and it points the other way.
[O] I want to flag that this is the first time in a while a paper has answered a methodological objection with data it collected for another purpose.
[G] It did not answer it deliberately. The authors never raise the concern. The data happens to be sufficient to check it, which is a virtue of publishing the full grid rather than a summary statistic.
[S] Fine. Let me move to the objections that do land. Start with the metric's validity.
[G] Here I think the paper overreaches by inheritance. RAGQuestEval is adapted from QuestEval, and the paper's supporting sentence is that QuestEval improves correlation with human judgments over previous metrics in their extensive experiments. Their experiments. That is QuestEval's own authors validating QuestEval, in English, on summarization, against source documents. RAGQuestEval is a modified metric, in Chinese, in a retrieval setting, scored against references instead of sources. There is no human-correlation study for it anywhere in this paper.
[O] Is there anywhere it could be hiding? An appendix?
[G] There is no appendix. It is a single self-contained 31-page paper with no separate limitations section. I searched the full text for correlation, validation, agreement, kappa. The only human-correlation claim in the paper is the QuestEval one.
[S] And the human annotation they did do?
[G] Validates that the generated questions read naturally. That is dataset quality. It is not evidence that RAGQuestEval's scores track human judgments of answer quality. Those are different claims and the paper does not conflate them, but a reader easily could.
[O] What would fix it?
[G] A small human-rating study on the actual system outputs, correlating RAGQuestEval against human quality judgments. It would not need to be large. Its absence is the single biggest gap in the paper for me.
[S] Second objection. The bold markers.
[G] Yes, and this is a specific finding, not a vague complaint. Checking the font layer against the recomputed column maximum, two of the six ablation tables have stale bold. Table 6, embedding models, has three of thirty wrong. Table 8, the language model table, has three of thirty wrong. Tables 3, 4, 5 and 7, chunk size, chunk overlap, retriever and top-k, are clean, zero of thirty each.
[O] Give me one so I can picture it.
[G] Table 8's two-document question answering row. The bold BLEU is GPT-3.5-turbo at 22.75, but GPT-4o prints 22.84. The bold ROUGE-L is GPT-3.5-turbo at 37.25, but Qwen2-7B prints 41.25. In both cases the bolded value is the true maximum among the original six models, and not the maximum of the printed eight.
[S] So the bold was never refreshed when the two new models were added.
[G] That is the reading, and there is corroborating evidence I found convincing. The experimental settings section still lists six base models, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, ChatGLM2-6B, Baichuan2-13B, Qwen-7B and Qwen-14B. The prose was not updated for the revision either. Same incomplete refresh.
[O] That is a copy-editing failure, not a scientific one.
[S] It is a copy-editing failure that changes which model a skimming reader thinks won.
[G] I would put it in the middle. The numbers are printed correctly. The emphasis is wrong on six of one hundred eighty scored cells. The practical instruction is check the digits before you quote a comparison out of Tables 6 or 8.
[S] Third and largest. Sample size.
[G] This is the one I would lead with if I were writing the limitations section they did not write. There is one sentence in the experimental settings. Considering that they used GPT-3.5 as the baseline model for the experiments, to reduce cost, they only conducted experiments on one fifth of their dataset.
[O] One fifth of the whole benchmark.
[G] Every number in Tables 3 through 8. All five parameter ablations and the eight-model comparison. Twenty percent subsample of a 36,166-instance benchmark, no repeated draws reported, no variance, no confidence interval anywhere in the paper.
[S] So the 24.61 versus 24.53 cell is a single draw from a fifth of the data.
[G] Correct. And so is every other close margin, including the 36.73 versus 36.69 in hallucination modification BLEU, which is four hundredths.
[O] Let me put the optimist case and then take my medicine. The durable thing here is the methodology. Sweep the whole pipeline, across four scenarios built by four different construction processes, and report the full grid instead of a headline. That design is right, and the negative result it produces, that there is no universally best configuration, is more useful to a practitioner than another leaderboard would have been. The paper's own recommendation table follows from that honestly, Qwen-14B for generation and summarization on cost grounds, Baichuan2-13B for question answering, GPT-4 for error correction where precision matters most.
[S] And my case. The specific numbers should not be quoted as current. The newest model in any version of this table is GPT-4o from mid 2024. No Claude, no Gemini, no Llama 3, no DeepSeek, no reasoning model. The margins that separate the recommendations are often under a point, drawn once from a fifth of the data. And the metric doing the distinctive work has never been validated against a human in the setting it is used in.
[G] I would score that roughly even and I would separate the two layers explicitly. The pipeline-wide ablation methodology is the contribution and it survives all of the skeptic's objections, because those objections are about precision of the estimates, not about the design. The specific rankings do not survive. They are a time-boxed snapshot with wide, unreported error bars.
[O] Is the four-way CRUD split itself sound? Or is that also more marketing than structure?
[G] Partly marketing. Create and Delete share the same construction method, the same retrieval database and the same metric set. The real methodological partition is closer to three groups. Generation from retrieved context, which is Create and Delete together. Question answering at three difficulty levels, which is Read. And targeted correction, which is Update and is the only one built from an entirely separate dataset, UHGEval. That is still a real spread of capabilities. It is just not four equal quadrants.
[S] What does this change for how people run RAG evaluations now?
[G] The transferable lesson is that the pipeline stages interact with the task shape, so a benchmark that fixes the retrieval stack and grades only the generator is measuring a configuration, not a system. Anyone reporting a RAG result should say what the chunk size, retriever and top-k were, because this paper shows those choices move the ranking. And the corollary for eval design generally, which I think is underrated, is that publishing the full grid rather than a summary is what let us test the self-preference objection at all.
[O] My takeaway. The most valuable output of this paper is a negative result delivered with enough resolution to act on. There is no best RAG configuration, only a best configuration per scenario, and the paper shows you the scenarios and the trade-offs rather than collapsing them.
[S] Mine. Treat the framework as the contribution and the leaderboard as a footnote. One fifth of the data, one run, no variance, a metric borrowing its validity from a different paper in a different language, and its own bold markers wrong in six places. The design is worth copying. The numbers are worth re-running.
[G] And the paper's own. Different RAG application scenarios require different pipeline configurations, and evaluating only the generator on question answering will systematically mislead you about which configuration to ship. That claim is well supported by what they built, independent of whether any specific row survives another year.
