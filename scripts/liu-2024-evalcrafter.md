[G] Thank you. One framing note that will save us confusion later. This paper is doing two separable things, and they succeed and fail independently. Thing one is a prompt benchmark and a battery of seventeen objective metrics. Thing two is a regression that calibrates those metrics against human ratings. You can accept the first entirely and still have serious questions about the second, and I think that is roughly the correct posture.
[S] Start with the gap. What was the state of text-to-video evaluation when this was written?
[G] Two metrics, both borrowed. Fréchet Video Distance and Inception Score. The abstract says it plainly — that these methods often use a few metrics to evaluate performance, and that it is hard to judge large conditional generative models from simple metrics, because those models are trained on very large datasets with multi-aspect abilities.
[O] And both of those metrics are distributional.
[G] Both are distributional, and that is the substance of the complaint. F V D needs a reference set of real videos and asks whether your generated distribution looks statistically like it. Inception Score asks whether your output is diverse and classifiable. Neither one looks at the prompt. You could generate a beautiful, temporally stable, entirely wrong video and both metrics would be content.
[S] There is a second constraint I want you to spell out, because I think it shapes the whole design.
[G] It does. Several of the strongest systems in this space were closed commercial services. Gen2 from Runway, PikaLab. No weights, no training data, and in PikaLab's case output you cannot get without a visual watermark. So any protocol has to work from generated video alone. That is the same box that L L M evaluation had already been forced into.
[O] Take us through the benchmark construction, because it is not what I expected.
[G] They start from real usage. They scrape over 600 thousand prompts with corresponding videos from text-to-video Discord communities, FullJourney and PikaLab, and filter to 200 thousand by removing repeats and meaningless entries. But — and this is the part people get wrong — they do not then sample the benchmark from that corpus. They use it only to learn the shape of real usage. Most prompts are between 3 and 40 words, and there are four recurring meta-subject classes. Human, animal, object, landscape.
[S] So the corpus is a distribution study, not a source.
[G] Correct. The prompts themselves are generated by G P T four against structured templates — describe a scene about this many humans doing this motion, and return the metadata in J S O N. So each prompt arrives paired with structured ground truth. Gender, activity, age, count, colour. That metadata is what makes the object-level metrics checkable later.
[O] And there is a self-check step.
[G] There is, and I think it is the most quietly sensible piece of engineering in the paper. They found that G P T four's generated attributes were not always consistent with its own generated description. So a second G P T four pass goes attribute by attribute and asks whether the description actually supports this metadata value, yes or no, and anything failing is dropped. Then the authors filter by hand on top of that. Real user prompts and existing text-to-image evaluation prompts, DALL-Eval and DrawBench, get folded in through the same metadata-and-check pipeline.
[S] Give me the final composition.
[G] 700 prompts. By meta type, Human 280, Object 210, Animal 140, Landscape 70. That sums to 700. Orthogonally, by sub-type, Realistic 450, Style 150, Camera motion 100. Also 700. There are 50 distinct styles and 20 camera-motion prompts in circulation, randomly mixed into 250 prompts of the benchmark, and the average prompt length is 12.3 words, which they note is similar to the real prompts they collected.
[O] Seven hundred is not a large benchmark.
[G] It is not, and the paper defends the size in the appendix rather than the body. It argues results stabilise as sample count increases, and it points at concurrent benchmarks working at comparable scale — it names FETV at 619 prompts overall, and VBench at around a hundred prompts per metric. The practical argument is the honest one. Text-to-video sampling is slow, so a small effective benchmark matters.
[S] Now the seventeen metrics. Group them for me.
[G] Four aspects. Visual quality gets three metrics — an aesthetic score and a technical score, both taken from Dover, which is a video quality model trained on real user rankings, plus Inception Score. Text-video alignment gets eight. Motion quality gets three. Temporal consistency gets three.
[O] Walk through the alignment eight, because that is where the new work is.
[G] CLIP-Score, which is frame-wise CLIP similarity between the prompt and the video. BLIP-BLEU, which captions the generated video with BLIP two and computes BLEU against the original prompt. Then three attribute-grounded scores computed by running SAM-Track object detection against the prompt's own metadata — Detection-Score for whether the named object is there, Count-Score for whether the count is right, Colour-Score for whether the colour is right. An O C R score using PaddleOCR, for prompts that ask the model to render text. A celebrity identity score using DeepFace distance against real photographs, for human prompts naming a specific person. And a new metric they propose called S D-Score.
[S] Explain S D-Score properly, because I think it is the most conceptually interesting thing here.
[G] The observation is that most video diffusion models are fine-tuned from a text-to-image base model trained on far more data. So the concern is concept forgetting — the video model loses semantics its own image ancestor had. S D-Score measures that directly. For each prompt, they generate five reference images with S D X L. Five is the value stated in the method section. Then they take the mean C L I P-embedding cosine similarity between every frame of the generated video and every one of those five reference images.
[O] So the text-to-image model is the yardstick.
[G] The text-to-image model is the yardstick, which is a clever move and also a limitation the authors do not dwell on. If S D X L is wrong about the prompt, S D-Score punishes a video model for being right.
[S] And there is a numeric detail people conflate here that I want on the record.
[G] Yes. Separately from S D-Score, in the user study, the raters were shown three reference images generated by S D X L to help them understand the prompt. Three, for the raters. Five, for the metric. Those are different numbers doing different jobs, and the paper says the rater aid is what inspired the metric.
[O] Motion and temporal consistency, briefly.
[G] Motion is Action-Score, which is Kinetics four hundred action classification accuracy via a VideoMAE V two model on the MMAction2 toolbox. Flow-Score, which is average R A F T optical flow magnitude. And a motion amplitude classification score, which checks whether a video crosses a flow threshold consistently with what the prompt asked for. That threshold is set to 5, and the paper's stated justification is, quote, based on our subjective observation. Temporal consistency is warping error, C L I P-Temp between consecutive frames, and face consistency against the first frame.
[S] Every one of those is a pre-trained model grading another model.
[G] Almost every one, and the appendix concedes the point directly. It says pre-trained models provide a reference to find meaningful objective metrics as an initial attempt, and that they would explore training end-to-end evaluation models later. That is an honest limitation, not a hidden one.
[O] Now the alignment step, which is the part that makes this paper this paper.
