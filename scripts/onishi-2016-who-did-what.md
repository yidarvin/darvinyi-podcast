[S] They built a reading comprehension dataset by deleting half of it. Forty-nine point nine percent of the candidate questions were thrown away on purpose, and the criterion for throwing them away was that a dumb baseline got them right. I want to know whether that produces a benchmark or a sculpture.
[O] That is the most interesting idea in the paper and you have just described it as vandalism. Every dataset before this one reported its shortcut baselines in a table and shrugged. This one treats the shortcut as a defect in the data and removes the data. That is a different theory of what a benchmark is for.
[S] Or it is a theory that lets you define difficulty as whatever your four scripts happen to fail at.
[G] Both are fair readings, and the paper supplies ammunition for each. What I would put down first is the pair of numbers that decides it. Before suppression, simply picking the most frequently mentioned person in the passage answers sixty-one percent of these questions. After suppression it answers thirty-three percent, against a random rate of thirty-two. That is the entire design in two numbers, and everything either of you wants to argue about is really about what it cost to get there.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio, where an optimist, a skeptic, and a visiting scholar take one paper apart. Today it is Who did What: A Large-Scale Person-Centered Cloze Dataset, by Takeshi Onishi, Hai Wang, Mohit Bansal, Kevin Gimpel, and David McAllester, all at the Toyota Technological Institute at Chicago, published at E M N L P in twenty sixteen.
[S] It is on the docket because it is a construction paper. The modelling in it belongs to other people. What this paper contributes is a procedure, and procedures age considerably better than architectures.
[O] Our guest is Lavinia, who has read this one closely, appendix included. Lavinia, welcome. What was actually wrong with the datasets that already existed?
[G] The reference point is the C N N and Daily Mail cloze datasets from Hermann and colleagues in twenty fifteen. Together, about one point four million questions from roughly three hundred thousand articles. Those news articles ship with human-written bullet-point summaries, so you take the article as the passage, take one summary bullet as the question, and blank an entity out of it.
[S] And the objection is that the summary came from the article.
[G] Precisely that. A summary sentence is a compression of sentences physically present in the passage, so it stays syntactically close to one of them. The paper's phrasing is that forming questions from two distinct articles reduces the syntactic similarity between question and passage, increasing the need for deeper semantic analysis. You are meant to be reading. You can get quite far matching.
[O] What about the Children's Book Test? It is usually named in the same breath.
[G] It is, and it gets summarised badly, so let me be exact. A Children's Book Test problem gives you twenty consecutive sentences from a children's story, then a twenty-first sentence with one word deleted, and a list of ten candidate words to fill it. It is not simply predicting the next word in a story. The paper's complaint is about the task rather than the format: it says the Children's Book Test measures story completion rather than reading comprehension, because the next event in a story is often not determined. Surprises arise.
[S] And the smaller options of the era?
[G] MCTest is six hundred sixty fictional stories with four questions each, which the paper calls too small to train general systems. And bAbI is synthetic to the point that a logical algorithm solves it exactly, and in fact generates its ground truth. So the field had large-and-shortcut-prone, or small, or synthetic.
[O] So they went for large and deliberately de-shortcut. Walk me through how one problem gets made.
[G] Two independent passes over the L D C English Gigaword newswire corpus. Pass one makes the question: draw a random article, call it the question article, take its first sentence. That choice is doing work, because the first sentence of a wire story is a lede, so it is dense, self-contained, and names the prominent people. Then run the Stanford named entity recogniser and the Stanford constituency parser over it.
[S] And the person entities become deletion candidates.
[G] With constraints from the appendix worth stating. The sentence must be at least ten and at most one hundred twenty words. A candidate person entity may be at most three words long. And the sentence must contain at least two person entities, or the whole thing is abandoned.
[O] Why at least two? Only one of them is the answer.
[G] Because the other is the retrieval anchor. The passage is later required to contain at least one person entity still visible in the question. Blank the only person in the sentence and you have nothing tying the two articles together.
[S] What exactly gets deleted? In a cloze task the size of the hole is the difficulty knob.
[G] This is the paper's quietest good idea. They do not delete the name. They walk up the parse tree from the entity, through nodes still headed by that same person, and delete the whole noun phrase. Their example: President Obama met yesterday with Apple Founder Steve Jobs. Delete the second entity and you get, President Obama met yesterday with blank. Not, President Obama met yesterday with Apple founder blank.
[O] So the appositive descriptor goes with it.
[G] And the descriptor is usually the giveaway. Leave Apple founder standing and any system that knows the passage mentions one technology executive is finished without reading a clause. Two guards on that walk, both in the appendix: it stops climbing at a subordinate clause, so it does not swallow a relative clause, and it refuses the entity outright if the resulting phrase contains a coordination, which would delete two people at once.
[S] Fine. Now the passage, which is the half I care about.
[G] Apache Lucene, querying with the question sentence. The deleted entity is included in the query and required to appear in the returned article, so the answer is guaranteed present. And the search is time-boxed, run at one, three, seven and fourteen days from the question article's date, in that order, so near-simultaneous coverage of the same event is preferred.
[O] So this is really cross-document reasoning over two newsroom accounts of the same day.
[G] That is fair, and it is why these problems feel unlike the C N N ones. Then three validity filters, and these are the anti-leakage core. No sentence in the passage may share more than seventy-eight percent of its words with the question. No sentence may contain the five words immediately to the left of the blank, and likewise the five to the right. And the passage must contain at least one of the question's other person entities.
[S] The five-word flank rule is the one I would have missed.
[G] It is the sharpest of the three, because seventy-eight percent overlap across a whole sentence is a coarse instrument, and near-verbatim phrasing around the blank is exactly what a language model latches onto.
[O] And the choice list?
[G] Every person entity in the passage not already named in the question. Subset names collapse, so Obama is dropped if Barack Obama is present. Ambiguous shared surnames are discarded, so a passage naming both Bill Clinton and Hillary Clinton is thrown out when the blank is Clinton. And the list must end with at least two and at most five choices.
[S] Why a ceiling of five? That feels arbitrary.
[G] It is not. The paper says the maximum of five helps avoid sports articles containing structured lists of results. A match report is a roster, and a roster is a choice list nobody can reason over.
[O] Then deduplication, then the famous suppression step.
[G] Deduplication first, because Gigaword carries many copies and light edits of the same wire story. Then suppression. The four baselines are: pick the person appearing first in the passage. Pick the most frequent person in the passage. A five-gram language model trained on Gigaword, with articles too similar to any question removed from its training data, filling the blank directly. And a unigram model picking the most frequent last name from that same five-gram model's counts.
[S] The naive move would be to delete every question any of those four gets right.
[G] Which would be drastic and badly biased, because it would systematically remove every question whose answer happens to be mentioned early or often. Instead it is a keep-rate optimisation. Sort the questions into the sixteen cells defined by which subset of the four baselines solves them. Assign each cell a keeping rate. Maximise the total kept, subject to one constraint per baseline: its accuracy over the surviving set is at most a threshold. The threshold is set to the random-guessing rate.
[O] So the objective is retention and the constraint is difficulty.
[G] Yes, and there is a wrinkle worth naming. The denominator of each constraint is the number of surviving questions, which is itself what is being maximised, so both parts of the ratio depend on the decision variables. The paper writes the program down and says the threshold is random performance. It does not say how it solves it.
[S] Does the constraint bind?
[G] Almost exactly. First person in passage goes from point six zero to point three two. Most frequent person, point six one to point three three. The five-gram model, point five three to point three three. The unigram model, point four three to point three two. Random is point three two. Two land one point above random and two land on it.
[O] And the cost was half the data.
[G] Forty-nine point nine percent, yes. And here is an arithmetic point the paper does not make. The fully suppressed splits come to about one hundred forty-eight thousand problems, so roughly three hundred thousand entered suppression. But the pipeline started from about seven hundred thousand question sentences, drawn from eight percent of Gigaword's articles. More than half the candidates were gone before suppression ever ran, at retrieval and at the choice-list constraint. The paper reports the suppression attrition and none of the earlier attrition.
[O] Give me the final shape.
[G] One hundred twenty-seven thousand, seven hundred eighty-six training problems, ten thousand validation, ten thousand test. Validation and test are a random split of the twenty thousand most recent problems by question-article date, so training subject matter barely overlaps evaluation subject matter.
[S] A temporal split, which is the right instinct.
[G] It is, though it is temporal rather than an explicit topical deduplication, so a slow-moving story could straddle the boundary. Now the part everyone gets wrong. There is a second training set. A relaxed training set, one hundred eighty-five thousand, nine hundred seventy-eight problems, built with a larger threshold in that same optimisation, so less suppression and more data. The paper is explicit that it therefore has a slightly different distribution from the fully suppressed splits.
[O] Does that matter for reading the results?
[G] More than anything else in the paper. Every headline number people quote exists in two versions, one to three points apart. The results table labels both. The prose does not always.
[S] Concretely.
[G] The introduction says the Attentive Reader achieves sixty-three percent on C N N but only fifty-five percent here, and the Attention Sum Reader seventy percent on C N N but only fifty-nine here. Fifty-five and fifty-nine are the relaxed-training rows. Then the benchmarks section says the Attentive and Stanford Readers drop by up to ten points and the Attention Sum and Gated-Attention Readers by up to seventeen. Those are the standard rows. Both statements are correct about their own rows. Carry a number from one part of the paper into a sentence from the other and you compute a delta that is in neither.
[O] Then let us do the table properly.
[G] Standard training first, relaxed in parentheses. Attentive Reader, point five three, relaxed point five five. Stanford Reader, point six four, relaxed point six five, and that is the strongest machine system here in either regime. Attention Sum Reader, point five seven, relaxed point five nine. Gated-Attention Reader, point five seven, relaxed point six zero. Human accuracy is eighty-four out of one hundred.
[S] And the non-neural systems, which I suspect are being quietly buried.
[G] They are not far off, and this is a genuine correction to how the paper gets cited. Word overlap, point four seven. The sliding window baseline from Richardson and colleagues, point four eight, the distance baseline point four six, the two combined point five one. And the feature-based semantic system of Wang and colleagues tops the non-neural group at point five two.
[O] Point five two against the Attentive Reader's point five three.
[G] One point apart. The best non-neural system is essentially level with the weakest of the four neural readers. Anyone saying the neural readers left feature engineering behind on this dataset has not looked at the top of the table.
[S] Although the Stanford Reader at point six four is twelve points clear of it, which is not nothing.
[G] Correct, and that is the honest version. One neural reader separates. Three do not separate much from good features.
[O] Now the ranking flip, which is the result I find most interesting.
[G] On C N N, as published in each system's own paper, the order is Gated-Attention at point seven four, Stanford at point seven three, Attention Sum at point seven zero, Attentive at point six three. Here the order changes: Stanford is clearly first, Attention Sum and Gated-Attention tie around point five seven, Attentive is last. The paper's explanation is that the Attention Sum and Gated-Attention Readers explicitly leverage the frequency of the answer in the passage, a heuristic that helps on C N N, and that suppressing the most-frequent-person baseline hits those two hardest.
[S] Satisfying story. Is it the only available one?
[G] No, and this is my main reservation, and it goes beyond what the paper discusses. The benchmarks section says that for the Attentive and Stanford Readers they anonymised the data, replacing named entities with identifiers as in C N N and Daily Mail. It says nothing about doing so for the other two. So the two systems that rose in the ranking and the two that fell are also the two that saw anonymised input and the two that saw raw names.
[O] Which is the paper's own headline design feature running as an uncontrolled variable inside its own experiment.
[G] That is how I read it. Non-anonymisation is sold in the abstract and the related work as a distinguishing property, on the grounds that it permits use of external resources while remaining difficult for language models due to suppression. And the strongest system in the table did not see it. The paper does not raise the confound. I want to be careful: I am not saying the frequency explanation is wrong. I am saying there are two differences between those groups and the paper analyses one.
[S] Second problem. The C N N column is not an experiment.
[G] It is not. Those four numbers are copied from four different papers, by Hermann and colleagues, Chen and colleagues, Kadlec and colleagues, and Dhingra and colleagues. Different training budgets, different tuning, possibly different preprocessing. The table footnotes each source, so nothing is hidden. But the ten-point and seventeen-point drops are cross-paper arithmetic, not a controlled comparison.
[O] What survives that objection is the direction. All four systems drop, in both training regimes, and the smallest drop in the table is eight points.
[G] That is the right defence and I score it to you. Four independent implementations by four different groups do not all fall by eight to seventeen points because tuning noise happened to point the same way.
[S] Then let me take the human number. Eighty-four out of one hundred, from two annotators on a sample of one hundred questions.
[G] Two annotators, one hundred questions, no inter-annotator agreement reported. It is a sanity check, not a human baseline in the modern sense.
[S] And the paper leans on it, because the comparison to eighty-two percent on the Children's Book Test named entity task is a two-point difference between two small samples on two different tasks with different chance rates. Random guessing is ten percent there and thirty-two percent here.
[G] All accurate, and the paper states both chance rates itself. I would add the residual. The authors attribute most of the sixteen percent human error not to genuine ambiguity but to noise in problem formation, from errors in the named entity recogniser and the parser, and say reducing that noise should significantly improve human performance.
[O] Which cuts both ways.
[G] Both ways, and neither is quantified. If a chunk of the sixteen percent is malformed problems, part of what looks like difficulty is unanswerability, and it sits in the training set too. There is no separation of noise-driven from comprehension-driven errors, and no error analysis by number of choices or passage length.
[S] Let me ask about the choice lists, because thirty-two percent random is oddly high.
[G] It is the most misread statistic in the paper. The statistics table gives the average number of choices as three point five in both training sets and three point four in validation and test. The footnote under the suppression table says many questions have only two choices and there are about three choices on average, which is a loose rounding of that same quantity.
[O] But uniform guessing over three point four five choices would be under twenty-nine percent, not thirty-two.
[G] Exactly, and the gap is informative. Random performance here is a deterministic function of how often each choice-set size occurs, and you only reach thirty-two percent from an average near three point four five if a substantial share of problems are two-choice. That inference is mine rather than the paper's, but it follows from its own numbers, and it means a meaningful slice of this dataset is a coin flip between two named people.
[S] Which bounds how impressive point six four can be.
[G] It bounds how you read it, yes.
[O] Here is my case, made on the paper's own table. Take the best machine system and the human estimate on each dataset. On C N N, humans are estimated at about seventy-five percent and the Stanford Reader is at point seven three. A two-point gap. Here, humans are at eighty-four and the Stanford Reader is at point six four. A twenty-point gap.
[S] Say the implication plainly.
[O] The construction did the thing it was designed to do, and it is the opposite of what most dataset papers achieve. It widened the human-machine gap by an order of magnitude. In twenty sixteen the field was close to declaring C N N-style comprehension nearly solved on the strength of a two-point gap. This paper says that gap was mostly shortcut, and rebuilds the task until it reappears.
[G] I grant that almost entirely, with one hedge. The two human estimates come from different studies with different samples, so the gaps are not measured on a common ruler. But the direction and magnitude are not close enough to be an artifact of that.
[S] Then here is mine. You hardened this dataset against exactly four heuristics, chosen in twenty sixteen, by people who could think of four. Nothing in the procedure touches a fifth shortcut nobody had named yet. And half the corpus was removed by a criterion correlated with where the answer appears and how often, and nobody characterised what that did to the distribution.
[G] The first half is true and the paper does not claim otherwise. The second half I want to correct on one point. Suppression targets the random rate, not below it. So afterwards, most-frequent-person is uninformative, not anti-informative. They did not build a dataset where the frequent person is the wrong answer, which would be a genuinely adversarial artifact. They built one where frequency tells you nothing.
[S] Fair correction. I take it.
[O] Is there evidence the suppression distorted the training data?
[G] There is, and it is the cleanest small result here. All four neural readers score higher trained on the relaxed set than on the fully suppressed one. Attentive gains two points, Stanford one, Attention Sum two, Gated-Attention three. I checked every row. So training on less-suppressed data helps on the fully-suppressed test set.
[S] So suppression applied to training data actively costs you.
[G] That is the natural reading, and I hold it loosely, because the relaxed set differs in two ways at once. It is less suppressed and about fifty-eight thousand problems larger. The paper does not run the experiment separating size from suppression level.
[O] Any other tell that the two regimes are genuinely different objects?
[G] The vocabulary. The statistics table reports three hundred forty-seven thousand, four hundred six types for the relaxed training set, and three hundred eight thousand, six hundred two for the fully suppressed data. That second figure spans train, validation and test together, not the training set alone, which is easy to misread from the table's layout. And the abstract's claim of over two hundred thousand problems only clears two hundred thousand if you count the relaxed training set, since the fully suppressed splits add to about one hundred forty-eight thousand. Both figures are printed, but they belong to different objects.
[S] Which is the same reading trap as the introduction's fifty-five and fifty-nine.
[G] The same trap, three times over. There are two datasets living inside this paper, and the abstract and introduction are quoting the larger, looser one.
[O] What should someone building an evaluation today take from this?
[G] That a shortcut is a property of your data, not of your models, and you can attack it at construction time. And that framing it as constrained retention rather than blanket filtering is what makes it affordable. That template is everywhere now, in adversarial filtering, in decontamination, in benchmarks that report and then remove their own trivial slice.
[S] And the warning is that suppression is only as good as your enumeration of shortcuts, and that a dataset reporting the attrition it likes is still not reporting the attrition it does not.
[O] Lavinia, last word to you first. One sentence, the paper's.
[G] Build problems from two independent articles, delete the whole noun phrase rather than the name, then remove questions until four cheap heuristics perform at chance, and you get a task humans solve eighty-four times in one hundred while the best reader of the day reaches point six four.
