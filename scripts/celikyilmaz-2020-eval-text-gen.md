[S] Fine. So what's the frame?
[G] Three categories. Human-centric evaluation, untrained automatic metrics, and machine-learned metrics. That triple is genuinely the contribution — it's the thing that has outlived every specific verdict inside it.
[O] Take them one at a time.
[G] Human-centric splits into intrinsic and extrinsic. Intrinsic means asking people to rate the generated text directly — either on a Likert or sliding scale, or by ranking and comparing outputs against each other. The paper's first figure contrasts exactly those two formats: a plain Likert informativeness question, versus a RankME-style comparison, which adds magnitude estimation so evaluators say not just which output is better but how much better.
[S] And extrinsic?
[G] Extrinsic measures whether the generated text actually works in a downstream task. The authors point to one of the earliest examples: automatically generated instructions, evaluated by counting the mistakes people made while following them. There's a better one still — Reiter and colleagues generate personalised smoking cessation letters, and report how many recipients actually gave up smoking.
[O] That's not a proxy at all. That's the real thing.
[G] It isn't a proxy, no. It's also expensive and slow, which is why the paper notes intrinsic evaluation is far more common — and, citing van der Lee and colleagues, increasingly so, as the field shifted focus toward subtasks rather than full systems.
[S] What about agreement? If you're leaning on human raters, inter-rater agreement is the whole ballgame.
[G] The survey walks the standard ladder. Percent agreement — simplest, but it doesn't correct for the chance that two raters would have agreed by accident. Cohen's kappa for two evaluators, Fleiss' kappa for more than two, both of which subtract out that chance agreement. And Krippendorff's alpha, which generalises further: it lets different kinds of disagreement be weighted differently, rather than treating every mismatch as equally bad. It's defined as one minus the ratio of observed to chance-expected weighted disagreement — so it isn't a raw agreement percentage at all.

[S] What does the survey have beyond the worked example?
[G] A compilation of other people's findings, which it is clear about. Reiter, in 2018, reports there's not enough evidence to support BLEU being the best metric for generation tasks other than machine translation. Caccia and colleagues found that generated text with perfect BLEU scores was often grammatically correct but lacked semantic or global coherence — the paper's phrasing is that it had poor information content.
[O] Perfect BLEU, incoherent text. That's a clean existence proof.
[G] And then the one I find most consequential: Mathur and colleagues, 2020, on outlier sensitivity in machine translation evaluation. They found that when outliers are present, metrics like BLEU produce high correlations with human judgment, and those high correlations lead to false conclusions about how reliable the metric is. Remove the outliers, and the correlation drops.
[S] So the headline correlation was being carried by the easy cases.
[G] By the systems that were obviously bad. Distinguishing a terrible system from a good one is easy for any metric. Ranking two good systems against each other is the hard problem, and that's exactly where BLEU stops working.
[O] What about ROUGE? ROUGE at least gets to play at home in summarization.
[G] It does better on home turf. The survey says ROUGE has been shown to correlate well with human judgments for generic text summarization. But its own verdict is that the correlation is lower for topic-focused summarization — extractive meeting summarization is the example they use, citing Liu and Liu, 2008.
[S] Why meetings specifically?
[G] Because meeting transcripts are spontaneous speech. They're full of disfluencies — pauses, "um" and "uh", discourse markers like "you know" and "I mean", repetitions. And there are two separate findings from that paper that get conflated constantly, so let me keep them apart. First: when those disfluencies are cleaned out of the transcripts, the ROUGE score itself improves. Second, and this is a different intervention: when they include the speaker information of the extracted sentences, they observe fair improvement in the correlation between ROUGE and human judgment.
[O] So one raises the number, and a different one raises the number's validity.
[G] Exactly that, and only the second is actually good news about ROUGE as a measurement instrument. Cleaning your input so the metric goes up is not evidence the metric is working. It's arguably evidence that it's fragile.
[S] That's a distinction I'd bet most people citing that paper get wrong.
[O] Let's move to the third family, because that's where the field's hopes were.
[G] Machine-learned metrics, and here's a detail worth getting right: the survey breaks these into six sub-categories, not three or four. Sentence semantic similarity models. Regression-based metrics. Models trained directly on collected human judgments. BERT-based metrics. Factual-correctness models. And composite metrics that combine several signals into a single learned score.
[S] Give me one from each end.
[G] Regression-based: RUSE, from Shimanaka and colleagues, treats evaluation as a regression problem. Encode the hypothesis and the reference, then extract their relation three ways — concatenate the two vectors, take their element-wise product, and compute the absolute element-wise distance — and regress that onto a quality score. At the other end, composite metrics. Sharif and colleagues build one for image captioning that combines METEOR, Word Mover's Distance and SPICE, to capture adequacy and fluency at once.
[O] And the human-judgment-trained category — that's ADEM?
[G] ADEM, from Lowe and colleagues, is the canonical example. Trained on human ratings of dialog response appropriateness, using Twitter data where each tweet response is a reference and its preceding turns are the context. The motivation the survey gives for this whole category is sharp: for open-ended tasks, word-overlap metrics fail because there are too many plausible references to ever collect them all, and running fresh human evaluation for every new task simply doesn't scale.
[S] And BERT-based is where BERTScore lives.
[G] BERTScore is the central example, and the pipeline is worth stating precisely. Embed both the reference and the candidate with a pretrained contextual encoder. Compute pairwise cosine similarity between every reference token and every candidate token. For each reference token, take its maximum similarity to any candidate token. Optionally weight those by inverse document frequency, so common words count for less. Then average into a precision, recall and F-one triple.
[O] So it's soft token matching instead of exact token matching. Which addresses the "life vest" versus "life jacket" problem directly.
[G] It's designed to, yes. The survey reports it correlates well with human judgments at both the sentence and the system level.
[S] Where's the actual quantitative evidence in this section, though? Because so far this is a taxonomy with adjectives attached.
[G] The clearest quantitative material is about robustness rather than raw correlation, and it's the BLEURT result. BLEURT takes a BERT checkpoint and fine-tunes it twice. First on synthetically generated sentence pairs labelled with automatic scores like BLEU and ROUGE. Only then on real system outputs and human references, using human ratings.
[O] Two stages. Why does the synthetic stage help?
[G] Because of what the BLEURT authors found when they varied the training data. The survey reports it directly: training skew has, in their words, a disastrous effect on BLEURT without pre-training, and that pre-training makes BLEURT significantly more robust to quality drifts. The figure the survey reproduces plots Kendall's tau against human ratings as test-set skew increases. Without the synthetic pre-training, that correlation falls off sharply. With it, the degradation is far gentler, and it tracks closer to BERTScore's line across the same range.
[S] Skew meaning the training set and the deployment distribution don't match in quality.
[G] Meaning the systems you trained the metric on were a different quality mix than the systems you're now scoring. Which is the normal condition, not the exceptional one. Your metric is trained on last year's models and deployed on this year's.
[O] That's the whole problem with learned metrics in one sentence.
[G] And it's why I'd single it out as the survey's most transferable finding. [S] What about factual correctness? None of the metrics you've described so far can even represent the concept.

[G] Over seventy percent. And the detail that makes it worse is that the finding held across several different modeling approaches. It wasn't one bad architecture.
[S] So we have a metric family that scores paraphrase quality beautifully and cannot notice that the summary invented a fact.
[G] That's the gap the section exists to name. [S] So what does an evaluation person actually take from this today?
[G] Three things, I'd say. First, the human-evaluation reporting numbers are still the cleanest citation available for the claim that our gold standard is under-specified. Fifty-seven percent reporting participant counts. Eighteen percent reporting agreement. If you're arguing for better evaluation hygiene, those are your numbers.
[O] Second?
[G] Second, the Mathur outlier finding generalises well beyond BLEU. Any time you validate a judge or a metric by correlating it against human ratings on a pool that includes obviously-bad systems, your correlation is inflated. [G] And third, the BLEURT skew result. A learned evaluator is trained on some distribution of system quality, and it degrades when the systems it scores drift away from that distribution. That is precisely the condition every deployed judge model operates under.
