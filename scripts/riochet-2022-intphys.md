[G] Thank you. And one piece of housekeeping before anything else, because it trips people up. This paper was retitled between its twenty eighteen preprint, which was called IntPhys, a framework and benchmark for visual intuitive physics reasoning, and the journal version, which is IntPhys twenty nineteen. The arXiv version three text and the journal author manuscript are the same body text, so the numbers are stable across both. But if you search for it you will find two titles for what is effectively one paper.
[S] Good. Then start with the gap. What was wrong with how people were evaluating physics models before this?
[G] The complaint is specifically about evaluation, not about training. Most of the machine vision work on intuitive physics trained models to predict future frames, or future object states, on the theory that predicting the physical world well requires understanding it. Fine. But then those models got scored on raw prediction loss, and the authors argue that a raw prediction loss is a bad instrument for this question, for three separate reasons.
[O] Give me the three.
[G] First, it is hard to interpret in absolute terms, and it depends on the sensor's scale and resolution, so a loss value does not transfer across datasets. Second, it does not rank systems the way you would want. A model that nails every object position but gets the colour or texture wrong takes a large penalty for something irrelevant to physics. Third, and this is the subtle one, macroscopic physics is deterministic, but its real world outcomes are often practically stochastic. The paper's own line is that this is why people play dice. A large prediction error is therefore not, by itself, evidence that a system misunderstands physics.
[S] So what replaces it?
[G] The prediction error is used only indirectly. The benchmark asks a system for one scalar per video clip, a plausibility score, and then checks only one thing, that the score behaves the way it should if the system knows which events are physically possible. Lower for a clip containing a violation than for a matched clip without one. That is the whole interface. Any vision system that can emit one number per clip can be scored, whether it does captioning, tracking, three D reconstruction, or planning.
[O] I want to sit on that for a second, because I think it is the underrated design decision. It makes the benchmark model agnostic in a way that most video benchmarks are not. You are not required to be a next frame predictor to take the test.
[G] That is right, and the authors are careful about what that buys them. They call it a diagnostic unit test for physics, explicitly not a full end to end capability test. A system could ace this and still fail to connect that physical knowledge to language or planning.
[S] Where does the infant literature come in? I want to know whether the developmental framing is load bearing or decorative.
[G] It is load bearing for the design of the test items, and it comes with a timeline. Days after birth, infants can parse visual input into solid objects. At two to four months they understand object permanence, and they expect objects to follow spatio-temporally continuous trajectories. At six months come stability, support, and causality. Between eight and ten months, gravity, inertia, and conservation of momentum in collisions. And between ten and twelve months, shape constancy, which is the latest of the three concepts this release actually tests.
[O] So shape constancy arrives last in a human infant, but it sits in the middle of the benchmark's three blocks.
[G] Correct, and it is worth noticing that the block numbering is not a difficulty ordering or a developmental ordering. The three blocks are object permanence, shape constancy, and spatio-temporal continuity, and the paper pairs each with a specific tracking challenge. Object permanence is occlusion resistant tracking. Shape constancy is appearance robust tracking, robust to changes in lighting, viewpoint, distance. Spatio-temporal continuity is tracking and predicting trajectories, including through occlusion.
[S] And the violation of expectation paradigm itself. In the infant lab, you show a baby something impossible and measure looking time. What is the machine analogue?
[G] The machine analogue is the matched set, and this is where the engineering is. Take block one, object permanence. A scene begins with one or two objects, an occluding screen rises and then falls, and the scene resumes. Combine the two possible beginnings with the two possible endings and you get four movies. Two are possible, the object count is unchanged across the occlusion. Two are impossible, the count changes. And critically, across all four movies the frames are pixel identical up to the point of divergence. Only the temporal coherence differs.
[O] That is a genuinely nice control. You cannot win by keying on low level pixel statistics, because the pixels are the same until the moment that matters.
[S] Unless the divergence point itself leaks. But grant it. How large is the test set, and how is it generated?
[G] Everything is procedurally generated in Unreal Engine four. Within each block, complexity is varied along three axes. Whether the violation happens in the open, which they call visible, or behind the occluder, which they call occluded. Motion complexity, which is static, or dynamic with one occlusion event, or dynamic with two occlusion events whose violations are inverses of each other, so that the clip's first and last segments look entirely consistent and only the middle diverges. And object count, one, two or three. That is two by three by three, so eighteen scenarios per block, at two hundred renderings each, giving three thousand six hundred test movies per block. There is also a smaller development set of three hundred sixty movies per block.
[O] And the training set?
[G] Fifteen thousand clips of physically possible events only, around seven seconds each at fifteen frames per second, about twenty-one hours of video in total, reusing the same objects, occluders and environments as the test set. It ships each object's ground truth three D position, the camera pose, and instance segmentation masks with object identities correctly linked across frames.
[S] Wait. If the training set hands you linked object identities across frames, that is a very strong supervision signal for exactly the thing being tested.
[G] It is, and the authors anticipated that. In the development and test sets, the object identity link is deliberately removed. The identities are randomly shuffled between frames. So linking masks over time is itself part of the object permanence problem the system is meant to solve at test time. You can learn from the linked training data, but you cannot lean on it when you are being scored.
[O] Now the two metrics, because that is where our cold open lives. Walk us through both, carefully.
[G] Both are error rates. Lower is better, and chance is point five zero for both. The first is L R, the relative error. Within each matched set, you take the sum of the plausibility scores the system assigned to the possible movies, and the sum it assigned to the impossible movies, and L R is the fraction of matched sets where the possible sum is not greater than the impossible sum. It is a within set ranking check. Does the model separate possible from impossible inside its own quadruplet.
[S] So it never compares across sets.
[G] Never. Which is precisely what the second metric does. L A, the absolute error, is one minus the area under the R O C curve, computed by pooling every possible clip's score against every impossible clip's score across the entire test set. Not set by set.
[O] So L A demands calibration and L R does not.
[G] That is the cleanest way to put it. L R only asks the score to rank correctly inside its own quadruplet. L A asks the score to be well calibrated across the whole dataset, which means an easy quadruplet's impossible clip must not out score a hard quadruplet's possible clip. They are genuinely different questions, and neither is a plain accuracy. I want to be firm on that, because people do quote these as accuracies and they are not. One is a set relative ranking error, the other is a pooled area under the curve error.
[S] Understood. And the baselines?
[G] Two, both shipped with the benchmark. One is a convolutional encoder decoder with a ResNet eighteen backbone. The other is a conditional G A N, structured along the lines of D C G A N. Both are trained self-supervised on the same task, predict a future three class semantic mask, background, occluder, object, from two past frames. And both are trained at two prediction horizons, a short term one predicting five frames ahead and a long term one predicting thirty-five frames ahead.
[O] How does a mask predictor turn into a single plausibility number?
[G] The frame level prediction plausibility is aggregated over the clip by taking the minimum. So a clip is only as plausible as its single most surprising frame. That is a deliberate choice and it matters for interpreting the results, because it means one bad moment dominates the score.
[S] And the human reference. I want the protocol, not a summary of it.
[G] Five hundred forty people on Amazon Mechanical Turk. Each was first shown eight examples of possible scenes from the training set, some simple and some more complex. They were told that some of the test movies would show events that could not possibly take place in the real world, without being told how. Each participant then saw forty randomly selected videos and rated each one from one, most implausible, to six, most plausible. The task took about seven minutes and paid one dollar. Every video in the test set was seen by two different participants. Then the same L R and L A formulas were applied to those ratings, exactly as for the models.

[G] Two places.
