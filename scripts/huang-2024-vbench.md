[G] The paper's own list in the introduction is Inception Score, Fréchet Inception Distance, Fréchet Video Distance, and CLIPSIM, and its charge against all four is that they are inconsistent with human judgement — that is the paper's phrase, with citations attached. But the deeper complaint is structural. [O] And the prompts feeding those metrics were borrowed rather than designed.
[G] Right. Inception Score, F I D and F V D lean on U C F one oh one's action class labels. CLIPSIM leans on M S R V T T's human-written captions. The paper also notes that off-the-shelf video quality assessment methods are built for camera-captured footage and do not account for the artifacts synthesis produces.
[S] So the bet is that video generation quality is not one thing.
[G] The bet is that it is at least sixteen things, each of which needs its own prompt set, its own automatic metric, and independent evidence that the metric agrees with a human.
[O] Take us through the sixteen, because the structure is the contribution.
[G] Two branches at the top. Video Quality asks whether the video looks good ignoring the prompt entirely. Background Consistency is CLIP feature similarity across frames. Temporal Flickering is a mean absolute difference across static frames. [O] That is eight or nine separate off-the-shelf models doing the grading.
[G] It is, and that is a genuine design decision with a cost attached. Every one of those detectors carries its own failure modes into the benchmark, and VBench inherits all of them. The authors' answer to that is the human alignment study, which we will get to.

[G] Two prompt suites. Per dimension, the authors hand-curate what they call around one hundred prompts for each of the sixteen, and I would keep that hedge. [S] Now the human study, which is the load-bearing part.
[G] Section three point three. For a given prompt, all four models generate a video, which forms a group of four. Five such groups are sampled per prompt. Each group is broken into all six pairwise combinations, and an annotator picks the better video for one specific dimension only, with video order randomised. The paper states the formula directly: a suite of N prompts yields N times five times six pairwise comparisons per dimension.
[O] So with roughly one hundred prompts per dimension, that is on the order of three thousand comparisons per dimension.
[G] That arithmetic is yours and mine, not the paper's — it prints the formula and the around-one-hundred hedge, not the product. But yes, the order of magnitude is thousands per dimension, and twenty videos per prompt. That is a serious labelling exercise, and I want to give it full credit before anyone criticises what happens to it downstream.
[S] What was the quality control?
[G] Supplementary section I point one, and it is a five-step procedure. Written instruction documents per dimension, including what to consider and explicitly what to discard. A sixty-sample pre-labelling trial where every wrong label is discussed with the annotator. Instructions updated from that trial. Then post-labelling checks: samples are packaged in sixties, annotators spot-check a random twenty percent of each package, and if the error rate in that twenty percent exceeds ten percent the whole package goes back to a different annotator. Finally the authors run the same check themselves, and if a package still exceeds ten percent, the entire dimension's data is thrown out and redone.
[S] That is more rigour than most papers bother with. And yet.
[G] And yet it is an error-rate process, not an agreement statistic. Every one of those five steps measures labels against the authors' own adjudication of what is correct.
