[G] Thank you. And I want to plant a flag on the framing before we start, because it is the thing this paper is easiest to misquote. The benchmark reports a human reference row alongside the model rows, and those are two different kinds of row. Every time I give a model number today I will say it is a model number, and every time I give the human number I will say it is the human reference. If you blur those together you get a completely wrong picture of what the paper found.
[O] Noted, and I will hold you to it. Start with the gap. Why did this need to exist?
[G] Because two literatures grew up separately and each one is missing what the other has. On one side you have mainstream multimodal benchmarks — M M E, Seed Bench, M M M U — where the item is a fixed image or a fixed video with a question attached. Nothing the model says changes what it sees next. So those benchmarks structurally cannot test whether a model can move, look around, pick something up, or recover from its own mistake.
[S] And on the other side, embodied benchmarks that do put an agent in a simulator.
[G] Right, and the paper argues those fall short on three separate axes. First, diversity. It points at ALFRED, which the paper says includes just seven predefined tasks within four room types — most embodied benchmarks lean on a handful of fixed templates rather than open-ended task generation. Second, the input and output format. Benchmarks built around vision-and-language navigation, or around three D point-based action spaces, demand a low-level output that today's multimodal models simply cannot emit natively, so evaluating an off-the-shelf model against them is awkward at best. Third, several benchmarks dodge that problem by describing the environment in text instead of showing it, which is convenient for a language model and throws away exactly the visual grounding and spatial reasoning that make an agent embodied in the first place.
[O] So the pitch is: real rendered scenes, tasks that are not template-bound, and an output format a general instruction-following model can already produce.
[G] That is the pitch, and the third piece is the load-bearing one. We will come back to it, because it is simultaneously the paper's smartest decision and its most obvious critique surface.
[S] Let us do mechanics. What is the actual loop?
[G] It runs on top of Legent, an open platform for embodied agents from the same group. The simulator initializes a three D scene and drops the agent somewhere in it. At each step the model receives the task description, the current option list, the accumulated observation history — egocentric images, or a video for the video-native models — and the action history. It picks one option. The environment executes that action, updates the world, and returns a new observation plus feedback on whether the action succeeded, and all of that is appended back into the history for the next step.
[O] And it ends when?
[G] Three ways. When a set of predefined success predicates are all satisfied. When the step budget runs out — the maximum number of attempt steps per task is set to twenty-four in the main experiments. Or, for the question-answering categories, as soon as the model commits to an answer. The paper is explicit here, and I will quote it exactly: "Once an answer is chosen, the task is immediately judged as correct or incorrect."
[S] Wait. Say more about that, because I think it changes how the Q A scores read.
[G] It does, and it is under-discussed. On a question-answering task the episode can terminate on a wrong answer. It is one-shot. The model can explore as long as it likes before committing, but the commitment itself is terminal. So a Q A score in this benchmark is not "did the model eventually get there" — it is "did the model both gather enough evidence and know when to stop." Those are different skills and they are fused into one number.
[O] Which cuts both ways for me. It is stricter than I assumed, and stricter is usually better.
[S] Agreed, actually. I will take a hard commit over a benchmark that lets you keep guessing. Alice, the output space — this is the thing you flagged.
[G] Here it is. The model never emits a coordinate, a grasp pose, or a free-form sentence of intent. Movement is constrained to a pre-built navigation graph for each scene, so the model chooses a direction among navigation points rather than producing a three D position. Object interaction uses an open-vocabulary but still discrete action list, where each action comes bundled with the objects it applies to and the conditions under which it succeeds. Question answering is multiple choice over annotated textual answers. At every step, the model is doing constrained selection.
[O] And that is why nineteen off-the-shelf models could be evaluated at all, with no fine-tuning and no bolted-on grounding head.
[G] Precisely, and the paper discloses it as a design decision rather than burying it. There is also a protocol detail worth naming: the models are prompted with chain of thought. The paper says, quote, "We prompt the model to output thoughts before deciding options." So the numbers we are about to discuss are chain-of-thought numbers, not raw single-shot picks.
[S] What are the other knobs?
[G] Image resolution is four forty-eight by four forty-eight, field of view ninety degrees, and temperature is set to zero for every model. That last one is interesting, because the appendix says all models perform slightly better at temperature one. The authors chose zero anyway, on the argument that removing sampling randomness better isolates whether a model can recognize and escape its own erroneous action trajectory.
[O] I like that reasoning and I suspect it costs them a couple of points across the board.
[S] It probably does. Tell me about the tasks themselves.
[G] Five categories. Navigation, coarse or fine-grained, reach a location or find an object. Object interaction, meaning actually modifying the environment. Social interaction, human-agent exchanges — delivering an item, interpreting feedback, perspective-taking. Attribute question answering, explore then answer about object and scene attributes. And spatial question answering, about size, distance, layout, and spatial relationships, where you have to move to gather the evidence rather than just look.
[O] And where do the scenes come from?
[G] Four sources: A I two Thor, plus public-space assets from H S S D and Sketchfab picked by human experts, and a large share of indoor scenes generated procedurally from Objaverse assets. The generation pipeline is worth getting right because it is easy to garble. G P T three point five was used twice, for two different jobs. First to filter out unsuitable outdoor assets, which left a database of about fifteen thousand objects spanning over five hundred categories. Then, separately, to annotate room occurrence, position, and function — and crucially, at the category level, not per instance. A different model, Gemini one point five Flash, did its own pass labelling the orientations of large objects. Then a procedural arrangement step, then hand-editing in a custom runtime scene editor.
[S] So it is not one model refining another model's output.
[G] Correct, and I have seen that misread. Two different jobs for G P T three point five, one separate job for Gemini, then procedure and then humans.
[O] And the tasks?
[G] Seeded from over thirty existing datasets, then expanded by prompting Claude and ChatGPT for diverse novel variants targeting grounding, episodic memory, spatial reasoning, quantitative reasoning, common-sense reasoning, and planning. That produced a candidate pool of over three hundred tasks. Then eight expert annotators, from professional data annotation companies, aligned each candidate to a scene, configured its action and predicate output space, and defined the success criteria — over two rounds, completeness first and then diversity, clarity and difficulty. Every task independently checked by at least three reviewers, and validated by running expert demonstrations in the simulator.
[S] That is more annotation rigour than I expected. Final size?
[G] Three hundred and twenty-eight tasks, one hundred and twenty-five unique scenes, five hundred and seventy-five predicate instances, and one thousand five hundred and thirty-three options — one thousand two hundred and thirteen textual answers and three hundred and twenty interaction options. [O] So what changes if this holds?
[G] Two things I would watch. First, it gives the field a cheap, reproducible interactive harness — the models plug in through ordinary instruction-following, which means the cost of adding a new model to this leaderboard is nearly zero. That matters for keeping an eval alive as models turn over. Second, the failure taxonomy is more actionable than the aggregate score. [S] For evaluation practice specifically, my takeaway is about interface design as a hidden construct. The moment you make an eval tractable by constraining the output space, you have changed what the eval measures, and the score inherits that constraint forever.
