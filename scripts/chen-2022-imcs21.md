The corpus is called IMCS twenty one.
[S] It is on the docket because it is a five-task benchmark on a single shared corpus, which is a design choice with real consequences, and because the paper is a nice case study in how much of a benchmark's meaning lives below the abstract.
[O] Lena, thank you for coming on. You have spent time with this one. Give us the shape of it.
[G] Happy to be here. The shape is: one corpus, four thousand one hundred and sixteen annotated Chinese doctor-patient consultations, one hundred sixty four thousand seven hundred and thirty one utterances, drawn from Muzhi, a Chinese online pediatric health community, and covering ten pediatric diseases. On top of that corpus the authors define five tasks — named entity recognition, dialogue act classification, symptom label inference, medical report generation, and diagnosis-oriented dialogue policy. Each task gets its own metrics and its own baseline family, all on one shared train, dev, and test split.
[S] Before the method, give me the gap. Why did this need to exist in 2022? There were already Chinese medical dialogue corpora.
[G] There were, and the paper sorts them into two buckets. Bucket one is raw, unannotated conversation logs at enormous scale — MedDialog with three point four million Chinese conversations, KaMed with more than sixty thousand sessions. Those support end-to-end response generation, and the authors' complaint is that chatbot outputs trained that way, in their words, lack interpretability and controllability, which they argue is a hard limitation in healthcare. Bucket two is a scatter of smaller annotated sets, most built for exactly one modelling task apiece — one for slot filling, three for medical information extraction, three carrying structured symptom features for diagnosis policy, one for end-to-end diagnosis-oriented generation.
[O] So the field had either scale without labels, or labels without breadth.
[G] That is the argument, and I want to be careful about how the paper phrases it, because it matters. The authors write that the majority of those datasets provide only one specific medical label, and that annotated sets are typically on the order of hundreds of dialogues. The majority. Not all of them. And that hedge is doing real work, because the paper itself describes MedDG in the same related-work paragraph as having more than seventeen thousand conversations with annotated entities and two established tasks. So by the paper's own account there is at least one prior dataset that does not fit the critique it is making.
[S] I appreciate that you led with that, because the usual move in a benchmark introduction is a blanket dismissal of everything prior. They hedged, and the hedge is accurate.
[O] Then let us get into the design. Lena, take us through the two frameworks.
[G] The authors split automatic medical consultation into a static half and a dynamic half. The static half they call the Dialogue Understanding framework — you have a recorded conversation, extract structure from it. The dynamic half is Task-oriented Interaction — you are in a conversation, decide what to ask next. Four of the five tasks live in the static half, one in the dynamic half, and the interesting bit is that they are deliberately coupled rather than independent.
[O] Walk the static four.
[G] They are organized by granularity, which I find genuinely elegant. At the token level you have named entity recognition. Five entity categories chosen with domain experts — symptom, drug name, drug category, examination, and operation — under a BIO tagging scheme, which gives eleven possible tags per Chinese character. Drug name versus drug category is a real distinction here: aspirin is a drug name, anti-inflammatory drug is a drug category.
[S] Utterance level next.
[G] Dialogue act classification. Every utterance gets one of sixteen labels. The scheme is a cross product: two speech acts, request and inform, against eight content categories — basic information, symptom, etiology, existing exam and treatment, medical advice, drug recommendation, precautions, and diagnosis. Diagnosis only exists in the inform version, which is why you get fifteen rather than sixteen from the cross product, plus an "other" catch-all.
[O] And then the whole dialogue.
[G] Two tasks at the dialogue level. Symptom label inference asks which of three hundred and thirty one normalized symptom names came up, and for each one whether the patient is positive, negative, or not sure. The symptom names are normalized against SNOMED CT, so different phrasings of the same symptom collapse to one standard entry. And medical report generation asks the model to write a six-part structured report from the conversation — chief complaint, present disease, auxiliary examination, past medical history, diagnosis, and suggestions.
[S] Six-part reports were written by hand by the annotators?
[G] Yes. Annotators were required to fill in each part and leave it blank if the conversation never mentioned it. And on annotation generally, the process is better instrumented than most benchmark papers bother with. Undergraduates and postgraduates from medical school, two independent annotations per dialogue, a third annotator resolving disagreements. They report Cohen's kappa: eighty three point one one percent for named entities, seventy six point four one percent for dialogue acts, and ninety two point seven one percent for symptom labels.
[O] That symptom-label kappa is very high.
[G] It is, and it is worth noting they did not report a kappa for medical reports at all — for those they simply kept both annotations for reference, which is the honest thing to do given you cannot meaningfully compute agreement on free text.
[S] Now the dynamic task. This is where I expect the trouble.
[G] Diagnosis-oriented dialogue policy. The agent is given the patient's explicit symptoms from the self-report, and its job is to query for implicit symptoms and then commit to a disease, within a capped number of interactions — the authors set the cap at ten. The crucial detail is the patient simulator. It follows the design from Wei and colleagues in 2018, and it is a lookup function, not a language model. You hand it a patient id and a symptom, it returns that patient's label for that symptom, and it returns Unknown if that symptom never appeared in the dialogue.
[S] So the dynamic task is not actually a conversation.
[G] It is not, and the paper says so plainly. They write that a more practical simulator taking natural language in and out would require a language transmitter and a language interpreter, and they name that as future work explicitly beyond the scope of the article, because their diagnosis-oriented dialogue policy task focuses on policy learning with structured data. I think it is important to give them credit for stating that rather than letting readers assume otherwise.
[O] Where is the coupling you mentioned?
[G] Symptom label inference is trained to produce exactly the structured symptom features that the policy task consumes. So the static half is, by construction, the feature extractor for the dynamic half. That is the argument for one corpus rather than five — the tasks share representations, not just a file format.
[O] Splits?
[G] Two thousand four hundred and seventy two dialogues for training, eight hundred and thirty three for development, eight hundred and eleven for test. Identical across all five tasks, split at the dialogue level, so no conversation has its turns scattered across partitions.
[S] Good. Let us do results, and Lena, I want you to hold the line on which task each number belongs to, because with five tasks and five metric families this is exactly where people blur things.
[G] Agreed, and I will name the task, the model, and the metric every time. Start with the corpus analysis, before any model. Symptom entities are the single most common annotated entity category at roughly fifty eight point three percent of all tagged entities. Among dialogue acts, the two most frequent categories are inform-symptom and request-symptom — the patient telling the doctor a symptom and the doctor asking about one. The authors read that as confirmation that doctor-patient conversations mainly discuss the patient's symptoms.
[O] Any structure across the conversation?
[G] Yes, and this is my favorite figure in the paper. They divide each dialogue into five positional fifths and plot where each dialogue act category concentrates. Requests about basic information and symptoms cluster early; acts about drugs, treatments and precautions cluster late. The consultation has a visible arc.

[O] Tell me about the explicit versus implicit gap, because I think that is the most interesting number in the paper.
[G] It is stark. Explicit symptoms — symptoms the patient volunteers in the self-report — reach subset accuracy in the low-to-mid seventies. Implicit symptoms, the ones scattered through the full dialogue, collapse to subset accuracy between thirty four point one six and thirty seven point four eight. Same task family, roughly half the score.
[S] But the label-level F one stays around ninety for both, which is the trap.
[G] Exactly the trap, and it is worth being precise about why. Subset accuracy demands you get every symptom in a dialogue exactly right. Label-level F one credits you per symptom. With dozens of candidate symptoms in a single conversation, the strict metric falls off a cliff while the lenient one barely moves. On implicit symptoms the label-level F ones run from eighty nine point six three up to ninety one point six two, best for the BERT multi-task variant, while subset accuracy sits in the mid thirties. The authors say so themselves — strict prediction of symptoms is very challenging.

Five tasks, one corpus, one split, and the tasks are wired together — the symptom inference task feeds the policy task by design. [S] What do I get beyond variance?
[G] You get the scope of the policy task, and I think it is the biggest caveat in the paper.
