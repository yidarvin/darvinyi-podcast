[O] That sounds like an embarrassment of riches, not a problem.
[G] It's a problem the moment you slice it. The authors say that when they inspected examples inside those long transcripts, people are talking about three or more distinct topics, or scenes. [S] And the third family, Twitter and Weibo.
[G] Post-and-reply pairs scraped off a social network. Noisy, informal, frequently more than two participants, and structurally not a conversation at all, it's a broadcast with responses attached. [O] So the gap is a corpus that's open-domain but still topic-coherent and genuinely two-party.

[G] They crawl raw text from various websites that serve English learners practising daily dialogue. Hence the name. [G] Two axes, both applied by hand to every single utterance. Dialog act follows the criteria of Amanova and colleagues from 2016, chosen because it's compatible with the ISO twenty-four-six-one-seven standard and with existing annotated corpora like Trains and DBox. Four classes. Inform, statements and questions by which the speaker provides information. Questions, where the speaker is seeking information. Directives, covering request, instruct, suggest, and accept or reject an offer. And Commissive, accepting or rejecting a request, suggestion, or offer. [S] Four classes is very coarse.
[G] Deliberately. The ISO scheme can go far finer. They took a compressed version that's still standard-compatible, which is precisely why cross-corpus comparison with Trains and DBox remains possible at all.

[G] Ekman's Big Six from 1992. Anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise, plus an added Other category for everything that doesn't fit. Seven classes. [O] So what changes if you take this paper seriously today?
[G] Two things for evaluation practice. First, this is a case study in a dataset's own reported imbalance becoming the field's unmanaged problem. Eighty-three percent majority class was right there, in a table, with a footnote, and it propagated anyway, because nothing about the release forced downstream users to handle it consistently. If you ship an eval today, ship the reporting convention with it, not just the data.
[S] And the second?
[G] The self-aware-but-unaddressed pattern. This paper cites Liu and colleagues on how not to evaluate your dialogue system, agrees that word-overlap metrics are inadequate for open-domain generation, calls its own equivalence metric weak, and then reports Bleu and equivalence with no human evaluation of a single generated or retrieved response. Naming a validity threat is not the same as measuring it. That is still the most common failure I see in evaluation papers.
