[O] Picture the simplest possible judge call. A language model is asked which of two answers is better for a riddle about a footrace: you've just passed the person in second place, what place are you in now, and where is the person you passed. Answer A gets it right, second place, they're now third. Answer B just says, I stopped to tie my shoelaces and I'm not racing anymore, which doesn't even engage the question. Left alone, the judge picks A every time. Then today's paper appends twenty extra tokens to answer B, tokens that never even show up in what the judge prints back to you, and the same judge flips and declares B the winner.

[G] Happy to be here. It earns the slot because LLM-as-a-Judge stopped being a research toy a while back — it's the mechanism deciding leaderboard rankings, the automated annotator inside a lot of preference-tuning pipelines, and increasingly the thing filtering search results and choosing which tool an agent calls. This paper is the first to treat attacking that judge as a real optimization problem instead of a handcrafted trick, and the headline numbers are genuinely striking. But the seam you both just poked at, the "no matter what" line, is real, and it's worth the whole episode.
[S] Let's set up the target properly before we get to the attack. What is LLM-as-a-Judge actually being used for, mechanically?
[G] Mechanically it's simple. You give a model a question, a set of candidate responses, and an instruction that says pick the best one and output only "Output, index, is better," no explanation. Under the hood it's one forward pass over a header instruction, the question, every candidate concatenated together, and a trailer instruction repeating the task — that repeated trailer is called a sandwich template, specifically designed so an injected instruction hidden in the middle can't hijack the model's final task. The idea goes back to a separate twenty twenty-four NeurIPS paper that introduced the concept using MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, and it caught on fast because it's cheap and it correlates well with human judgment.
[O] And "cheap and correlates with humans" is exactly why it spread everywhere. It's not just judging chatbot outputs anymore.
[G] Right, the paper names three concrete deployments beyond leaderboards. LLM-powered search, where the judge filters and ranks results before they reach you. RLAIF, reinforcement learning from AI feedback, where the judge stands in for a human annotator and its preference becomes the training signal a later model gets aligned on. And tool selection for LLM agents, where the judge picks which registered tool description best matches what the user asked for.
[S] Which means in every one of those settings, the judge is reading text that somebody besides the judge's operator wrote. Search results are scraped from the open web. Tool descriptions are registered by third-party developers. Leaderboard submissions come from whoever's model is being ranked.
[G] Exactly, and that's the opening the paper walks through. Prompt injection against LLMs isn't new, burying an instruction inside data the model processes has been documented since twenty twenty-two. What's new here is applying it specifically to a judge, and the paper is precise about why that's a harder problem than injecting into an ordinary chatbot. Normal prompt injection assumes the attacker controls the entire input. A judge attacker controls exactly one candidate response and nothing else.
[O] Meaning they don't get to see the competition.
[G] Correct, and the paper's own threat model spells this out explicitly. The attacker knows the target question and their own response. They do not have access to the other candidate responses, they don't know how many of them there are, and they don't know what position their own response will land in once everything's assembled. Three unknowns, and the injected sequence has to work despite all three, while also surviving that sandwich template built specifically to blunt this kind of attack.
[S] So before this paper, what did an attacker actually do?
[G] Handcrafted heuristics, mostly. Just append a sentence telling the judge to pick you. Sprinkle in escape characters like newlines to visually separate your instruction from the response text. Tell the model to ignore its previous instructions. Pretend the original task already finished, so the model treats your text as a fresh command. The paper benchmarks six variants like that directly, plus four jailbreak methods repurposed from a completely different problem, models designed to bypass a model's own safety training, where by construction the attacker owns the entire prompt. None of those six assumptions match a judge's actual situation, and that mismatch is the gap JudgeDeceiver is built to close.
[O] So walk us through it. What does closing that gap actually mean, mechanically?
[G] It means treating "find twenty tokens that hijack the judge" as a discrete optimization problem, in the tradition of gradient-guided token search — the same family as a jailbreak method called GCG, which we'll come back to as a baseline. Formally, the judge is a function that eats a header instruction, the question, every candidate response concatenated together, and a trailer instruction, and outputs a judgment naming the winning index. The attacker appends a sequence of twenty tokens to their own response and wants that same function to instead output their chosen index.
[S] But they don't know the other candidates, you just said. So what are they optimizing against?
[G] This is the first clever piece. Since the attacker can't see the real candidates, they build a stand-in, a shadow candidate response dataset. For the target question, they use an off-the-shelf model to generate a handful of plausible rival answers, and during optimization they sample a smaller working set from that pool, three shadow responses by default standing in for the unknown real ones. Everything downstream is optimized against those stand-ins, never against the real competition, which the attacker won't see until the attack is already live.
[O] And "three" is going to matter a lot later in this conversation.
[G] Hold onto it, it's going to matter enormously. With the shadow set in hand, they optimize a weighted sum of three loss terms. The first, the target-aligned loss, just pushes the judge's output distribution toward literally generating the target verdict text for the target response, straightforward pressure toward "Output, this index, is better."
[S] That alone doesn't sound like it should need two more terms.
[G] It doesn't, on its own, and that's the second clever piece. As you add shadow candidates to make the attack realistic, the target response's position in the list gets harder to pin down during optimization, sometimes it's candidate two, sometimes candidate four, depending on how the shadow set gets assembled. So there's a second term, the target-enhancement loss, that separately maximizes the probability of just the positional index token, independent of the rest of the verdict. That's what makes the attack robust to where the response actually lands, and it's also what lets it survive a defense that swaps response order to check for consistency.
[O] So term two is specifically buying robustness to the one thing a judge operator might actually do to catch you, reordering the candidates.
[G] Exactly that. The third term is adversarial perplexity: it penalizes the injected tokens for reading as obvious garbage, pushing them toward something with lower measured perplexity so a fluency-based filter is less likely to flag it. All three terms get summed with two tunable weights and minimized together with a greedy, gradient-guided coordinate search — compute the gradient with respect to a one-hot token encoding at each position, take the most promising replacement candidates per position, randomly try a batch of substitutions, keep whichever one drops the loss the most, repeat. Six hundred iterations, and critically, the loss at every step is summed across every shadow candidate set and every position index the target response might occupy, so the final twenty tokens have been stress-tested against a spread of scenarios, not just one.
[S] That's a lot of moving parts for something that's supposedly practical to deploy. Doesn't three loss terms plus six hundred iterations of gradient search put this out of reach for a casual attacker?
[G] It's out of reach for someone with no machine-learning background, sure. But worth flagging honestly, the paper never actually reports wall-clock time or specific hardware for its own attack, only a qualitative note that a larger shadow count costs more compute and GPU memory. That's a real gap in what we can say about accessibility. What we do know is it sits in the same method family as GCG, which the field already treats as practical enough to run outside a dedicated lab.
[O] What does the result actually look like, as text? "Twenty optimized tokens" could mean anything from readable English to line noise.
[G] It's genuinely closer to line noise before the perplexity term is doing its job, stray symbols, code fragments, capitalization in strange places. Once the stealth term is active, it's still not fluent, but it starts looking more like fragments of real phrases stitched together, which is enough to meaningfully lower its measured perplexity even though a human reading it would still find it odd.
[S] And nobody grading the leaderboard ever actually sees any of this, right? That's the detail that got me in the cold open.
[G] Right, and that's a structural feature of the setup, not extra work the attacker has to do. The judge's output format is constrained to exactly "Output, this index, is better," no explanation allowed. So even with the injected tokens sitting right there in the submitted response, the judge's own printed verdict never surfaces them. Anyone auditing outcomes only ever sees a clean-looking ranking.
[O] Let's get to numbers, then.
[S] And let's be disciplined about which number is which, because this paper has four metrics that all look alike on the page.
[G] Fair, let me define them once, cleanly. Success rate is the main one, the average probability the judge picks the target response once the injected tokens are attached, averaged across both possible orderings of the two responses. Positional consistency is stricter, it only counts a win if the attack succeeds in both orderings, not just on average, so it's always the harder number to hit. And there's a no-attack baseline, just how often the target response would have won anyway with nothing attached. That baseline starts near zero for the best-behaved judge, Openchat-3.5, and climbs to roughly a third for the weakest one, Llama-2-7B, but never gets close to the eighty-plus percent success rates the actual attack achieves.
[O] So give me the headline. What number does the abstract lead with?
[G] On Mistral-7B as the judge, on MT-Bench questions, success rate is ninety point eight percent, positional consistency eighty-three point four percent. Across all four open judges they tested and both benchmark datasets, success rate ranges from eighty-eight percent up to ninety-eight point nine percent, and positional consistency ranges from seventy-nine percent up to ninety-seven point eight percent.
[S] How does that compare to just trying harder with a handcrafted prompt?
[G] That's the manual-baseline table, six variants, everything from a bare "pick this one" sentence up to the paper's own hand-tuned attack against the sandwich template specifically, which they call Fake Reasoning. And its consistency score is worse than that comparison suggests, the paper states directly that no manual method anywhere in the table exceeds nineteen percent consistency. [O] And the jailbreak comparison, that's the one I find most persuasive, because those are real optimization methods, not handwritten sentences.

Nothing about that comparison was rerun, it was rescoped and expanded, not silently changed.
[S] Good, that's worth being exact about. So, on Mistral-7B, what do the four jailbreak methods get?
[G] Two tree-search and iterative-rewriting methods, TAP and PAIR, land under twenty percent success on both datasets. The two stronger ones: AutoDAN reaches fifty-three point six percent on MT-Bench, its best showing, and GCG reaches forty-nine point nine percent on LLMBar, its best showing. JudgeDeceiver gets ninety point eight and ninety-three point two on those same two rows. Here's a fairness detail worth flagging: JudgeDeceiver and GCG both optimize a twenty-token suffix, exactly the same length. TAP and PAIR are producing sequences roughly six to eight times longer, well over a hundred tokens on average, and still losing badly. So the gap isn't a longer-text advantage.
[O] What about compute budget, though, not text length? "Six hundred iterations" was mentioned earlier and I don't remember a comparable number for the baselines.

No iteration count, query count, or wall-clock figure is given anywhere for TAP, PAIR, AutoDAN, or GCG. The sequence-length comparison is clean and fair. The compute comparison isn't checkable at all.
[S] Noted, that's going in my column for later. What about the ablations, which piece of the three-term loss is actually doing the work?
[G] Held on one representative question and judge: the full recipe gets ninety-seven percent success, ninety-four percent consistency. Drop the target-aligned term and success falls to eighty-seven, consistency to seventy-eight. Drop the position-enhancement term and it falls further, to eighty-four and seventy-two. Those two are clearly load-bearing. Drop the stealth term, the perplexity one, and success actually ticks up slightly, to ninety-eight, consistency to ninety-six, so fluency costs a little raw power in exchange for evading a perplexity filter, exactly the trade you'd expect a stealth term to make.
[O] And the case studies, this is the part that made me sit up, because it's not just leaderboard vanity anymore.
[G] Right, three deployments, all landing between eighty and one hundred percent success. Attacking LLM-powered search, using real Google results as the clean candidates, stays in the nineties to one hundred percent through three and four rival results, with its single worst case at eighty percent once you push to five rivals. Attacking RLAIF annotation, on a real helpfulness-and-harmlessness preference dataset, holds ninety-five to one hundred percent, no-attack baseline at zero throughout, always exactly two candidates since that's how this pipeline compares a chosen versus a rejected response. [S] Five queries, five instruction pairs, five tools. Those are small target sets. Doesn't that make the case-study claims fragile?
[G] It's not as thin as it looks. Five targets, meaningfully repeated, not five coin flips.
[S] The RLAIF result is the one I keep coming back to, independent of sample size. That's not one bad leaderboard entry, that's poisoning the preference signal a whole model gets trained on afterward.
[G] That's exactly the paper's point in including it. [O] Let's zoom out. Where does this actually connect to how eval and benchmarking work gets done day to day?
[G] Three places, I think. First, the obvious one: any leaderboard or benchmark that lets participants submit their own response text, rather than running a fixed model in a controlled harness, has to treat that text as untrusted input now, not just as content to be graded.
