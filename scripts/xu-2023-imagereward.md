[S] Good. Then the gap, Alistair. What was broken about evaluating these models in early 2023?
[G] Two separate things, and the paper is really two papers stapled together along that seam. The evaluation half first. The standard metrics were Frechet Inception Distance and Inception Score, and both are batch-level statistics over the model's own output distribution. FID additionally compares that distribution against a set of real reference photographs. Neither one ever asks whether a particular image matches the particular prompt that produced it.
[O] And the per-image alternative was CLIP score.
[G] CLIP score or BLIP score, both just cosine similarity between a text embedding and an image embedding. Which measures one thing, alignment, and is silent on the other three failure modes the authors open the paper with. They name four: text-image alignment, the body problem, meaning distorted or duplicated limbs, human aesthetic preference, and toxicity and bias. A similarity score has no opinion about a hand with six fingers.
[S] And the FID complaint specifically.
[G] Two prongs. One, the conventional protocol is zero-shot FID against COCO, and the authors point out that many of these generators may well have seen COCO-like data in pre-training, so zero-shot is doing some work there it may not have earned. Two, and this is the sharper one, FID averages over a whole dataset. A person generating images is not averaging over a dataset. They are looking at a batch of eight and picking one. A whole-dataset statistic cannot function as a per-image selector, and selection is the actual user behaviour.
[O] What is the training half of the gap?
[G] That RLHF does not transfer. In language, the recipe is settled: collect pairwise comparisons, train a reward model, optimise the policy against it with PPO. All of that leans on the policy having a likelihood for the thing it generated, token by token. A diffusion model has no likelihood for a finished image. The image is the endpoint of a forty-step denoising chain, not a sampled sequence.
[S] Classifier guidance exists.
[G] It does, and the authors reject it for a specific and rather elegant reason. Classifier guidance is inference-time only, and it requires training a classifier on noisy intermediate latents. But human raters only ever judge fully denoised images. So you would be training the guidance signal on a kind of input no human has ever expressed a preference about. The paper's phrasing is that it contradicts the annotation, where images need to be completely denoised for humans to mark correct preference.
[O] So what did people do instead, before this?
[G] Indirect things. Filter your fine-tuning dataset by reward score and train on the survivors. Or re-weight the fine-tuning loss by each sample's quality. Both are data-oriented workarounds. Neither ever puts a reward gradient into the model's weights. The authors' word for them is virtually indirect.
[S] Right, let us do the dataset, because I suspect it is the most durable thing here.
[G] I think that is right, and it is a genuinely careful piece of work. Prompts come from DiffusionDB, which is one point eight million real user prompts. They diversity-select down to ten thousand using a graph algorithm over Sentence-BERT embeddings, each prompt connected to its one hundred and fifty nearest neighbours, then vertices picked repeatedly by a score the paper describes as related to the number of neighbours not yet selected. They do not write down the exact scoring function, which is a small reproducibility gap. Each surviving prompt keeps between four and nine of its sampled images.
[O] And the annotation is three stages, not one.
[G] Three, and the ordering matters. Stage one is annotating the prompt itself: category, whether it is ambiguous, whether it carries latent toxicity. Stage two is rating every image on three separate seven-point scales, overall, image-text alignment, and fidelity, plus binary checkboxes for specific problems like the body problem or toxic content. Stage three is ranking all of that prompt's images into five preference slots, ties allowed. The pairwise training data is derived from stage three.
[S] What happens when the criteria conflict? That is where most preference datasets quietly fall apart.
[G] They wrote a policy, and I think it is the single most underrated thing in the paper. Fidelity and harmlessness generally outrank alignment. The exception is when an image is only slightly less faithful or slightly less harmless but clearly better aligned. So an annotator facing the classic case, one image matches the prompt better but has a mangled hand, has an actual documented rule rather than a private intuition.

[G] Two months of work, professional annotators recruited through a data annotation company, most with at least college-level education. [G] They did. Candidates are trained by researchers, then take a test, and their agreement with the researchers and with the annotator ensemble is measured. Low scorers are not employed. [O] Alistair, the model itself. Why BLIP and not CLIP, given CLIP was the obvious default?
[G] They ablate it, and the answer is unusually clean. The architecture is a BLIP backbone, a ViT-Large image encoder and a twelve-layer text encoder, combined through cross-attention, then an MLP head down to one scalar. Training is the standard pairwise logistic loss: for a prompt and a preferred and dispreferred image, maximise the log sigmoid of the score difference. Same loss as language reward models.
[S] And the CLIP comparison.
[G] They bolt the same MLP head onto CLIP and train it the same way. CLIP at four thousand training prompts gets sixty one point eight seven percent. At eight thousand, sixty two point nine eight. BLIP at one thousand prompts already gets sixty three point zero seven.
[O] So BLIP with an eighth of the data beats CLIP at full data.
[G] That is exactly the comparison the authors draw, and it is the strongest form of the argument, because it says more data does not buy you out of the weaker backbone. Their explanation is partly BLIP's bootstrapped training set, and partly that BLIP's image-grounded text encoder fuses the two modalities, where CLIP keeps separate encoders and only meets at a dot product.

[O] Now ReFL. This is the part I think is the real contribution, and I want it slowly.
[G] Then let me start with the observation rather than the algorithm, because ReFL is downstream of an empirical finding. They take the denoising trajectory, forty steps, and at each step they ask the model to predict the final image directly from where it currently is. Then they score that prediction with ImageReward. The question is: how early can you tell how good the finished image will be?
[S] And the answer.
[G] Three regimes. Through roughly step fifteen of forty, the scores are uniformly low and carry essentially no information. Between fifteen and thirty, good generations start to separate from bad ones, but not reliably. Past step thirty, the scores are reliably distinguishable across seeds. Figure four shows this with eight seeds on one prompt. The best seed goes from point three five at step thirty to point five nine at step forty. A bad seed only claws from minus two point one five to minus one point six two. The ordering is already settled by thirty.
[O] So the last quarter of the schedule is where the reward signal becomes real.
[G] And that is the whole opening. ReFL denoises without gradient tracking all the way down to a randomly chosen step in that late window. Then it takes one single step with gradients on. From that one step it predicts the final image directly, rather than iterating the rest of the way. It scores that prediction with ImageReward and back-propagates.
[S] The gradient path is one step deep.
[G] One step. That is what makes it tractable at all. You never back-propagate through forty denoising steps.
[O] Why random, though? Why not always attach at the last step, where the signal is strongest?
[G] They tried, and the paper is blunt: if only the gradient of the last denoising step is retained, the training is proved very unstable and the results are bad. Random sampling over the window is a variance-reduction and regularisation move.
[S] There is an indexing thing here that confused me on first read.
