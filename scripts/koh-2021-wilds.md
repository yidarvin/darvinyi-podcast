[S] Good. Then start with the gap. By twenty twenty there was no shortage of distribution shift benchmarks. What was wrong with them?
[G] That almost none of them contained a distribution shift that happens on its own. ImageNet-C corrupts images with synthetic noise and blur. The Backgrounds Challenge and Waterbirds swap image backgrounds. Colored MNIST recolours digits. PACS generalises from photographs to cartoons. These are controlled testbeds and they are genuinely useful as such. The authors' objection is a transfer objection: robustness to a synthetic shift need not transfer to the kinds of shift that arise in real deployments, and prior work had already demonstrated exactly that failure to transfer.
[O] So the fix is to stop simulating the shift.
[G] Stop simulating, and go find shifts that already exist and already hurt. And crucially, build the datasets alongside the domain experts who live with those shifts — pathologists, ecologists, genomicists, remote sensing people — rather than inventing splits for machine learning convenience.
[S] What was the admission criterion? Because "real world" is the kind of phrase that does no work on its own.
[G] Three bars, and they are strict. One: a real measured drop from in-distribution to out-of-distribution performance, not merely a distribution difference that happens not to matter. Two: real-world relevance, meaning the split and the metric were chosen with the domain experts rather than for convenience. Three: potential leverage. The shift has to be solvable in principle, which in practice means domain annotations and multiple training domains, so a method actually has something to learn from.

[O] Give me the two regimes first.
[G] Every data point is a triple: input x, label y, and a domain d. In domain generalization, the training and test domains are disjoint. In subpopulation shift, the test domains are the same domains you trained on, just in different and sometimes much smaller proportions, and you are graded on the worst one rather than the average. Five of the ten datasets are pure domain generalization, one is pure subpopulation shift, and four are hybrids of both.

[S] Now here is the part I actually care about. Every one of those has a different structure. How do you define an in-distribution baseline that means the same thing across ten datasets?
[G] You do not, and the honest answer is the one the paper gives. It works through five distinct ways of constructing an in-distribution comparison and uses whichever is feasible per dataset. First, test-to-test: train on data drawn from the shifted distribution itself and evaluate on it, holding the evaluation distribution fixed. That is the gold standard, and it is used for zero of the ten datasets.
[O] Zero?
[G] Zero. They describe it in order to explain why it is not feasible — it would require as much labelled data from the test domains as they have from the training domains, and that data does not exist. Second is mixed-to-test: train on a mixture of in-distribution and out-of-distribution data, then evaluate only on the shifted distribution. That still holds the evaluation distribution fixed, and it is used for four datasets — the cell imaging set, wheat detection, satellite land use, and poverty mapping.
[S] And the remaining three protocols?
[G] Train-to-train: train and evaluate on held-out data from the training distribution. Used for camera traps, the hospital tumour set, and code completion, on the reasoning that those have many roughly interchangeable domains. Fourth, average: report mean performance rather than worst-case, used for the two subpopulation datasets. And fifth, a random split that simply shuffles everything together, used only for the molecular dataset, to stay consistent with the Open Graph Benchmark's own protocol.
[S] So only the first two control for the evaluation distribution, and the first is never used. Which means exactly one protocol out of five actually isolates the shift, and it covers four datasets out of ten.
[G] That is precisely right, and the paper states the trade-off itself in section five point two. On the other six datasets the reported gap confounds the shift with any difference in intrinsic difficulty between the two distributions. [O] What changes downstream if this all holds?
