[S] Every embedding leaderboard entry since twenty twenty-two has quoted one number, the M T E B average. I want to open by saying what that number actually is. It is not an average over the task types the benchmark advertises. It is a plain mean over fifty-six individual dataset scores, and fifteen of those fifty-six are retrieval.
[O] Which is a design choice, not a scandal. If you build a benchmark by unifying every serious embedding dataset you can find, you get uneven counts, and averaging over datasets is the most obvious thing to do with them.
[S] It is an obvious thing to do. It is also the thing that makes summarization — one dataset out of fifty-six — contribute under two percent of a score the entire field reads as "how good is this embedding".
[G] Both of those are true, and the paper says the second one itself, in its own limitations list. What it does not do is print a second number that fixes it. That gap is most of what we should argue about today.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio, where an optimist, a skeptic, and a visiting scholar take one paper apart. Today it is M T E B, the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark.
[S] Niklas Muennighoff, Nouamane Tazi, Loïc Magne and Nils Reimers — the first three at Hugging Face, Reimers listed at Cohere. It went up on arXiv in October twenty twenty-two and was published at E A C L in twenty twenty-three.
[O] Our guest is Cressida, who has been through this one down to the appendix. Cressida, this is an old benchmark paper by our standards. Why is it on the docket?
[G] Because it is the reference design. Almost every embedding evaluation released since copies its shape, and the leaderboard it launched is still the thing people cite in release posts. If the aggregate at the centre of it has a known bias, that bias has been propagating for four years.
[S] Start with the gap. What was embedding evaluation before this?
[G] Narrow, and narrow in one specific direction. Sim C S E and S BERT — two of the most influential embedding papers of the era — evaluate on semantic textual similarity and classification, and nothing else. So you had state-of-the-art claims carrying no evidence about whether the model was any use for search or for clustering.
[O] S T S is at least a real task, though.
[G] It is, but the paper cites prior work showing S T S correlates poorly with other real-world use cases. So the field's default yardstick was one that had already been shown not to predict the thing practitioners actually wanted.
[S] There were broader toolkits before this.
[G] Three, and each covered a slice. Sent Eval, from twenty eighteen, aggregates S T S datasets but has no retrieval and no clustering, and it predates transformer embeddings entirely. U S E B is mostly reranking, so it skips retrieval and classification. And B E I R had become the standard for zero-shot retrieval, but it is retrieval-only.
[O] So it's a coverage union problem.
[G] Coverage plus harness. The authors' second complaint is that each of those tools implements its own pipeline, its own preprocessing, its own hyperparameters. So when a paper reports a gain, you cannot tell whether the embedding improved or the evaluation code was friendlier. The word they use for what follows is blind — a model validated on S T S gets deployed for search with no evidence it transfers.
[O] So what does M T E B actually do?
[G] It freezes the model and does something deliberately dumb downstream. Nothing is fine-tuned. You hand it a function that turns a list of texts into vectors, and it runs a fixed protocol per task. Classification embeds a train and a test split, fits a logistic regression with a hundred maximum iterations on the train embeddings, and scores accuracy on the test set. Clustering runs mini-batch k-means with k set to the number of gold labels, and scores V measure, which is invariant to how the labels happen to be permuted.
[S] Keep going. I want the whole list, because the metric zoo is the part everyone skips.
[G] Pair classification embeds two texts, takes the cosine similarity, sweeps for the best binary threshold, and reports average precision. Reranking embeds a query and a mixed list of relevant and irrelevant references, ranks them by cosine similarity to the query, and reports mean average precision. Retrieval reuses B E I R's protocol wholesale and reports N D C G at ten. S T S and summarization both report the Spearman correlation between cosine similarity and a human label. And bitext mining finds the closest cross-lingual pair by cosine and reports F one.
[S] That's six different metrics.
[G] Six, and this is the line I would put in bold. Accuracy, V measure, average precision, mean average precision, N D C G at ten, and Spearman correlation are not the same measurement. They happen to land in a similar numeric range in the results table, which invites exactly the comparison you must not make. A column is comparable across models. A row is not comparable across columns.
[O] Spell out what breaks if you do it anyway.
[G] You end up concluding a model is better at similarity than at search because its Spearman is eighty-two and its N D C G at ten is forty-two. Those two numbers have different zeroes and different ceilings and a point means a different thing in each. Spearman near zero means no monotone relationship at all. N D C G at ten near zero means nothing relevant surfaced in the top ten. There is no exchange rate between them.
[O] What's the scope of the thing? The headline is eight tasks, fifty-eight datasets, a hundred and twelve languages.
[G] That's the benchmark overall. The main results table is the English subset, and it has a detail people miss constantly: fifty-six datasets and seven task types, not eight. Bitext mining is inherently cross-lingual, so it has no English-only subset at all — it shows up only in the multilingual tables. So whenever you see fifty-six datasets, the number to pair it with is seven.
[S] And the split across those seven?
[G] Retrieval fifteen, classification twelve, clustering eleven, S T S ten, reranking four, pair classification three, summarization one. Fifty-six.
[O] Which brings us back to your cold open.
[G] It does, and it is worth being precise about what was actually checked here, because this is arithmetic, not interpretation. The printed average is not the mean of the seven task columns. It is the mean over all fifty-six dataset scores, which is the same thing as weighting each task column by its own dataset count. Take S T five X X L. Seventy-three point four two times twelve, plus forty-three point seven one times eleven, plus eighty-five point zero six times three, plus fifty-six point four three times four, plus forty-two point two four times fifteen, plus eighty-two point six three times ten, plus thirty point zero eight — all divided by fifty-six. That gives fifty-nine point five one, which is exactly what the table prints.
[S] One row could be a coincidence.
[G] It was checked on thirty-one rows. The dataset-weighted formula reproduces the printed average to within six thousandths on every one of them. The unweighted seven-task mean misses by as much as four point eight four. That is not an ambiguity, it is a settled question about what the number is.
[O] And the authors know.
[G] They name it. Limitation two on their own list is task imbalance. Their words are that M T E B average scores are computed over all datasets and are therefore biased towards tasks with many datasets, notably retrieval, classification and clustering. They say they hope to add datasets to underrepresented tasks as the benchmark grows. They do not add a second, task-normalized aggregate alongside the one they print.
[S] Let's get to the models.
[G] Thirty-three of them. Four self-supervised — Glove, Komninos, BERT with mean pooling, and unsupervised Sim C S E — and twenty-nine supervised. The supervised set spans B E R T-based encoders like Contriever, co-Condenser and Lab S E, Specter on top of Sci B E R T, the sentence-transformers M P Net and Mini L M, two T five encoder families in G T R and S T five, and a decoder family, S G P T, running up to a seven point one billion parameter BLOOM backbone.
[O] And OpenAI is in there.
[G] The Ada Similarity endpoint, yes, and it is worth knowing where the paper files it. Not with the encoders. It goes under transformer decoder methods, because it is c p t text — a pre-trained G P T decoder with last-token pooling. People routinely describe it as an encoder, and the paper does not.
[S] Scale of the run?
[G] Close to five thousand experiments on over thirty models. That is their own phrasing in the conclusion.
[S] And the headline finding.
[G] No model wins everything. Concretely, across the seven English task columns, the winners are these. Classification, S T five X X L at seventy-three point four two accuracy. Clustering, S T five X X L again at forty-three point seven one V measure — and that one is a photo finish, because M P Net is at forty-three point six nine, and M P Net is roughly fifty times smaller. Pair classification, G T R X L at eighty-six point one three average precision, with G T R X X L at eighty-six point one two and S T five X L at eighty-six point zero six, three models inside seven hundredths of a point.
[O] Keep going, that's four.
[G] Reranking, M P Net at fifty-nine point three six mean average precision. Retrieval, S G P T five point eight billion tuned on M S MARCO, at fifty point two five N D C G at ten. And summarization, the multilingual M P Net at thirty-one point five seven Spearman.
[S] So the model with the best average is not the best model where?
[G] Four of the seven. S T five X X L takes the overall average at fifty-nine point five one, and loses pair classification, reranking, retrieval and summarization. Five different models win at least one of the seven columns.
[S] That is a genuinely good result for a benchmark paper and I want to say so out loud. The negative finding is the contribution. Most benchmarks are built to produce a ranking, and this one's headline is that the ranking doesn't exist.
[O] Is there a mechanism behind the split, or is it noise?
[G] There's a mechanism and the paper names it: bifurcation. Models contrastively fine-tuned on N L I are aimed at symmetric similarity, where you compare one kind of text to another of the same kind. Models tuned on M S MARCO are aimed at asymmetric retrieval, where a query and a document are different sorts of object. The S G P T retrieval variants even use different special tokens for queries and documents. So the similarity-tuned models do badly on retrieval, and the retrieval-tuned models do badly on similarity.
[S] That's a story. What's the test of it?
[G] The Quora retrieval dataset, which prior work had shown is unusually symmetric for a retrieval dataset. On that one dataset the ordering flips: S G P T five point eight billion tuned on N L I beats the same model tuned on M S MARCO. Everywhere else in retrieval it is the other way round. That is the cleanest evidence in the paper that the split is about task symmetry and not about one family simply being better.
[S] Scaling. The figure caption says M T E B performance scales with model size. Does it?
[G] Broadly. The paper's own phrase in the text is that performance strongly correlates with model size, which is the right hedge. But if you trace the individual curves for the two T five families, six of the twelve size curves are non-monotone somewhere. The sharpest case is S T five on pair classification: eighty-five point one seven at Base, eighty-four point nine seven at Large, eighty-six point zero six at X L, and eighty-five point zero six at X X L. It ends below where it started, after four scale steps and about two orders of magnitude of parameters.
[O] That's one curve out of six.
[G] It is, and I wouldn't over-read a single dip. But half the curves wobble, and the caption is a smooth statement about a set of lines that are not smooth.
[S] What about S G P T's curve? That family is doing something unusual.
[G] It is, and that is the more interesting scaling story. S G P T fine-tunes under a tenth of one percent of its parameters — bias terms only, with weighted-mean pooling. The smallest variant, a hundred and twenty-five million parameters, lags similarly-sized G T R and S T five models on five of the six panels. But on retrieval it wins: thirty-seven point zero four against S T five Base's thirty-three point six three N D C G at ten. The authors' explanation is that bias-only tuning has very few knobs at small scale, and only catches up as the parameter count, and therefore the number of bias parameters, grows.
[O] Efficiency next, because nobody actually deploys the X X L.
[G] The efficiency figure is the most useful page in the paper for a practitioner, and it resolves thirty-three models into three clusters. Maximum speed: word embeddings, where Glove leads on both speed and quality within that cluster. Maximum score: the slow cluster of G T R X X L, S T five X X L and S G P T five point eight billion, with the caveat that S G P T's embeddings are sixteen point four kilobytes per example against one point two for Glove — nearly fourteen times the storage, per item, forever. And a middle cluster, the fine-tuned M P Net and Mini L M, giving up a few points for an order of magnitude more throughput.
[S] How was speed measured?
[G] On one dataset, S T S fifteen, on a single A one hundred eighty gigabyte card. It is a throughput measurement, so the text-length distribution matters, and that is short text. I would not transfer those numbers to a long-document workload.
[O] The hundred and twelve languages. What's actually in there?
[G] Ten of the fifty-eight datasets are multilingual, and this is another place to be careful with the headline. Only three of the eight task types have any multilingual data: bitext mining, classification and S T S. The other five, including retrieval and clustering, are English-only. The authors flag that as limitation three.
[S] So a hundred and twelve languages describes three tasks, not eight.
[G] Correct. Within those three, Lab S E dominates bitext mining across a wide language spread. Laser two is high-variance across languages. Multilingual M P Net is the strongest all-rounder on multilingual classification and S T S. And S G P T BLOOM seven point one billion leads specifically on languages BLOOM saw heavily in pre-training — Chinese, French, Portuguese, Hindi — while, in the authors' own words, not being much ahead of the far cheaper M P Net.
[O] Let me make the optimist case, and I'll make it narrow. Before this paper, state-of-the-art embedding model meant won on S T S. After it, that sentence is not sayable without naming a task. The authors ran a negative result, published it as the headline instead of burying it, and that negative result is now the field's default assumption. That is a benchmark doing its job.
[S] I'll grant every word of that and still say the artifact undercuts the finding. The paper proves no model dominates all seven tasks, and then, on the same page, prints a single column that ranks all thirty-three models on one axis. Which of those two things do you think the field picked up?
[O] The column. Obviously the column.
[S] And the column's weighting is an accident of dataset availability. Retrieval has fifteen datasets because B E I R existed and could be imported wholesale. Summarization has one because there was one. That's provenance, not a judgment about what matters.
[G] I'd score that to the skeptic, with one correction. It isn't purely accidental — retrieval is genuinely the largest commercial use of embeddings, so weighting toward it is defensible if you say that's what you're doing. The paper doesn't say that. It presents the average as neutral and discloses the imbalance separately, as a limitation.
[O] What would the number look like if you fixed it?
[G] You don't have to guess. Average the seven task columns equally instead. On the rows that were checked, that differs from the printed average by up to four point eight four points, which is much larger than the gaps between adjacent leaderboard entries. The ordering would move.
[S] There's a second thing the average hides, and it's in the appendix correlations.
[G] Figure six, the second panel — pairwise correlation between task-type results across models. Clustering and reranking correlate at ninety-five, the highest pair in the matrix, so those two are telling you nearly the same thing twice. Summarization correlates with the other six at three, minus four, eight, minus eight, minus sixteen, and zero.
[O] All effectively nothing.
[G] Effectively nothing, or slightly negative. So a model's summarization score is close to independent information about that model — and it's also the task with a single dataset, contributing one fifty-sixth of the average. The most orthogonal signal in the benchmark is the one the aggregate very nearly throws away.
[S] Contamination. Here I want the paper's own words, not a paraphrase.
[G] You can have them, because they're unusually direct. On the Sci Docs R R reranking dataset, M P Net and Mini L M perform far better than much bigger models, and the authors write that this is likely due to parts of Sci Docs R R being included in their training data. Then: their scale of experiments and that of model pre-training make controlling for data contamination challenging, so they ignore overlap of M T E B datasets with model training datasets in M T E B scores — and, their phrasing, as long as enough datasets are averaged, they believe these effects to be insignificant.
[S] Believe is doing a lot of work in that sentence.
[G] It's an assumption, stated as one rather than measured. And the honest reading cuts both ways. Disclosing it at all was better practice than most benchmarks of that period. But the single case they inspected is a case where it did bite, and averaging only dilutes contamination if contamination is spread randomly across datasets. The paper itself notes that the scientific datasets — Sci Docs R R, SciFact, the arXiv clustering sets — are highly similar to each other even though they sit in different tasks. If a training corpus overlaps several of those at once, the errors are correlated, and averaging doesn't rescue you.
[O] That's the sharpest version of that critique I've heard and I'll take the point.
[S] Where do I give ground? Coverage. Thirty-three models spanning word vectors, a commercial API, and a seven-billion-parameter decoder, all run through the same code — that's a real baseline set for twenty twenty-two. And the reproducibility story, versioned datasets with released result files, was ahead of its time.
[G] I'd add the design's most underrated property, which is that the downstream procedures are deliberately unsophisticated. A logistic regression with a hundred iterations. Mini-batch k-means. A threshold sweep. Nothing with enough capacity to launder a bad embedding into a good score. That is why the harness argument holds — a difference between two rows really is about the vectors.
[O] Implications. What survives, four years on?
[G] The shape survives. Fixed protocols, one primary metric per task, a frozen model, minimal downstream machinery — that is now what an embedding benchmark looks like, and this is where it came from. What has not been fixed is the aggregate. The leaderboard still ranks on a dataset-count-weighted mean, and every generation of models since has been optimized against it.
[S] Which is Goodhart with a known direction. If your average is fifteen fifty-sixths retrieval, the efficient move is to tune for retrieval and eat whatever happens on the three-dataset tasks.
[O] Though the paper hands you the antidote in the same table. The per-task columns are right there. Anyone quoting only the average has chosen to.
[G] That's fair, and it's the practical advice. Read the column for your task. The average is a summary of a benchmark, not a summary of a model.
[S] And carry the rest of the limitations list too. Four of them. No long documents — the longest datasets in M T E B run to a few hundred words. Task imbalance. Multilinguality confined to three task types, with no code datasets at all. And no other modalities.
[O] Takeaways. Mine: this paper turned which embedding model is best from a vibe into a question with a task attached, and the answer being it depends is the finding, not a failure of the study.
[S] Mine: any benchmark that publishes a single headline number should be made to state its weighting in the same breath. This one put the weighting in a limitations appendix, and four years of leaderboard have quoted the number without it.
[G] The paper's own: eight tasks, fifty-eight datasets, a hundred and twelve languages, thirty-three models, close to five thousand experiments — and no single method state-of-the-art on all of them. The authors read that as the field not yet having converged on a universal text embedding and scaled it up sufficiently. That conclusion has held.
