[S] I want to open by defusing the headline number, because I think most people who cite this paper have it wrong. The best agent here scores fifty-six point two. That is not a success rate. That is not accuracy. That is a weighted area under a curve, and the weights are chosen so that an agent needing more than ten attempts per puzzle is mathematically barred from scoring above fifty.
[O] Which I think is the most interesting design decision in the whole paper, and you are describing it like a flaw.
[S] I am describing it like a thing people should know before they compare it to anything else.
[G] You are both right about the mechanics, and the paper is completely upfront about them. The measure is called AUCCESS. The weight on attempt k is the log of k plus one minus the log of k, summed over k from one to one hundred. Those weights are front-loaded so heavily that the first ten attempts carry roughly half the total. The paper states the consequence in plain text, in the same paragraph where it defines the formula.
[O] Welcome to Litsearch Audio. The paper is PHYRE, a new benchmark for physical reasoning, by Anton Bakhtin, Laurens van der Maaten, Justin Johnson, Laura Gustafson, and Ross Girshick, out of Facebook AI Research, at NeurIPS twenty nineteen. Beatrix, thank you for coming in.
[G] Delighted. It is one of my favourite benchmark papers to teach, precisely because the metric is a genuine contribution and not an afterthought.
[S] And it is on our docket because of the gap. Fifty-six point two on templates the agent has never seen. Seventy-seven point six on templates it has. Same architecture family, same tier, same folds.
[O] Beatrix, set the stage for us. What was the state of intuitive physics work that made this benchmark feel necessary in twenty nineteen?
[G] Mostly passive. The dominant setup was prediction. You train a model to say whether a stack of blocks will topple, or you show it a video and ask whether the scene is physically plausible. Perception and forecasting, not intervention. Separately there was a visual reasoning line, the VQA and CLEVR family, which does demand multi-object reasoning but assumes a static world with no dynamics at all. And then there were the simulated robotics environments, driving simulators, humanoid control, embodied navigation. Those genuinely require physical understanding, but they are realistic, non-deterministic, partially observed three-D worlds. The authors argue that richness actively hampers systematic study of reasoning.
[S] So the pitch is a controlled middle.
[G] Exactly that. Goal-driven intervention, meaning the agent has to act on the world to cause a goal state rather than predict or classify. Sample-efficient generalization to puzzles it has not seen. And a fully deterministic, symbolically specified two-D environment simple enough to actually analyse. The paper's claim is that nothing at the time combined all three.
[O] Take us through the world itself.
[G] Two-D, constant downward gravity, a small amount of friction. Every body is non-deformable and comes from a small vocabulary. Balls, bars, standing sticks, and jars. Each body is either static, meaning it is fixed and unaffected by gravity and collision, or dynamic, meaning it responds to both. Colour tells you which. A task is an initial world state plus a goal, and the goal is a subject-relation-object triplet. In the current release there is exactly one relation. Touching for at least three seconds.
[S] One relation. That is a much smaller benchmark than the phrase physical reasoning implies.
[G] It is, and the authors say so. They found one relation sufficient for a diverse set of tasks and note the environment can be extended later. But yes, every puzzle in this release is make this thing touch that thing.
[O] And the action?
[G] A single action. The agent places one or more new dynamic bodies into the scene, and then it is done acting. A placement that overlaps an existing body or leaves the world boundary is rejected as invalid. The simulator runs until the goal is met or a time limit elapses. The agent cannot intervene while it runs. Then it gets a binary reward, plus observations of the intermediate world states. If it failed, the world resets and it tries again.
[S] So it is a bandit.
[G] A contextual bandit, with two twists the paper is explicit about. There is an offline training phase before the online testing phase, with unlimited simulator access but no access to the solutions. And the agent gets privileged information beyond the binary reward, namely those intermediate world states from previous attempts.
[O] What does the agent actually see? Pixels?
[G] Not photographs. The world is rasterised to a two fifty-six by two fifty-six grid, and each cell takes one of seven values. Dynamic goal object, static goal subject, dynamic goal subject, static confounding body, dynamic confounding body, body placed by the agent, or background. Because there is only one relation, the colours in that initial observation fully encode the goal. There is no natural language anywhere in the loop. The full state, exact positions, orientations, masses, velocities, is deliberately withheld, on the argument that human observers cannot directly perceive those values either.
[S] Now the tiers, because this is where people get sloppy in citation.
[G] Two tiers. PHYRE B, where the action is placing a single ball, which is a continuous three-D action, x, y, and radius. And PHYRE two B, where the action is placing a pair of balls, continuous six-D. Each tier has twenty-five task templates. A template is a family of related tasks generated by varying parameters like body positions. Every template defines one hundred tasks. So twenty-five hundred tasks per tier, five thousand total.
[O] And templates are how they measure generalization.
[G] Two settings. Within-template, where the agent trains on a subset of a template's one hundred tasks and is tested on the rest of that same template. And cross-template, where test tasks come exclusively from templates that were not used in training. All the splits are deterministic and pre-defined across ten folds, so any two agents are always compared on identical partitions.
[S] Beatrix, I want to push on the metric before results, because I think this is where the paper earns or loses its credibility. Why log weights rather than just reporting success at some fixed budget?
[G] The paper wants the whole curve summarised without picking an arbitrary budget, and it wants the summary to reward sample efficiency. AUCCESS is the weighted average of the success percentages, with those log-difference weights, over attempts one through one hundred. It is equivalent to the area under the success-percentage curve when you put attempts on a log axis. The design consequence, that agents needing more than ten attempts are capped near fifty, is intended, not incidental. The stated goal is to penalise methods that only master a task after thousands of attempts.
[S] Fine. I will grant that it is principled. What I will not grant is that a fifty-six means what a reader assumes a fifty-six means.
[O] Nobody is asking you to grant that. Beatrix, walk us through the agents.
[G] Five that are scored, and three families that are discussed but not scored. The scored five all work by ranking candidate actions given the observation of the initial state. RAND samples uniformly from the tier's action space. MEM is a non-parametric memorisation agent. At training time it samples a fixed pool of actions, uses the simulator to check which training tasks each solves, computes for each action the fraction of training tasks it solves, sorts by that, and replays the ranking at test time.
[O] And in cross-template it has nothing task-specific to condition on.
[G] Right, so there it uses one shared ranking for every test task and ignores the observation entirely. In within-template it is given the template id and keeps a separate per-template ranking over the same shared action pool. MEM O is the online variant, which keeps updating those per-action success estimates using the rewards it receives on test tasks.
[S] And critically, when does that update land?
[G] After finishing a test task. The updated ranking is used when the next test task is attempted. It is between tasks, not within one. Same for the deep agent.
[O] The deep one being the D Q N.
[G] Yes. It collects observation-action-reward triplets by sampling actions and running the simulator, then trains a network to predict the reward for an observation-action pair. The observation encoder is a ResNet eighteen. The action encoder is a multi-layer perceptron with a single hidden layer of five hundred and twelve units. The fusion is FiLM-style, meaning the action encoder predicts a per-channel bias and gain for the convolutional features, applied just before the last residual block. They report trying other fusion points without seeing performance differences. Training is class-balanced cross-entropy with equal positive and negative examples per batch, Adam, and a half-cosine learning rate anneal to zero. D Q N O is the same thing with gradient updates on test-task feedback, again applied after finishing a task and used for the next one.
[S] And the budgets, because this is the single most-garbled fact about this paper.
[G] There are three distinct numbers and they are easy to collide. At test time, every non-random agent ranks a shared pool of ten thousand candidate actions per task. That is the test-time budget. Every agent is capped at one hundred solution attempts per task. That is the attempt budget the metric integrates over. And separately, the D Q N agents were trained on one hundred thousand actions per task. That is a training figure. MEM and MEM O were trained on the same ten thousand actions they rank at test time.
[O] What about the methods they discuss and do not score?
[G] Three families. Linear and Gaussian process contextual bandits, which they rule out by argument rather than experiment. Linear models do not yield useful representations of observation images, and a sensible kernel over that image space is difficult to define. Second, a deep-network contextual bandit adapted from published code, which they report being unable to train once they swapped the shallow perceptron for a convolutional image encoder. And third, policy learners, P P O and A two C, which did train but which they could not get to outperform the random baseline, and they attribute that to poor training stability.
[S] I want to flag that as unusually candid. Most launch papers quietly omit the baselines that did not work.
[O] Agreed, and I will take the point. Beatrix, results. Start with how hard these puzzles actually are.
[G] Before any learned agent, they calibrate against chance. They designed the tiers so that, on average, a random agent needs no more than ten thousand attempts to solve a PHYRE B task and no more than one hundred thousand for a PHYRE two B task. But the average buries an enormous spread. The per-template plot shows the mean probability that a single random attempt succeeds ranging from about four times ten to the minus four up to about seven times ten to the minus two across PHYRE B's twenty-five templates, and from about ten to the minus five up to about six times ten to the minus three across PHYRE two B's.
[S] So more than two orders of magnitude within a single tier.
[G] Yes. The paper's own phrasing is that some tasks require thousands of attempts to be solved. And at around one thousand attempts, a random agent has already solved about eighty percent of PHYRE B tasks but only about forty percent of PHYRE two B tasks.
[O] Now the main table.
[G] Mean AUCCESS over the ten folds, plus or minus one standard deviation. Cross-template first, PHYRE B. Random is thirteen point zero, plus or minus five point zero. MEM is eighteen point five, plus or minus five point one. MEM O is twenty-two point eight, plus or minus five point zero. D Q N is thirty-six point eight, plus or minus nine point seven. And D Q N O is fifty-six point two, plus or minus ten point five, which is the statistically best score in that column by a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test across folds at p of zero point zero one.
[S] Hold on the error bar. Plus or minus ten point five.
[G] It is large, and it should discipline how you read the ordering. The Wilcoxon test is doing real work there precisely because the raw fold-to-fold variance is high. Small differences in that column are not meaningful.
[O] And PHYRE two B, cross-template.
[G] Random two point six plus or minus one point five. MEM three point seven plus or minus two point three. MEM O four point nine plus or minus three point one. D Q N twenty-three point two plus or minus nine point one. D Q N O thirty-nine point six plus or minus eleven point one, again statistically best in column.
[S] So one extra ball costs you sixteen and a half points.
[G] Fifty-six point two down to thirty-nine point six. In relative terms that is a drop of about thirty percent. Not a halving, which is a mis-statement I see fairly often. And the physics and object vocabulary barely change between tiers. What changes is that the action space goes from three continuous dimensions to six.
[O] Now within-template, which is the comparison the whole paper is built around.
[G] Within-template, the offline D Q N reaches seventy-seven point six plus or minus one point one on PHYRE B, and sixty-seven point eight plus or minus one point five on PHYRE two B. Both best in column. And notice the standard deviations. One point one and one point five, against ten point five and eleven point one on the cross-template side. Within-template is not just easier, it is dramatically more stable across folds.
[S] Why are there dashes for MEM O and D Q N O in the within-template columns?
[G] Because cross-validation selects zero online updates for both of them in that setting, which makes them identical to MEM and D Q N. For D Q N O specifically, the paper's section four point four analysis finds that online updates actually impede performance within-template. So the online machinery is not merely neutral there. It is, on that evidence, counterproductive.
[O] That is a genuinely surprising result and I want to sit on it. Adaptation helps enormously when the templates are new, and hurts when they are familiar.
[G] That is a fair reading of what the paper reports. It does not offer a mechanism.
[S] There is a second oddity in that table and I want you to explain it, Beatrix, because I do not think it can be explained.
[G] You mean MEM.
[S] I mean MEM. On PHYRE B, MEM scores eighteen point five cross-template and two point four within-template. It does dramatically worse on the setting that is supposed to be easier. And the paper's own summary of its results says within-template is much easier than cross-template for all the non-random agents.
[G] It is real and the paper's table shows it without further comment. I would scope it carefully, though. On PHYRE two B the equivalent numbers are three point seven cross and three point two within, which are close enough to be inside the error bars. So the inversion is clearly established only on PHYRE B. What I can tell you is that the within-template MEM is a different object than it sounds like. It maintains a per-template ranking over the same shared action pool, and the set of actions it actually attempts varies because invalid actions are skipped. Beyond that, the paper does not diagnose it.
[O] Is there anything at the fixed ten-attempt budget?
[G] Yes, a second table, and it is a different metric. Success percentage at exactly ten attempts is a single point on the curve, not a weighted average over one through one hundred. Cross-template, D Q N O leads there too, at fifty-eight point two plus or minus ten point nine on PHYRE B and forty-one point six plus or minus eleven point seven on PHYRE two B. Within-template, D Q N is at eighty-one point four and seventy-four point nine.
[S] And the oracle.
[G] There is an analysis varying how many candidate actions each agent ranks at test time, which includes an OPTIMAL ranker given perfect knowledge of which actions solve which tasks. It solves all of PHYRE B and ninety-five percent of PHYRE two B by ranking fewer than one hundred thousand attempts. The authors read that narrowly, as evidence that ranking actions is a viable strategy at all. Not as a justification for any particular budget.
[O] Right. Let me make the optimist case, then, and I will keep it to what survives.
[S] Please.
[O] First, this is a benchmark whose metric is a real intellectual contribution. AUCCESS has a clean motivation, log weighting over a bounded attempt budget, and it makes a claim about what progress should look like. Second, the reproducibility engineering is genuine. Deterministic simulator, ten fixed folds released, an actual statistical test rather than eyeballing means. Third, the central finding replicates the thing everyone worries about. Same architecture family, twenty-one point four points worse on unseen templates than on seen ones. That is a clean, measurable generalization gap, and it was measured in twenty nineteen, before it was fashionable to worry about this. And fourth, online adaptation is the biggest single lever in the paper. Thirty-six point eight to fifty-six point two cross-template on PHYRE B, just over nineteen points, from letting the agent update between tasks.
[S] Deflationary case. One, the scored comparison set spans exactly two model families. Non-parametric memorisation, and one convolutional value network. That is it. The paper's own related-work section discusses object-oriented prediction-and-planning models and simulation-conjoined predictors as the sort of method PHYRE is meant to push toward, and none of them are benchmarked. So the finding is that generic value-based reinforcement learning beats memorisation and random guessing. It is not that any twenty-nineteen-era method is near solving cross-template generalization.
[O] I do not think the paper claims otherwise.
[S] Two, and this is the one I care about most. The online agents' early-curve numbers are optimistic. The paper states plainly that all agents get up to one hundred attempts per task, and that when computing the success percentage at k below one hundred, online agents will have learned from up to one hundred attempts per task rather than k. It calls the choice pragmatic, and it is, computationally. But the consequence is that by the time an online agent is scored on, say, the fifth test task in a fold, its weights or its ranking have already absorbed the complete hundred-attempt outcome of every earlier test task in that fold.
[G] I want to be precise about what that is and is not. It is not a leak from later attempts on the very task being scored. It is a cross-task accumulation. But your conclusion follows. Relative to a strictly sequential deployment, the reported small-k sample efficiency of the online agents is optimistic.
[S] Three. AUCCESS is reported only as a single aggregate over each tier's twenty-five templates times one hundred tasks. There is no per-template breakdown for the learned agents. Only the random baseline gets one, and that one shows several orders of magnitude of spread in template difficulty. So a fifty-six point two is consistent with broadly competent but imperfect performance, or with nailing a handful of easy templates and failing hard ones outright. The released numbers cannot distinguish those.
[G] That is accurate, and I would rank it as the most consequential gap for anyone using these numbers as a yardstick.
[S] And four. The introduction is entirely about comparing physical reasoning across humans, children, capuchins, chimpanzees, and crows. And then nobody measures a human on PHYRE's own fold splits. There is no human ceiling. The OPTIMAL oracle is an upper bound on ranking-based solving, which is a different object.

On the leakage point, I give it to the skeptic outright. The paper discloses the simplification, which is to its credit, but disclosure does not undo the effect on the reported curve. Hard-coding PHYRE-specific rules, or calling the real simulator inside the agent to brute-force a solution before submitting. Naming your own benchmark's exploits in the launch paper is rare.
[O] What does this mean for how we build evaluations, beyond physics?
[G] Three things travel. First, metric design as a first-class contribution. PHYRE decided that eventual success is nearly worthless information and encoded that decision in the weights. Most benchmarks make the same decision implicitly, badly, by reporting pass at some k without arguing for k. Second, the online-update leakage is a general hazard, not a PHYRE quirk. The moment your evaluation lets a system adapt during the test phase, you have to specify exactly what information is available at each scoring point, and it is very easy to write down a computationally convenient definition that quietly inflates the early part of your curve. Third, aggregate-only reporting over heterogeneous difficulty. If your items span orders of magnitude in difficulty and you publish one mean, you have made your headline number unfalsifiable in a specific way.
[S] My takeaway. Treat fifty-six point two as a floor to beat under a specific metric, not as evidence that anything here understands physics. And when you quote it, quote the setting and the tier with it, because the same architecture family spans thirty-nine point six to seventy-seven point six across those four cells.
[O] Mine. This is what a launch paper should look like. Deterministic environment, released folds, a defended metric, a statistical test, and an honest catalogue of what the authors could not get to work. I would rather have a benchmark with weak baselines and strong methodology than the reverse.
[G] And the paper's own. It set out to show that present-day methods would struggle to quickly solve new physics puzzles, and it showed that, with the generalization gap as the measurement rather than the absolute score. Its stated hope is that this pushes work toward agents that use intermediate observations to refine attempts, learn forward models, and diversify their guesses.
